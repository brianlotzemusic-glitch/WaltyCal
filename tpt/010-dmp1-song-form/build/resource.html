What this is ·

A complete 4-day song structure unit for high school music technology. Day 1 is a listening day: students map the form of real pop songs on a Song Form sheet, two as a class and two on their own. Then they plan a form on paper and build it in Logic Pro: their own chords, bass and melody arranged into at least 4 sections (intro, verse and chorus required), with a chord layer and a drum transition at every section change. They present on Day 4.

This is Unit 5 of my Digital Music Production 1 course. It works on its own too, as long as students have a short chord progression and bassline to arrange (page 4).

Quick start ·

  1. Read the overview and pacing (pages 3–6) and the teacher notes (pages 9–12).
  2. Cue up the two class songs on YouTube or Spotify: Crazy (Gnarls Barkley) and Blinding Lights (The Weeknd). Check that students can play the four choice songs on headphones.
  3. Check that every student has a Logic file with chords and a bassline (page 4).
  4. Practice the 5-minute chord layer demo once (page 10).
  5. Print or post the student pages (pages 13–24).

Every slide has a day: Day 1 (slides 1–7), Day 2 (slides 8–16) and Day 4 (slide 17). The day plans name the slide for each block. Every slide has speaker notes.

Song FormUnit overview
OVERVIEW
DMP1 · Unit 5

What students make ·

Four completed Song Form tables and a written response about the patterns they found. Then a structured arrangement in Logic of their own chords, bass and melody: at least 4 distinct sections (intro, verse and chorus required; bridge and outro encouraged), each at least 4 measures, labeled with arrangement markers, with a chord layer for texture and a drum transition at every section change. The worked example is a 32-measure skeleton (page 26).

What this unit is really about ·

This unit is a listening unit first and a building unit second. The Song Form sheet is not a worksheet to fill in: it is a tool for training students to hear structure the way a producer hears it. Students who genuinely engage with the analysis will arrive at the building phase already knowing what they want to do.

Mixing concept: arrangement as mix ·

Denser sections are fuller and louder; sparse sections have room and clarity. Every addition and removal of an element is audible and intentional. The chord layer demo makes it concrete: same notes, thicker texture, a bigger chorus.

Goals ·

  • Identify and label sections in real songs using the Song Form sheet
  • Understand what makes each section feel different from the others
  • Recognize common structural patterns across different songs and genres
  • Plan a song form before building it: intro, verse, chorus at minimum
  • Build a structured arrangement of your chords, bass and melody in Logic with at least 4 distinct sections
  • Use the presence and absence of elements to differentiate sections
  • Signal every section change with a deliberate transition

SETUP
Teacher

Everything students need in Logic for this unit, in one table. The planning table (page 16) comes before any of it.

Arrangement markersShow the global tracks (G), then the Arrangement track. Click + to add a marker; click its name to pick Intro, Verse, Chorus, Bridge or Outro, or rename it. Drag a marker’s right edge to set the section length.
Move or copy a sectionDrag an arrangement marker to move the whole section with its regions. Option-drag the marker to copy the section.
Copy a regionOption-drag a region to copy it, or select it and press Command-R to repeat it.
New trackTrack › New Tracks (Option-Command-N), Software Instrument.
Change the soundOpen the Library (Y) and pick a patch for the selected track: a pad, an electric piano, a slow synth.
Chord layerOption-drag a chord region onto a new track below, then change that track’s instrument. Same notes, new texture.
Take an element outDelete its region in that section (or never copy it in). To silence a whole track while you listen, click its M button.
Loop a sectionPress C for Cycle and drag the cycle area over the section you are working on.
Volume by sectionTrack faders are in the Mixer (X). Show automation (A) and draw volume points to make a section louder or softer.
Drum transitionsEdit the last measure of a drum region (Piano Roll or Step Sequencer) for a fill, a build or a cut. With a Drummer track, raise the Fills knob for that region.
ExportFile › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx project and choose Compress.
Measure math

Sections are almost always 4 or 8 measures. The worked skeleton: 4 + 4 + 8 + 4 + 8 + 4 = 32 measures. At 4 beats per measure that is 128 beats: about a minute at 120–128 BPM.

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 4

What this unit is really about ·

This unit is a listening unit first and a building unit second. The Song Form sheet is not a worksheet to fill in: it is a tool for training students to hear structure the way a producer hears it. Students who genuinely engage with the analysis will arrive at the building phase already knowing what they want to do. Students who rush through it will spend Day 2 guessing.

The central insight you are trying to land: sections feel different because of what is playing, not just because of a label. A chorus is not called a chorus because someone decided to call it that. It is called a chorus because it is texturally fuller, emotionally bigger, and more memorable than the verse. That is a compositional decision, not a naming decision.

Day 1: how to run the class analysis ·

The two class examples should be tight: 10 to 12 minutes each. Play the first 2–3 minutes of each song only, enough to get through at least two verse-chorus cycles. Here is how to structure each one:

  • First listen (90 seconds): Play without stopping. Ask students to raise their hand when they feel the section change. Do not define: just listen.
  • Second listen: Pause at each section change. Ask: what changed? What came in or dropped out? Students call it out. You label it on the board.
  • Measure count: Play one more time and count together out loud. Students will usually discover on their own that sections land in multiples of 4.
  • Key question before moving on: Why does the chorus feel bigger? What specifically is different? Push for musical answers, not just ‘it sounds louder’.

The share-out at the end of Day 1 is important. Ask: what patterns did you notice across all four songs? Students should arrive at: sections are almost always 4 or 8 bars, chorus is always fuller than verse, intros build in and outros strip back. These are the principles they will apply the next day.

How this kit differs from my classroom version
  • My Teacher Notes pace the unit in 3 days; this kit uses the 4 days of my course plan and gives building its own day (page 6).
  • Students arrange chords and a bassline they made earlier; if they have none, see page 4. The worked 32-measure skeleton (page 26) is new.
  • The Song Form spreadsheet is printed as tables, with the written response and an optional comparison grid on one page (page 21). My LMS and Padlet are written as “your class page” and “the class feedback board”.

Day 2: enforce the planning table ·

Do not let any student open the arrangement view until their planning table is filled in. Walk around at the start of the work time and check each one. What to look for:

  • The chorus has more elements than the verse
  • The intro starts with fewer elements than the verse
  • Every section change has a transition type written down
  • Sections are labeled with real names, not just ‘Part 1’ and ‘Part 2’

If a student has the same elements in every section, send them back to the sheet: look at the song you analyzed. What dropped out in the verse? That is what you need to do here.

The chord layer demo (5 minutes, start of Day 2) ·

  1. 1
    Option-drag a chord region to duplicate it onto a new track below
  2. 2
    Change the instrument to something with a different character: a pad, a Rhodes, a slow-attack synth
  3. 3
    Play both chord tracks together: the texture thickens without the notes changing
  4. 4
    Mute the second track in the verse, unmute it in the chorus
  5. 5
    Play the full arrangement and ask: can you hear the chorus get bigger? That is arrangement as mix.

TEACHER NOTES
3 of 4

Callbacks to earlier units ·

If your students did these projects (all part of my DMP1 course), name them out loud.

Drum beats (Beat From Nothing): Drum beats return here to anchor sections. Remind students: a drum pattern that does not change across section changes sounds like a loop, not a song.

Transitions (Stems & Grooves): The four transition types (fill, breakdown, cut, build) are the vocabulary for every section change in this unit. Ask students to name which type they used and why.

Chords, bass and melody (Building Blocks): These are the raw materials. This unit is purely about where those materials go and what they do in each section.

Common student struggles ·

All sections sound the same: The most common failure. The test: mute the melody track. Does the chorus still feel bigger? If not, the arrangement is not doing any work. Ask the student to listen to their analyzed song again and compare.

Rushing through the spreadsheet: Students who treat the analysis as a checkbox exercise will not have the vocabulary or intuition to plan their own form. Enforce quality: every section labeled, every measure counted, the written response answered thoughtfully.

Intros at full energy: Students start with everything playing because they are not thinking about the listener. Ask: if someone walked into the room mid-sentence, would they know what was coming? The intro should earn attention, not assume it.

No transitions: Students paste their drum groove everywhere and call it done. The transition is what tells the listener something changed. A section change with no acknowledgment sounds like a loop.

Suggested variations ·

More accessible: Pre-build the arrangement markers in the Logic file and ask students to drag elements in rather than build from scratch. Reduce the minimum to 3 sections: intro, verse, chorus.

More advanced: Require a bridge that uses a chord not in the main progression (specifically the vi or iv) held for 8 bars before the final chorus. Require all four transition types to appear at least once.

Grading tips ·

  • Each category is scored 2–5, for a total out of 25. Students self-score first; you score in the second column (page 24).
  • Song Form Analysis: check the measure counts against the answer key (page 25). Small differences (a pickup counted, a section split in two) are fine when the student clearly counted.
  • Listen once eyes-closed for Section Differentiation: can you name each section by ear? Then open the project to check the markers, section lengths and the chord layer track.
  • The presentation question is the quickest evidence of intention: point to your chorus; why does it feel different from your verse?

Project sheet

Digital Music Production 1 · Unit 5 · Structured · 4 Days · Arrangement as Mix

Overview ·

Every song you have ever heard is built on a structure. Verses, choruses, bridges, intros, outros: these sections are not accidents. They are deliberate decisions about energy, tension, and release. In this unit you will train your ear to hear those decisions in real songs, then make your own. You will build a structured arrangement of your chords, bass and melody with clearly differentiated sections: ones that feel different from each other because of what is playing, not just because a label says so.

Goals ·

  • Identify and label sections in real songs using the Song Form sheet
  • Understand what makes each section feel different from the others
  • Recognize common structural patterns across different songs and genres
  • Plan a song form before building it: intro, verse, chorus at minimum
  • Build a structured arrangement of your chords, bass and melody in Logic with at least 4 distinct sections
  • Use the presence and absence of elements to differentiate sections
  • Signal every section change with a deliberate transition

What each section does ·

A section is not just a label. It has a job. Understanding the job tells you how to build it.

IntroEarns the listener’s attention. Usually starts with one or two elements and builds. Does not need to be complicated: just needs to feel like a beginning.
VerseGives the listener space to settle in. Sparser than the chorus. If the chorus is the payoff, the verse is the setup. A weak verse makes a chorus feel less powerful.
Pre-ChorusOptional but effective. Builds tension in the last 2–4 measures before the chorus drops. Often raises energy slightly so the chorus feels like a release.
ChorusThe emotional peak. Should feel fuller, louder, and more energetic than the verse. This is where the melody is most prominent. Copy and paste: it is the same every time it returns.
BridgeBreaks the pattern. Usually appears once, after the second chorus. Different chord, different energy, or stripped back completely. Makes the final chorus feel earned.
OutroBrings it home. Often mirrors the intro, stripping elements back one by one. Ends cleanly. Does not just fade out in the middle of a loop.

Tips for success ·

  • The chorus should feel bigger than the verse. If they sound the same, something is missing. Add the melody, layer the chords on a second instrument, or thicken the drums.
  • Sections are almost always 4 or 8 bars long. If you analyzed the four songs carefully, you already know this from experience.
  • Copy and paste is not lazy. Every professional arranger does it. Write once, arrange many times.
  • Your intro does not need to be complicated. One element playing alone for 4 bars is a stronger intro than everything at full volume from bar one.
  • Every section change needs a transition. If you can hear where one section ends and another begins without looking at the screen, your transitions are working.

    Day 2 · Plan before you buildFill in the planning table on this sheet. Every section needs a name, a length, a list of what is playing, and a transition. Do this on paper. Do not open the arrangement view until your plan is complete.
  4. 4
    Days 2–3 · Build your structureAdd arrangement markers in Logic for every section in your plan. Copy your existing elements into the correct sections. Build the skeleton first (correct elements in the right places) before refining anything.
  5. 5
    Day 3 · Differentiate your sectionsGo back through and make sure the chorus feels different from the verse. Add a chord layer on a second instrument for the chorus. Add the melody only where it belongs. Make sure every section change has a drum transition.
  6. 6
    Day 4 · Mix and submitPlay the full arrangement from start to finish. Can you hear which section you are in without looking at the screen? If yes, your arrangement is working. Adjust volumes, export, and submit.

WordMeaningIn my song
Song formthe overall structure of a song: how its sections are organized from beginning to end
Introopens the song: establishes the sound and draws the listener in before the verse begins. Usually sparse.
Versetells the story. Sparser than the chorus. Repeats with varied content. Sets up the emotional release of the chorus.
Pre-chorusa short section between verse and chorus that builds tension before the drop. Not all songs have one.
Chorusthe emotional peak. Fuller, louder, the most memorable part. Usually returns multiple times unchanged.
Bridgea contrasting section that breaks the verse-chorus cycle. Creates tension or surprise before the final chorus.
Outrobrings the song to a close. Often strips elements back one by one. Mirrors the intro in energy.
Texturehow thick or thin the sound is: determined by how many elements are playing at once
Arrangementthe decisions about which elements play in which sections: what comes in, what drops out, and when
Transitionthe moment between sections that signals a change: drum fill, breakdown, cut, or build

EXAMPLE
Teacher

A 32-measure skeleton to model on Day 2 or to play from while you circulate: chords I–V–vi–IV in C major (C G Am F), one chord per measure. It is one possible answer, not the answer.

SectionBarsElements playing
chords / bass / melody / drums / chord layer		Transition into next section
Intro4chordsFill: a one-measure drum fill in m. 4 brings the drums in
Verse 14chords, bass, drumsBuild: snare on every eighth note in m. 8, rising into the chorus
Chorus 18chords, chord layer, bass, melody, drumsCut: everything stops on beat 4 of m. 16
Verse 24chords, bass, drumsFill: a drum fill in m. 20
Chorus 28chords, chord layer, bass, melody, drumsBreakdown: the drums and the pad drop out after m. 28
Bridge (optional)–not used in this skeleton
Outro4chords, bass (first 2 measures)End: the bass drops out after m. 30; the last chord rings on the I chord
Compare it to the songs

Like Crazy and Blinding Lights, every section is 4 or 8 measures and the chorus is the fullest part. There is no pre-chorus: the build in m. 8 does that job. The verses are 4 measures because this is a skeleton; double them for a full song. The intro starts with one element and the outro strips back to one.

What a 5 sounds like

A listener can name every section by ear; the chorus still feels bigger with the melody muted (the pad and full drums carry it); every change has a transition you can name; the denser sections sit a little louder in the mix, and nothing clips.
