What this is ·

A 4-class-period chiptune project for high school music technology. Every student arranges the public-domain carol “O Come, All Ye Faithful” as an 8-bit game tune in Logic Pro: they enter its melody from a piano-roll note chart on a square-wave lead, then build the other three channels of a classic game-music chip (arpeggiated chords, a triangle-wave bass and a noise drum) and crush it with a Bitcrusher. The arrangement is 45 to 75 seconds. Students plan it, build and mix it, present it and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Check the tune and its sources (page 9) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 12–26), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–6), Class 2 (slides 7–9), Class 3 (slides 10–11) and Class 4 (slides 12–15). Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

8-Bit HolidayProject overview
OVERVIEW
Chiptune project
The project in one line

8-Bit Holiday · Chiptune · Logic Pro
Rebuild a carol as an 8-bit game tune.

What students make ·

One original arrangement, 45 to 75 seconds long, of a public-domain carol. The melody is entered from a piano-roll chart on a square-wave lead and stays recognizable; the tempo is 120 to 150 BPM; arpeggios play the chords on a pulse wave; a triangle bass plays the chord roots; a noise drum keeps the beat; and a Bitcrusher makes it lo-fi. It has an intro, the whole carol and the chorus again with a change, and it is mixed with volume and EQ. Students hand in a bounced .mp3 or .wav and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play the tune and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a few simple parts make a whole sound. Students learn that a game tune is a melody, chords turned into arpeggios, a bass and a drum, and they practice entering notes, spelling chords and doing the arithmetic of bars, tempo and seconds in Logic Pro’s Piano Roll.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Enter a melody into Logic Pro’s Piano Roll from a note chart, with the right pitches and lengths
  • Build four chip channels: a square-wave lead, arpeggiated chords, a triangle-wave bass and a noise drum
  • Spell the chords of a key and play them as arpeggios with the Arpeggiator MIDI plug-in
  • Plan an arrangement in bars and seconds, and prove that its length is between 45 and 75 seconds

SETUP
Teacher

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The three chart pages, printed (pages 12–14), or shown on the projector.
  • The channel cards, arpeggio cards and drum patterns, printed (pages 15–17).
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 12–26).
  • A MIDI keyboard is optional: every melody can be drawn with the mouse.

Before Class 1 ·

Read the tune page (page 9) and decide whether the carol suits your class.
Open Logic Pro on a student computer: can you make a Software Instrument track, open the Piano Roll, put an Arpeggiator in a MIDI FX slot and a Bitcrusher in an audio effects slot?
Check that the tools on page 15 are there (Retro Synth or Alchemy, Arpeggiator, Drum Machine Designer, Bitcrusher, Channel EQ).
Read the worked example (pages 10–11).

What to watch ·

Students enter the notes but get a wrong one, the arpeggio plays the wrong chord, the sound is smooth instead of chip-like, or the arrangement is the wrong length. Pairs well with a listening day: play the finished tunes without saying the title and let the class name the carol. A tune that people name quickly is an arrangement that kept the melody.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: which channel is that? Which chord is the arpeggio spelling? Can you hear the carol?
  • On Class 1, tap 120, 135 and 150 BPM on the desk and have students pick the tempo that feels like a game level.
  • A student who is stuck on arpeggios can start from the Up pattern and one chord, then change the chord every bar.

Choosing the carol ·

A respectful choice

“O Come, All Ye Faithful” is a Christian Christmas carol. Offer it where carols are part of your winter concert or classroom. For a secular winter program, or for a student who prefers another melody, any public-domain tune you approve (first published in the United States in 1930 or earlier) works with the same plan, and the blank piano roll on page 18 is there for their own chart.

Keep the volume moderate

Square waves and crushed sounds are bright and can be tiring in headphones. Tell students to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

8-Bit HolidayTeacher notes · the big idea
TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a few simple parts make a whole sound. Students learn that a game tune is a melody, chords turned into arpeggios, a bass and a drum, and they practice entering notes, spelling chords and doing the arithmetic of bars, tempo and seconds in Logic Pro’s Piano Roll.

Common student struggles ·

  • The notes go in but the tune sounds wrong: check the grid setting and the note lengths against the chart, bar by bar (Logic quick reference, page 8). Remember that chart bar 1 is the pickup bar.
  • The chords play but the arpeggio sounds wrong: compare the notes with the chord table on page 16, and check that the Arpeggiator is in the MIDI FX slot of the arpeggio track, not of the melody track.
  • The sound is not chip-like: use a square, pulse or triangle wave, one note at a time, with a short release and no reverb (page 15).
  • The length is off: use bars × 240 ÷ BPM and the length helper (page 19), then set the end of the project at the right bar.

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Arrange the chorus only: enter the last beat of chart bar 13 and chart bars 14 to 21 (pages 13–14), loop that eight-bar chorus, and use the Up pattern on every chord.

More Advanced: Give the last chorus a key change: move it up two semitones with Transpose in the Region inspector (C major becomes D major), then rewrite the chords and arpeggios to match (page 16 shows how a chord’s notes are built). Or add a second drum pattern for the bridge.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Wrong notes: the chart pages (pages 12–14) list every note by bar, and the Logic quick reference (page 8) shows the grid.
  • A wrong arpeggio: the chord table and the four patterns (page 16) spell every chord.
  • A sound that is not chip-like: the four channel cards (page 15) and the noise drum patterns (page 17).
  • A length that is off: the length helper and the Layer map (page 19) and the worked example (pages 10–11).

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 23). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Melody & Notes (rubric row 1): open the project and compare the Piano Roll with the chart (pages 12–14), bar by bar.
  • Chip Sounds and Arps & Chords (rubric rows 2 and 3): check that the lead is a square or pulse wave, the bass a triangle and the drum noise, and that each chord’s arpeggio uses the right notes (page 16).
  • Form & Length (rubric row 4): compare the project with the Layer map (page 19) and check the arithmetic: bars × 240 ÷ tempo must be between 45 and 75 seconds.
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): My plan (page 18), the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 26) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 26): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

Notes / observations ·

8-Bit HolidayLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools an 8-Bit Holiday arrangement is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 to 3 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

New project and tracksFile › New…, then add a Software Instrument track for each channel with Track › New Tracks…. Name them Lead, Arps, Bass and Drum so that your Layer map matches (page 19).
Tempo and time signatureType the tempo in the control bar (120 to 150 BPM) and keep 4/4. The chart is in 4/4 too, so nothing needs to change.
Make a region, open the Piano RollWith the Pencil tool, click on the track where bar 1 starts to make an empty MIDI region, then double-click it to open the Piano Roll editor.
Draw notes and chordsPick a grid value in the Piano Roll’s Snap menu (for example, 1/16 note) so notes land on the chart’s sixteenth-note grid, click with the Pencil to add a note and drag its right edge to change its length. Draw a chord as notes stacked in one column. Logic names middle C “C3” by default; the chart calls it C4, so subtract one from the chart’s octave number when you read the roll.
Fix wrong notes, move a regionSelect a note and drag it up or down a row, or press delete. To move a whole region, change Transpose in the Region inspector by a number of semitones (12 is one octave).
Square, pulse and triangle soundsOn a Software Instrument track choose Retro Synth or Alchemy. In Retro Synth, use the Analog engine and pick a square, pulse or triangle wave for the oscillator (a pulse wave has a pulse width control); Alchemy can do the same from a basic square or triangle source if your version offers one. If a menu names the waves differently, pick the shape that matches the picture on page 15. Set a short release, no reverb, and Mono if the instrument has that setting.
ArpeggiatorClick a MIDI FX slot at the top of the arpeggio track’s channel strip and choose Arpeggiator. Set the Note Order (Up, Down, Up & Down), the Rate (1/16) and the Octave Range, then play or draw a chord: the plug-in plays its notes one at a time. Draw the chord as notes stacked in one column of the region (page 16). If a setting has a different name in your version, use the closest one.
Triangle bassAnother Retro Synth or Alchemy track with a triangle wave, Mono if available and a short release. Play the chord roots, C2 to A2 (page 14).
Noise drumMake a Drum Machine Designer track and open its Step Sequencer: click the 16 steps for each sound, as on the patterns on page 17. Give each pad a very short noise sound; a synthesizer’s noise source with a quick envelope works too, if your instrument has one.
Bitcrusher and Channel EQClick an Audio FX slot on the channel strip and choose Bitcrusher (look in the Distortion group of the plug-in menu). Lower Resolution and Downsampling for a grainier sound and set Mix to taste. Put it on one channel, or on the Stereo Out to crush everything. Add Channel EQ to the lead and the arps and cut the low end they do not need, so the bass owns it.
Automation, mixing and bouncePress A to show automation, pick a parameter such as the Bitcrusher’s Mix or a channel’s Volume, and draw points. Press X for the Mixer. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

8-Bit HolidayTune sources and the public-domain rule
TEACHER
Tune

The carol is in the public domain and is printed here as a note chart only. Pitches and lengths are checked against two independent transcriptions in code.

Tune“O Come, All Ye Faithful” (Latin: Adeste fideles), the whole melody: 21 chart bars, 62 notes
Composer, dateAttributed to John Francis Wade (about 1711–1786). Many hymn books date the tune to about 1743; the earliest dated manuscript is from 1751. Either way it is far older than the 1930 cutoff, so it is in the public domain.
In this kitC major, 4/4, a one-beat pickup, 21 chart bars, range G4 to G5 (eight different notes, all white keys)
Not includedNo lyrics, no recordings and no published edition. The words are not printed; students make every sound themselves.

Where the notes come from ·

  • Transcription 1 (G major): the melody of the Open Hymnal Project’s setting of “A Hymnal” (Episcopal, 1916), hymn 72, a free score of the public-domain tune.
  • Transcription 2 (C major): a second, independent transcription of the same tune, typed in C major.
  • The chart: transcription 1 moved up five semitones (a perfect fourth), so G major becomes C major. All three agree note for note and length for length. In some other editions the F# in hymn-book bar 12 (chart bar 13) is held for two beats; the chart uses the version that passes through E, as the 1916 hymnal does.

The public-domain rule ·

In 2026 a melody is in the public domain in the United States when it was first published in 1930 or earlier (1931 from 1 January 2027). Recordings, film and pop versions, and their arrangements are separate works, so students must not use them. If a student suggests another tune, check its first publication date before you approve it.

Why C major ·

The carol is usually printed in G major. Moving every note up five semitones puts it in C major: every note is a white key, and the lead sits in a bright range, G4 to G5. A strong student can move the project back to G major with Transpose in the Region inspector (down five semitones) and rewrite the chords.

Reading the chart

Pitch names run down the left; middle C is C4 (Logic’s piano roll calls it C3). Bold lines are bars, gray lines are beats. A note’s length is the name beside it: 16th, eighth, quarter, half, or dotted (a dotted quarter is one and a half beats). Chords are one idea that fits, not the only one.

The pickup bar

The carol starts on beat 4: one note before the first full bar. Chart bar 1 holds that note after three beats of rest, so chart bar 2 is the first bar of a hymn book. The last chart bar holds three beats and a rest, so the pickup and the last bar add up to one bar.

10 · Checking the numbers

One bar of 4/4 at 120 BPM lasts 240 ÷ 120 = 2 seconds, so 33 bars = 66 seconds, inside 45 to 75. The sections are 4 + 9 + 4 + 8 + 8 bars = 33. A moment in bar b happens at (b minus 1) × 2 seconds: bar 18 is 17 × 2 = 34 seconds, 0:34. All four channels play from bar 14 to the end. Three feedback questions (invented): 1. Which channel did you hear first, and what did it play? 2. When did you know which carol it was? 3. Where did the Bitcrusher change the sound most?

STUDENT
Class 2
NameDate

A classic game chip had only a few channels, and each channel plays one note at a time. This project uses four: two pulse-type waves, a triangle and noise. Because a channel cannot play a chord, the arps channel spells chords out one note after another (page 16).

ChannelWhat it doesIn Logic (starting settings)
1 · LEAD
Square wave		Plays the melody. A square wave switches between high and low, half of each cycle at each level: bright, buzzy and hollow. Keep it the loudest channel.Retro Synth (Analog engine) or Alchemy: pick the square wave, Mono if there is a Mono setting, a short attack and release, no reverb.
2 · ARPS
Pulse wave, 25%		Plays the chords as arpeggios. A narrower pulse, high for a quarter of each cycle, is thinner and more nasal than a square, so it sits behind the lead.A second Retro Synth or Alchemy track with a pulse wave (pulse width about 25%). Put the Arpeggiator in its MIDI FX slot (page 16). Keep it lower and quieter than the lead.
3 · BASS
Triangle wave		Plays the chord roots (page 14). A triangle wave is soft and round, with weaker high harmonics than a square, so it makes a good bass.Retro Synth or Alchemy with a triangle wave, Mono if available and a short release. Play the roots from C2 to A2.
4 · DRUM
Noise		Plays the drum. Noise has no pitch: a short burst is a hat, a longer burst a snare and a short, very low one a kick.Drum Machine Designer and its Step Sequencer (page 17), one pad per sound, or a synthesizer’s noise source with a quick envelope.
Bitcrusher · the lo-fi switch

What it does. It lowers the sound’s bit depth (Resolution) and its sample rate (Downsampling) on purpose, so the sound turns grainy and a little metallic. The lower the numbers, the more crushed it sounds.

In Logic. Click an Audio FX slot on a channel strip and choose Bitcrusher (look in the Distortion group of the plug-in menu). Put it on one channel, or on the Stereo Out to crush everything. Start with Mix at about half, then lower Resolution and Downsampling until it sounds like a game.

Try. Crush the intro more than the chorus, and automate Mix (press A) so the sound changes from section to section. Keep the arps and the bass clear: too much crush hides a chord.

It plays one note at a time, with a short release.
It has its own register: bass lowest, then the arps, then the lead on top.
No reverb on the chip channels: a chip is dry.
I can say which channel plays any note I point to.

Pattern B · Run · busy, for a chorus

Drums. Kick on beat 1, the “and” of 2 and beat 3; snare on beats 2 and 4; hat on every sixteenth note.

8-Bit Holiday

Music Technology | Chiptune Arrangement Project | 4 class periods

Overview ·

Rebuild a carol as an 8-bit game tune. Enter the melody of “O Come, All Ye Faithful”, a public-domain tune from the 1700s, into Logic Pro from a piano-roll note chart and play it on a square-wave lead. Then build the other three channels of a classic game-music chip: arpeggios that spell out the chords, a triangle-wave bass and a noise drum, and crush it all with a Bitcrusher so it sounds lo-fi. Your finished arrangement is 45 to 75 seconds long at 120 to 150 BPM, and your classmates should still recognize the carol.

Goals ·

  • Enter a melody into Logic Pro’s Piano Roll from a note chart, with the right pitches and lengths
  • Build four chip channels: a square-wave lead, arpeggiated chords, a triangle-wave bass and a noise drum
  • Spell the chords of a key and play them as arpeggios with the Arpeggiator MIDI plug-in
  • Plan an arrangement in bars and seconds, and prove that its length is between 45 and 75 seconds

Key Vocabulary ·

ChiptuneMusic made with the simple sounds of early game chips: a few channels of square, triangle and noise waves, often with fast arpeggios
Square waveA wave that switches between a high and a low level, spending half of each cycle at each; it sounds bright, buzzy and hollow
ArpeggioThe notes of a chord played one after another instead of together; a chip channel plays one note at a time, so an arpeggio suggests a chord
BitcrusherAn effect that lowers a sound’s bit depth and sample rate on purpose, so it sounds grainy and lo-fi

Tips for Success ·

  • Chip music is about limits. Each channel plays one note at a time, so let an arpeggio suggest the chord instead of stacking notes.
  • Keep the arpeggios quieter and lower than the lead, so the melody stays on top.
  • Add one channel at a time and listen before you add the next.
  • Save a new version of your project before each big experiment, so you can always go back.
