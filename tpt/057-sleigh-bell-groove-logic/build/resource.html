What this is ·

A 3-class-period percussion project for high school music technology. Every student builds a holiday percussion groove with no melody in Logic Pro: a Drum Machine Designer kit of sleigh bells, shaker, tambourine, wood block, kick, snare and hi-hat, programmed in the Step Sequencer on a 16-step grid. They program 4 bars straight, add swing and compare the two feels by ear and by the numbers, then grow the groove to 8 bars. Students plan it, build and mix it, present it and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look at the cards and the swing table (pages 11–13) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–23), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–6), Class 2 (slides 7–9) and Class 3 (slides 10–14). Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

No audio included

No audio, recordings, lyrics, artists or albums are named or included, and no melody or song is notated. Students make every sound themselves, from the drum kits, samples and plug-ins in Logic.

Make it fit your class ·

  • Feedback: students write three questions and answer classmates’ on a class board or on paper.
  • Grading: the rubric, /25, the average of the student’s and your score (page 20).
In one sentence

Students learn that a groove is layers on a 16-step grid, and that swing is the second note of each pair arriving a measurable moment late.

Sleigh Bell GrooveProject overview
OVERVIEW
Percussion project
The project in one line

Sleigh Bell Groove · Percussion · Logic Pro
Program a groove, then hear it straight and swung.

What students make ·

One original percussion groove with no melody: 4 bars programmed straight, a swung copy, and a final 8-bar groove with a variation and a fill. It uses at least five of seven layers (sleigh bells, shaker, tambourine, wood block, kick, snare and hi-hat) in a Drum Machine Designer kit, programmed on a 16-step grid in the Step Sequencer at 90 to 120 BPM, and it is mixed with volume, Channel EQ and a Compressor. Students hand in two bounced files and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play both feels and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a groove is built from simple parts. Students learn that a rhythm is a stack of layers on a grid, that feel comes from tiny timing differences measured in milliseconds, and they practice programming a 16-step pattern, comparing straight with swing, and doing the arithmetic of bars, tempo and seconds in Logic Pro’s Step Sequencer.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Build a Drum Machine Designer kit with at least five percussion layers, such as sleigh bells, shaker, tambourine, wood block, kick, snare and hi-hat
  • Program a 4-bar groove in the Step Sequencer on a 16-step grid, with four steps to each beat
  • Compare straight and swing feel, and explain swing in percent and in milliseconds
  • Plan an 8-bar groove in bars and seconds, and prove its length with bars × 240 ÷ BPM

SETUP
Teacher

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav files, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The step-grid cards and the swing table, printed (pages 11–13), or shown on the projector.
  • Sounds for the pads: a Logic drum kit from the Library, or sounds the students record themselves (jingle bells, keys, a shaker).
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–23).
  • A MIDI keyboard or pad controller is optional: every hit can be clicked in with the mouse.

Before Class 1 ·

Read the swing page (page 13) so you can answer ‘how late is late?’ in milliseconds.
Open Logic Pro on a student computer: can you make a Drum Machine Designer track, open its Step Sequencer and set 16 steps at a 1/16 rate?
Find the swing setting in your version (see page 8) and note which scale it uses.
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Hits land on the wrong step, swing seems to do nothing, the bells and shaker cover the kick and snare, or the groove is the wrong length. A listening day pairs well with this project: play the finished grooves without naming the tempo and let the class say which are straight and which are swung. If you want a feel reference, name a carol your students know and tap its tempo; the kit prints none.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: which layer is that? Which grid steps are you using? Can you hear the swing yet?
  • On Class 1, tap 90, 105 and 120 BPM on the desk and have students pick the tempo that feels right for a sleigh-bell groove.
  • A student who is stuck can start from the shaker card, which has a hit on every step, then add the kick and snare.

Finding swing in your version ·

Check before Class 2

Logic’s controls move between versions. Look for a Swing control in the Step Sequencer’s pattern settings, and for Q-Swing in a MIDI region’s Quantize settings, if your version shows them. If a scale starts at 0 for straight, tell students to find the even setting and call it 50 percent.

Keep the volume moderate

Bells, shakers and hats are bright and can be tiring in headphones. Tell students to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

Sleigh Bell GrooveTeacher notes · the big idea
TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a groove is built from simple parts. Students learn that a rhythm is a stack of layers on a grid, that feel comes from tiny timing differences measured in milliseconds, and they practice programming a 16-step pattern, comparing straight with swing, and doing the arithmetic of bars, tempo and seconds in Logic Pro’s Step Sequencer.

Common student struggles ·

  • The hits are in the wrong place: check which row is which pad and count the steps against the card, 1 e & a for each beat (pages 11–12). Remember that four steps make one beat and sixteen make one bar.
  • Swing seems to do nothing: the layers on the beat do not move. A 16th-note swing moves only the “e” and “a” hits, so use the shaker or a 16th-note hat to hear it (page 13).
  • The groove sounds crowded: turn the bells and shaker down, give each layer its own steps and use the Channel EQ to cut the low end the high layers do not need (page 8).
  • The length is off: use bars × 240 ÷ BPM and the length helper (page 15), then set the end of the project at the right bar.

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Use three layers only (sleigh bells, kick and snare), keep one 4-bar groove for all 8 bars, and compare straight with one swing setting, 60 percent.

More Advanced: Program a second groove with 8th-note swing instead of 16th-note swing (set the Step Sequencer’s step rate to 1/8 if your version allows it) and work out in milliseconds how far the “&” moves. Or add velocity changes and a second fill in bar 4.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Hits in the wrong place: the pattern cards list every hit with its count (pages 11–12), and the Logic quick reference (page 8) shows the grid.
  • Swing that seems to do nothing: the timing table and the pair diagram (page 13) show which steps move and by how many milliseconds.
  • A crowded mix: the Layer map (page 15) shows when each layer enters, and My groove plan (page 14) gives each one a job.
  • A length that is off: the length helper (page 15) and the worked example (pages 9–10).

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 20). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Grid & Patterns (rubric row 1): open the Step Sequencer and compare the hits with the cards or with the student’s My groove plan (pages 11–12).
  • Sounds & Mix (rubric row 2): listen to the bounce. Can you hear the kick and snare under the bells and shaker?
  • Straight vs Swing (rubric row 3): read the listening worksheet (page 16) and ask which grid steps swing moves; the answer is on page 13.
  • Form & Length (rubric row 4): compare the project with the Layer map (page 15) and check the arithmetic: bars × 240 ÷ tempo.
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): My groove plan (page 14), the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 23) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 23): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

Notes / observations ·

Sleigh Bell GrooveLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a Sleigh Bell Groove is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 and 2 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

New project and kit trackFile › New…, then make a Software Instrument track. Click its instrument slot and choose Drum Machine Designer, or load a drum kit from the Library (a library kit is a Drum Machine Designer with a sound on each pad). Name the track Sleigh Kit. Set the tempo in the control bar (90 to 120 BPM) and keep 4/4.
Give each pad a soundEach pad in Drum Machine Designer holds its own sound. Drag an audio file onto a pad, or open a pad’s Quick Sampler to import a sound or record your own, for example a ring of jingle bells, keys or a shaker close to the Mac’s microphone. A library kit can supply the kick, snare and hat. Name the pads Bells, Shaker, Tambourine, Wood Block, Kick, Snare and Hat so your Layer map matches (page 15).
Open the Step SequencerWith the Drum Machine Designer track selected, open the Step Sequencer (look in the editors area at the bottom of the window; the exact button differs by version). Each row is one pad. Click a step to switch it on and click it again to switch it off.
Pattern length and rateSet the pattern to 16 steps and the step rate to 1/16, so every step is a sixteenth note: four steps make one beat and sixteen make one bar of 4/4, as on the cards (pages 11–12). If your version offers a different rate, such as 1/8, one step is an eighth note and the grid changes with it.
Velocity and accentsIf your Step Sequencer shows a velocity lane under the steps, draw softer hits for the in-between steps and louder ones on the beats. Bells and shakers sound best when the hits are not all the same strength.
SwingLook for a Swing control in the Step Sequencer’s pattern settings, if your version shows one. Other places, if your version has them: the Q-Swing setting in a MIDI region’s Quantize settings, and a swing or feel control on a Drummer or percussion player. Scales differ: on most tools 50 percent is straight, but some start at 0. Find the setting that sounds even first, call it straight, and use the table on page 13 to check what you hear.
Loop, copy and extendPress C to turn Cycle on, so the 4 or 8 bars repeat while you work. Copy the 4-bar region to bar 5 (for example, Option-drag it), then edit the copy to make the variation and the fill in bar 8.
Mix: volume, EQ and CompressorPress X for the Mixer. Put a Channel EQ on the track and cut the low end the bells and shaker do not need; Drum Machine Designer can also show a separate mixer channel for each pad if your version offers it. Add a Compressor to the kick and snare or to the whole kit and lower its threshold until it just tames the loudest hits.
Automation, mixing and bouncePress A to show automation, pick a parameter such as volume, and draw points. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

An invented plan on paper: an 8-bar groove at 100 BPM. No audio is included. Student projects will differ. Page 1 of 2: the plan and groove B.

1 · A weak plan

I will make a holiday drum beat with bells and swing.

It names no tempo, no layers, no length and no swing amount.

2 · A clear plan

My groove is 8 bars at 100 BPM in 4/4 = 19.2 seconds. Bars 1 to 4 are groove A: bells, a kick that pushes on the “e” of 2 and 4, a backbeat snare from bar 3 and steady hi-hat eighths, with the shaker joining in bar 2. Bars 5 to 8 are groove B: the bells gallop and the tambourine and wood block join, with a snare fill in bar 8. The whole groove has 60 percent swing, so each swung 16th lands 180 ms into its pair instead of 150 ms.

8 · Checking the numbers

One bar of 4/4 at 100 BPM lasts 240 ÷ 100 = 2.4 seconds, so 8 bars = 8 × 2.4 = 19.2 seconds and 4 bars = 9.6 seconds. A 16th lasts 15000 ÷ 100 = 150 ms, so a pair of 16ths lasts 300 ms. At 60% swing the second note of a pair starts at 0.60 × 300 = 180 ms instead of 150 ms, which is 30 ms late; at 66% it starts at 198 ms, 48 ms late. A moment in bar b happens at (b − 1) × 2.4 seconds: bar 5 is 4 × 2.4 = 9.6 seconds. The layers playing in bars 1 to 8 are 3, 4, 5, 5, 7, 7, 7, 7.

9 · Three feedback questions (invented)

1. Which layer did you hear first, and what did it play? 2. Could you hear the swing? Which layers made it clear? 3. Where did the groove change, and what caused the change?

A bar of 4/4 has 4 beats, and each beat holds 4 sixteenth notes, so one bar is 16 grid steps. Count every beat 1 e & a. Each card shows one layer’s hits on that grid: the numbers 1 to 16 are the grid steps, the counts sit under them, and the shaded steps are the beats. Sleigh Bell Groove

Music Technology | Percussion Programming Project | 3 class periods

Overview ·

Program a holiday percussion groove with no melody. In Logic Pro, build a Drum Machine Designer kit of sleigh bells, shaker, tambourine, wood block, kick, snare and hi-hat. Use the Step Sequencer to program a 4-bar groove on a 16-step grid, one layer at a time. Then copy it, add swing and compare the straight feel with the swung feel by ear and by the numbers: at 120 BPM a 16th note lasts 125 milliseconds, and a swung one lands later. Grow it into an 8-bar groove with a variation and a fill. Your groove runs at 90 to 120 BPM, and nobody needs to play an instrument or read music.

Goals ·

  • Build a Drum Machine Designer kit with at least five percussion layers, such as sleigh bells, shaker, tambourine, wood block, kick, snare and hi-hat
  • Program a 4-bar groove in the Step Sequencer on a 16-step grid, with four steps to each beat
  • Compare straight and swing feel, and explain swing in percent and in milliseconds
  • Plan an 8-bar groove in bars and seconds, and prove its length with bars × 240 ÷ BPM

Key Vocabulary ·

16-step gridOne bar of 4/4 split into sixteen equal steps, four to each beat, counted 1 e & a 2 e & a 3 e & a 4 e & a
SwingA feel in which the second note of each pair is played late, so the pair is long then short instead of even; its amount is given in percent
LayerOne percussion part of a groove, such as the shaker or the kick, programmed on its own pad and its own row of the grid
GrooveThe pattern and feel of a rhythm that makes people want to move, built from layers that fit together

Requirements ·

Build a Drum Machine Designer kit with at least 5 of the 7 layers (sleigh bells, shaker, tambourine, wood block, kick, snare, hi-hat) and name every pad (page 14)
Program a 4-bar straight groove on the 16-step grid, using at least 3 of the pattern cards or patterns of your own (pages 11–12)
Set a tempo from 90 to 120 BPM in 4/4 and write it on My groove plan (page 14)
Make a swung copy of your groove, try 50, 60 and 66 percent, and compare the feels on the listening worksheet (pages 13 and 16)
Grow the groove into 8 bars: bars 1 to 4 are your groove, bars 5 to 8 a variation with a fill in bar 8
Balance the layers with volume, Channel EQ and a Compressor, so the bells and shaker do not cover the kick and snare
Show the length arithmetic for your 8 bars on the Layer map (page 15), then reflect (page 23)

Tips for Success ·

  • Program one layer at a time and listen to it with the kick before you add the next one.
  • Bells and shakers are bright and carry far: turn them down until the kick and snare are clear.
  • Small amounts of swing, about 55 to 66 percent, are the most useful. Much more than that starts to sound like a different rhythm.
  • Save a new version of your project before each big experiment, so you can always go back.
