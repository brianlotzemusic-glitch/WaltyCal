# SOURCES: 089 July Music Sub Plans K-5

Every date, name and fact printed in the product, with where it was checked (WebFetch or WebSearch, 27 Nov 2026, unless a line says otherwise). Pages 10-12 are Day 1 (the reading), pages 16-18 are Day 3 (sounds of summer),
pages 26-28 are Day 6 (jobs), page 8 is the July facts page, page 37 is the bingo clue list. Wording is kept to the basics the sources agree on, in our own words.
No lyrics, recordings, speeches or sheet music are used and none is needed: the sub never performs or sings anything. No tune is taught. No piece of music is named on a student sheet; the facts page names two public-domain pieces ("The Star-Spangled Banner" and "The Stars and Stripes Forever") only to answer a question, with the year and the law that are sourced below. No poem by another author is quoted (the four-line poem is original). No copyrighted characters.
Objects are shown as simple drawings made in code (sun, wave, cricket, firework, kite, drum, flip-flops, popsicle, star) and are labeled by name. The hero art on the cover (firework burst, beach ball, popsicle, drum with stars) is the pre-made art in art/hero.png, used as is: its sky-blue fills match the blue accent, so nothing is recolored.

Pages that could NOT be fetched (so nothing printed rests only on them): loc.gov item ihas.200000015 (403); bls.gov musical instrument repairers page (403; fact 9 now rests on O*NET, fetched by QA); britannica.com (not fetched). The Library of Congress and NARA pages for the Declaration were not needed: archives.gov and Wikipedia agree.
Where sources disagree or are silent the product prints the safer wording or leaves the detail out: the exact day the anthem law was signed (March 3 in most sources, March 4 in one passage of Wikipedia: only 1931 is printed); the first performance of "The Stars and Stripes Forever" (1896 composed, 1897 first performance in some sources: only "wrote ... in 1896" is printed);
the origin and date of "Yankee Doodle" (not printed); the year the sousaphone was first built (not printed). The pages make no "first" claims. Years printed: 1776, 1814, 1896, 1931, 1987 (and the copyright year 2026).
Hemisphere is named wherever a season is stated (July is summer in the Northern Hemisphere and winter in the Southern). Independence Day is optional, secular and appears only on Day 1, the facts page and the teacher notes; every student sheet that names it carries a Summer choice line, which --check enforces. Fireworks appear only as pictures and the sound word BOOM, which a student may whisper.

### 14. Patriotic music facts on the facts page (answers only, not taught): 'The Star-Spangled Banner' has words by Francis Scott Key from 1814 and became the national anthem by law in 1931; John Philip Sousa wrote the march 'The Stars and Stripes Forever' in 1896, and Congress made it the national march in 1987; these pieces are old (public domain)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star-Spangled_Banner (fetched 27 Nov 2026: Key wrote the poem 'on September 14, 1814'; Congress passed a joint resolution and President Hoover signed it on March 3, 1931 (the article also gives March 4 in one passage, so the pages print only the year 1931)); https://history.house.gov/HistoricalHighlight/Detail/35621 (fetched 27 Nov 2026: Hoover signed the bill designating it the national anthem on March 3, 1931; Key wrote it during the 1814 siege of Fort McHenry); https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/march-3/the-star-spangled-banner-becomes-official (fetched 27 Nov 2026: official on March 3, 1931; Key wrote the lyrics in 1814). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stars_and_Stripes_Forever (fetched 27 Nov 2026: Sousa wrote the march in 1896; Congress made it the national march in 1987) and a WebSearch summary (27 Nov 2026) citing Public Law 100-186 (Dec 11, 1987). Britannica dates the first performance 1897, so the pages say 'wrote ... in 1896' only. Public domain: words 1814 and a march from 1896 are both published well before 1931. No lyrics are printed and no tune is taught or named. 'My Country, 'Tis of Thee' (words by Samuel Francis Smith, 1831, per Wikipedia and a search summary) and 'Yankee Doodle' (an old tune; its date is disputed in the sources, so no year is printed) are NOT mentioned on the pages.

## Rules followed
Respectful, secular and inclusive wording; short facts only; no speech quotes, no lyrics, no recordings and no named songs on student sheets; no patriotism quiz, pledge or ceremony; Independence Day optional through a Summer choice line; no guessing of students' families or how they celebrate; mention of the hemisphere wherever a season is stated; no technology, singing, performing or music reading by the sub.
