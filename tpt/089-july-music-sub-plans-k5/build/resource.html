July Music Sub Plans K–5 · 8 no-prep, no-tech days · Summer sounds, marching bands, parades

Why July matters

July is the seventh month of the year. In the Northern Hemisphere, July is a summer month; in the Southern Hemisphere, it is a winter month. Summer is a good time for music that happens outdoors: waves at a beach, crickets at dusk, marching bands in parades, concerts in a park. In the United States, July 4 is Independence Day, and many communities hold parades and concerts around it. The days teach summer sounds, speech rhythm, marching bands and music jobs, and treat Independence Day as a short, optional, secular fact.

Independence Day: optional and inclusive

  • Independence Day is described only in plain facts: on July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress approved the Declaration of Independence, and today many communities celebrate with parades, picnics, concerts and fireworks. Some families do not celebrate it, so it appears only on Day 1, and every Day 1 sheet carries a Summer choice line that skips it and asks about summer sounds instead.
  • No patriotism quiz or ceremony is used, and nobody is asked how their family celebrates. The patriotic music facts on the facts page are for answering a student’s question: no lyrics are printed, nobody has to perform anything, and no tune is taught.

Summer sounds and movement

  • Waves, crickets and fireworks appear only as pictures and sound words on paper. A student who dislikes loud sounds may whisper the word BOOM. Nothing depends on a beach or any trip: students draw any summer place they like.
  • Students march in place quietly to a pencil tap, and may sit and pat instead.

Marching band instruments: simple and accurate

  • A marching band usually has brass, woodwind and percussion instruments. The days keep these to short, simple definitions: brass players buzz their lips into a mouthpiece (trumpet, sousaphone), woodwinds are blown into or across (flute), and percussion instruments are hit, shaken or scraped (drums). The sousaphone is a brass instrument in the tuba family that coils around the player’s body.

Why subs can teach it

Each lesson has timed steps, words to say and a K–2 and a 3–5 sheet. Rhythm work is speech rhythm: clap the syllables of words. Nobody has to read music or make music; games need only voices, hands and a pencil.

Standards

Supports relating music to varied contexts (MU:Cn11.0) and generating musical ideas (MU:Cr1.1), National Core Arts Standards, Music, grades K–5.

Prints well in black and white (facts: page 8). All lessons, chants, games, words and art are original; no lyrics or copyrighted characters are used.

Tips for a calm class ·
  • Use the teacher’s attention signal from the welcome sheet (page 3), or clap two times and have the class clap back two.
  • If a game gets too loud, switch to a worksheet or to Cricket Quiet.
  • Let students take turns as the leader. Keep turns short.
  • Students may know more than the page says, or may feel nervous. Thank them kindly.
Not sure which game to pick? Use Cricket Quiet to settle a class, Word Claps to wake it up, and Parade Echo to finish with a smile.

NameDate
Summer choice: Independence Day is optional. To skip it, skip the last 3 sentences of the passage (they begin “In the United States”) and question 6, and ask the class instead: What is a summer sound you like?
Read the passage. Underline one fact that was new to you. Then answer the questions in complete sentences.
July is the seventh month of the year. In the Northern Hemisphere it is a summer month, and in the Southern Hemisphere it is a winter month. Summer has its own sounds, such as waves rolling onto a beach and crickets chirping. A male cricket chirps by rubbing parts of its wings together. Many parks and towns hold outdoor concerts in the summer, and marching bands often take part in parades. A marching band is a group of musicians who march while they make music. Its instruments are usually brass, woodwind and percussion. One brass instrument, the sousaphone, coils around the player’s body so it can be carried on a march. It was named after the bandleader John Philip Sousa. A marching band keeps a steady beat so that everyone steps together. Every musician, from a drummer in a parade to a listener on the lawn, starts by listening. In the United States, July 4 is Independence Day. On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress approved the Declaration of Independence, and today many communities celebrate with parades, picnics, concerts and fireworks. Some families do not celebrate this day, and that is fine: everyone can enjoy the sounds of summer.

Summer SyllablesDay 2 · Speech rhythm: clap the syllables · no tech, no prep
DAY 2
Sub plan
GoalStudents clap the syllables of summer words, hear how a short poem has a rhythm, and build a four-beat chant.
You needPencils, crayons, the worksheets and the word-rhythm chart (page 7). No music reading needed: every word shows its claps.
CopiesK–2: page 14
3–5: page 15
about 35 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Steady pat
    Pat your legs slowly and evenly, like a clock. Say: “Copy me!” Students pat with you. Say: “This steady pulse is called the beat.”
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Clap the words
    Say a word, then say it again while clapping once for each syllable: sun (1 clap), sum-mer (2 claps), sou-sa-phone (3 claps), wa-ter-mel-on (4 claps). Students echo. Try five or six words from page 7.
  3. 5 minStep 3
    Summer word claps
    Say: “Every word has a rhythm. Who has a summer word to clap?” Go around the room: each student says a summer word they like (beach, popsicle, swimming, anything), and the class claps its syllables together. A student who would rather not may pass.
  4. 5 minStep 4
    Poem and chart echoes
    Read this original poem line by line, clapping each syllable (every line has 4 claps): Waves roll in slow. Crickets chirp soft. Parade drums boom. Summer is here. Then read a line from page 7, one box per beat, clapping the dots: one dot = ta, two dots in one box = ti-ti, shh = a silent beat (open hands). Students echo. K–2 use lines 1–4; 3–5 use all 8.
  5. 10 minStep 5
    Worksheet
    K–2 page 14, 3–5 page 15. Read Part A aloud and clap each word with the class before they color or write. Answers are on page 41.
Extra time? Word Claps from the quick games (page 6). Short class? Do only Part A and Part B; skip the poem.

Summer Sound SceneDay 3 · Design a scene full of sound words · no tech, no prep
DAY 3
Sub plan
GoalStudents think of the sounds of a summer place, match sound words to four sound makers, and draw and label a summer scene.
You needPencils, crayons and the worksheets. Nothing else is needed: students think, say sound words and draw.
CopiesK–2: page 17
3–5: page 18
about 35 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Listening minute
    Say: “Detectives, close your eyes or look down. Listen for one minute. Count the different sounds you hear on your fingers, with no talking.” Wait one minute. Ask: What sounds did you find? Take four or five answers (a door, a chair, a pencil, a clock, a hallway).
  2. 5 minStep 2
    Four sound makers
    Say: “Summer things make sound too. A wave can swish, a cricket can chirp, a firework can boom, and flip-flops can slap.” Point to the four pictures on the sheet and say each sound word with the class, in a soft voice. Clap the syllables of each word. A student who dislikes loud sounds can whisper the word BOOM.
  3. 5 minStep 3
    Sound word echo
    Say: “Let’s make the sounds with our mouths, very softly.” Say each sound word in a different way: tiny, huge, slow and fast. Ask three students: Which summer sound would you add?
  4. 15 minStep 4
    Sound scene
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 17, 3–5 page 18. Say: “Draw a summer place and show what makes sound there.” Read the directions aloud. Students may draw a beach, a park, a backyard, a lake or any place they like, or a parade. There are no wrong answers.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Gallery walk
    Students hold up their scenes one table at a time. Ask three students: Which sound in your picture is the loudest, and which is the softest?
Extra time? Cricket Quiet or Summer Sound Share from the quick games (page 6). Short class? Skip the gallery walk.

March and FreezeDay 4 · Move to a steady tap and freeze · no tech, no prep
DAY 4
Sub plan
GoalStudents move like summer things to a steady tap, march in place and freeze when it stops.
You needPencils, the worksheets, a clear space to move, and the picture cards on page 22 (point at them, or cut them out with scissors).
CopiesK–2: page 20
3–5: page 21
about 35 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Steady march
    Say: “A marching band keeps a steady beat so everyone steps together. Let’s try it.” Everyone stands. Tap a pencil on a desk at a steady speed while students march in place, quietly. Stop tapping and everyone freezes. Do it three times. A student may sit and pat their legs instead.
  2. 5 minStep 2
    Cricket Quiet
    Do Cricket Quiet (page 6): raise your fingers slowly from one to ten while the class counts silently in their heads. Do it twice. Then say: “Our class made ten quiet seconds together.”
  3. 10 minStep 3
    Picture cards
    Say: “When I tap, you move. When I stop, you freeze like a statue. Keep your hands to yourself.” Hold up (or point at) one card at a time. Students move as the card says while you tap a pencil on a desk. Use at least six cards, and try a slow tap, a fast tap and a bouncy tap (long, short, long, short, like skipping). The words under each card tell you what to say.
