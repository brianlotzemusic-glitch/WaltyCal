Teacher Notes

April Music Sub Plans K–5 · 8 no-prep, no-tech days · Jazz Month, Poetry Month, Earth Day, spring

Why April matters

April is full of ideas for a music room. Jazz Appreciation Month was created at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History in 2001, and National Poetry Month was started by the Academy of American Poets in 1996, so this bundle meets three jazz musicians (Dizzy Gillespie, Mary Lou Williams and John Coltrane), looks at improvisation, swing and call and response, and finds the rhythm in words and in a short original haiku. Earth Day, first held on April 22, 1970, and Arbor Day, the last Friday of April in the United States, bring ideas for instruments made from recycled things (drawn on paper only: nothing is built or performed), and April Fools’ Day on April 1 adds a little silliness. In the Northern Hemisphere April is spring; in the Southern Hemisphere it is autumn. The music stays simple: clapping, patting and saying words. Facts are kept short and secular.

What it is

Eight complete music lessons a substitute can teach with no tech, no instruments and no music background. Each day has a step-by-step plan with words to say and timed steps, and two worksheets: one for grades K–2 and one for grades 3–5.

The eight days

  1. Jazz Month Reader: reading about jazz, poems and Earth Day.
  2. Poem Word Claps: speech rhythm: clap the syllables.
  3. Recycled Instrument Lab: design an instrument from recycled things (a picture only).
  4. Swing and Freeze: move to a tapped beat and freeze.
  5. April Puzzle Pack: April vocabulary puzzles.
  6. Behind the Band: music jobs.
  7. Poem Chant Book: compose a four-beat chant.
  8. April Bingo Jam: review game day.

Use them in any order. The welcome sheet has a “Day #” column so the sub knows which day each class gets next.

Set up the sub tub (about 15 minutes, once)

  1. Fill in the welcome sheet (page 3).
  2. Print the plans, the quick start, the word-rhythm chart, the facts page and the sub report; copy each worksheet for your largest class.
  3. Cut out the folder labels (page 45), put each day in its own folder, and add the answer keys.

Why subs can teach it

Rhythm work is speech rhythm: clap the syllables of words, with the claps printed as dots. Nobody has to read music or make music with an instrument. Games use only voices, hands and a pencil.

Standards

Supports relating music to varied contexts (MU:Cn11.0) and generating musical ideas (MU:Cr1.1), National Core Arts Standards, Music, grades K–5.

No prep, no tech
No-prep games for the last five minutes of a class (or any gap). They need only voices, hands and a pencil.
GameHow to do itDay
Call and ReplySay a short phrase, like “jazz, swing, jazz, swing.” The class answers with the same phrase, then once softly.any
Word ClapsSay a word from the month, like “Gil-les-pie.” The class claps its syllables out loud, then holds up that many fingers.2
Stop and FreezeTap a pencil on a desk. Students move while you tap and freeze when you stop.4
Quiet RainStudents tap their fingertips softly on the desk like rain, then slower and softer until there is silence.any
Pass the PencilPass a pencil around a circle while you tap a steady beat. When you stop, that student says an April word.8
Mystery WordRead a clue from the Day 8 quiz. Students call out the answer together.8
Silly EchoFor April Fools’ Day fun, say a silly four-beat phrase, like “banana, shh, banana, shh.” The class echoes it and tries not to laugh.any
Seed to TreeStudents crouch small like seeds. Count slowly from 1 to 5 while they grow tall like a tree, then everyone freezes.4
Tips for a calm class ·
  • Use the teacher’s attention signal from the welcome sheet (page 3), or clap two times and have the class clap back two.
  • “Strong voice, not shouting” keeps every game safe and happy.
  • If a game gets too loud, switch to a worksheet or to Quiet Rain.
  • Let students take turns as the leader. Keep turns short.
  • Students may know other musicians or celebrate other traditions. Thank them and move on.
Not sure which game to pick? Use Quiet Rain to settle a class, Word Claps to wake it up, and Silly Echo to finish with a smile.

Jazz Appreciation MonthEvery AprilCreated at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History in 2001 to honor jazz as a living and historic music. April 30 is International Jazz Day.
Jazz ideasEarly 1900sJazz grew from African American communities in New Orleans, Louisiana, in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Improvisation: making up music on the spot. Swing: a bouncy rhythm that makes people want to move. Call and response: one musician or group makes a phrase and another answers.
Dizzy GillespieOctober 21, 1917 – January 6, 1993Born in Cheraw, South Carolina. Trumpeter and bandleader who helped create a style called bebop in the 1940s. In 1953 his trumpet’s bell was bent upward by accident; he liked the sound and began using trumpets made that way.
Mary Lou WilliamsMay 8, 1910 – May 28, 1981Born in Atlanta, Georgia, and raised in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Pianist, composer and arranger, known as “The Little Piano Girl” as a child. She wrote and arranged music for big bands, including Duke Ellington’s and Benny Goodman’s.
John ColtraneSeptember 23, 1926 – July 17, 1967Born in Hamlet, North Carolina, and raised in High Point. Saxophonist, composer and bandleader.
National Poetry MonthEvery AprilStarted by the Academy of American Poets in 1996. Poems have rhythm and syllables. A haiku is a short poem traditionally written in three lines of 5, 7 and 5 syllables, though many poets vary the pattern.
Earth DayApril 22First held on April 22, 1970; an estimated 20 million Americans took part. Some musicians build instruments from recycled materials: the Recycled Orchestra of Cateura, Paraguay, performs on instruments built from recycled materials.
Arbor DayLast Friday in April (April 30 in 2027)A day in the United States for planting and caring for trees. The first Arbor Day was held in Nebraska on April 10, 1872.
April Fools’ DayApril 1A day of harmless jokes and silly fun in many places. Nobody knows for sure how it began.
Spring in AprilNorthern HemisphereApril is a spring month in the Northern Hemisphere. (In the Southern Hemisphere, April is autumn.) Buds open, leaves grow, and rain taps on roofs and windows.

Read the passage. Underline one fact that was new to you. Then answer the questions in complete sentences.
April is packed with celebrations that connect to music. Jazz Appreciation Month was created at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History in 2001. Jazz grew from African American communities in New Orleans, Louisiana, more than a hundred years ago. Two ideas are at its heart. Improvisation is when a musician makes up music on the spot, and swing is a bouncy rhythm that makes listeners want to move. Dizzy Gillespie, born in South Carolina in 1917, was a trumpeter whose trumpet had a bell bent upward. Mary Lou Williams, born in 1910 and raised in Pittsburgh, was a pianist who wrote and arranged music for big bands. April is also National Poetry Month, which the Academy of American Poets started in 1996. Poems have rhythm too, and you can feel it by clapping their syllables. Earth Day was first held on April 22, 1970, and people still celebrate it by caring for the planet. Arbor Day, on the last Friday of April in the United States, is a day to plant and care for trees. In the Northern Hemisphere, April is a spring month.

Poem Word ClapsDay 2 · Speech rhythm: clap the syllables · no tech, no prep
DAY 2
Sub plan
GoalStudents clap the syllables of jazz, poetry and spring words, hear how a short poem has a rhythm, and build a four-beat chant.
You needPencils, crayons, the worksheets and the word-rhythm chart (page 7). No music reading needed: every word shows its claps.
CopiesK–2: page 14
3–5: page 15
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Steady pat
    Pat your legs slowly and evenly, like a clock. Say: “Copy me!” Students pat with you. Say: “This steady pulse is called the beat.”
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Clap the words
    Say a word, then say it again while clapping once for each syllable: jazz (1 clap), trum-pet (2 claps), pi-an-o (3 claps). Students echo. Try five or six words from page 7.
  3. 5 minStep 3
    Poem and chart echoes
    Read this original poem line by line, clapping each syllable: Soft rain on the leaves (5 claps), a green seed wakes up and grows (7 claps), April hums with sound (5 claps). Then read a line from page 7, one box per beat, clapping the dots: one dot = ta, two dots in one box = ti-ti, shh = a silent beat (open hands). Students echo. K–2 use lines 1–4; 3–5 use all 8.
  4. 15 minStep 4
    Worksheet
    K–2 page 14, 3–5 page 15. Read Part A aloud and clap each word with the class before they color or write. Answers are on page 41.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Chant parade
    Volunteers read their chant aloud while the class pats a steady beat. Everyone claps at the end.
Extra time? Word Claps from the quick games (page 6), with the names of students. Short class? Do only Part A and Part B.

Swing and FreezeDay 4 · Move to a tapped beat and freeze · no tech, no prep
DAY 4
Sub plan
GoalStudents move to a steady tap, answer the sub’s moves with their own (call and response), and freeze when it stops.
You needPencils, the worksheets, a clear space to move, and the picture cards on page 22 (point at them or cut them out).
CopiesK–2: page 20
3–5: page 21
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Rules of the freeze
    Say: “When I tap, you move. When I stop, you freeze like a statue. Keep your hands to yourself.” Practice once: tap a pencil on a desk for five seconds, stop, and wait for stillness.
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Call and response
    Say: “Jazz musicians often answer each other: one calls, the other answers.” Make a simple move (a clap, a head nod, a shoulder shrug) and say “Call!” The class copies it and says “Answer!” Do five moves. Then let a few students be the caller.
  3. 10 minStep 3
    Picture cards
    Hold up (or point at) one card at a time. Students move as the card says while you tap. Use at least six cards, and try a slow tap, a fast tap and a bouncy tap (long, short, long, short, like skipping). The words under each card tell you what to say.
  4. 10 minStep 4
    Reflection sheet
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 20, 3–5 page 21. Students sit and fill it in. Read the directions aloud.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Statue garden
    Tap a few beats, stop, and let the class freeze in a favorite pose. Walk around and name two poses you notice.
Extra time? Seed to Tree from the quick games (page 6). Short class? Use only four cards.
