Teacher Notes

Around the World, Music Edition · Grades 3–6

Students read a short passage about each of five celebrations, listen to music that you choose from that tradition, and describe what they hear with five music elements: instruments, tempo, dynamics, mood and beat feel. Then they compare and contrast, build vocabulary and reflect. No audio or recordings are included or named. You choose the music.

Learning about cultures

  • These pages are for learning about cultures and traditions through music. They are not for practicing any religion. Students are never asked to take part in a ritual, to say what they believe or to say what their family does.
  • Invite student voices without requiring them. Some students celebrate one of these, some celebrate several, some celebrate other things and some celebrate none. Make no assumptions about any family, and avoid saying “we” or “everyone.”
  • Each reading keeps to basic facts. Customs differ from place to place and from family to family, and each reading says so. Invite students to ask family or community members what they would like to add.

Choosing the music

  • Use any music from the tradition that you have the right to use: public domain or traditional music with a source you have checked, a recording licensed to your school or covered by a service your district allows, a live performance by you, a colleague or a guest, or a recording shared with permission.
  • Write the title or a short description in the “teacher-chosen example” box on each response page. One to two minutes is enough; students listen twice.
  • No tradition has one sound. If you can, choose examples that differ in style, and tell students the example is one of many.

The calendar

  • Dates are listed on each reading. Hanukkah begins on 25 Kislev, so it moves around on the usual calendar. Kwanzaa runs December 26 to January 1.
  • Lunar New Year is not a December celebration. It falls in late January or February, so it works well as a January or February lesson, and the whole pack can be taught in any month.

The facts are limited to basics that are widely documented. Lunar New Year is called by other names in different cultures; this pack uses the general name.

Pacing for each celebration (25 minutes)

1. Read together and answer the reading questions8 min
2. Listen once with no writing3 min
3. Listen again and fill in the response page10 min
4. Share and discuss4 min
One celebration (reading page and response page)25 min

Teach the 5 sets in any order. All 5 sets take 125 minutes. Add 25 minutes for the compare and contrast pages (page 16 and page 17) and 10 minutes for the reflection (page 19): 160 minutes in all.

Differentiation and support

  • Grades 3–4: read each passage aloud, listen once together and fill in the first response page as a class. Students circle choices and add a few words.
  • Grades 5–6: students read silently, write full sentences for their reasons and use the vocabulary words (page 5).
  • Support: page 4 gives word choices for every music element. Students can point to a choice and say one reason.

Scoring

The rubric (page 18) has 5 categories scored 1–4, 20 points in all. The response pages have no single correct answer: accept any answer that matches what the student heard and gives a reason. Reading questions have answers on page 20.

How to ListenFive things to listen for
GUIDE
student reference
Name Date
You are going to listen to music from five celebrations. Music from every tradition has instruments or voices, a tempo, dynamics, a mood and a beat feel. Use these words to describe what you hear. There are no wrong answers if you can give a reason from the music.
ElementAsk yourselfWords you can use
InstrumentsWhat is making the sounds?voices, strings, winds, brass, percussion, keyboard
TempoHow fast is the steady beat?slow, medium, fast, speeding up, slowing down
DynamicsHow loud or soft is it, and does it change?quiet, medium, loud, getting louder, getting softer
MoodWhat feeling does it give you?joyful, calm, festive, peaceful, lively, hopeful, serious
Beat feelCan you tap along? How does the beat move?steady and strong, gentle and flowing, bouncy, groups of 2, 3 or 4, no clear beat

Listening routine

  1. Listen once. Close your eyes or look at the floor. Just listen.
  2. Listen again. Tap the steady beat very quietly on your leg.
  3. Respond. Circle choices and write a reason for each.
  4. Share. Tell a partner one thing you heard. Say it with a music word.

Respectful listening

  • We are learning about cultures and traditions. We do not have to celebrate any of them.
  • Keep your voice off while the music plays, and keep your comments kind.
  • Share about your own family only if you want to. Everyone celebrates differently, and that is fine.

Vocabulary12 words for listening and reading
WORDS
student reference
Name Date

Read each meaning. On the line, write a sentence or a note that uses the word in your own way.

Music words

tempo

How fast or slow the beat of the music is.

dynamics

How loud or soft the music is, and how that changes.

mood

The feeling the music gives you, such as calm or joyful.

beat feel

How the steady beat moves: strong, flowing or bouncy, in groups of 2, 3 or 4.

instrument

Something that makes music, such as a drum, a flute or a violin. A voice counts too.

percussion

Instruments you hit, shake or scrape, such as drums, gongs and cymbals.

Culture words

tradition

A custom passed down over time in a family or a community.

carol

A song, often sung in a group, that is linked to Christmas.

menorah

A candleholder used at Hanukkah. It has a place for eight candles and a helper candle.

principle

A basic belief that guides how people act.

procession

A group of people moving along together, often walking, as part of a celebration.

lunar calendar

A calendar that uses the phases of the moon to mark the months.

ChristmasReading and questions
READING 1
of 5
Name Date

Christmas is celebrated on December 25. For Christians, it marks the birth of Jesus. Some Orthodox churches follow the older Julian calendar, so their Christmas falls on January 7. Christmas is observed in many countries, and families celebrate it in many different ways. Singing carols is one long-standing tradition. A carol is a song, often sung in a group, and carols are sung in many languages and musical styles. Some people who are not Christian also join in Christmas customs as part of their culture, and many people do not celebrate it at all. No two families celebrate in exactly the same way.

WhenDecember 25 (January 7 in some Orthodox churches)
WhereObserved in many countries
Key wordscarol, Julian calendar
Check your reading
  1. On what date do most Christians celebrate Christmas?
  2. What is a carol?
  3. Why do some Orthodox churches celebrate on January 7?

HanukkahReading and questions
READING 2
of 5
Name Date

Hanukkah is a Jewish holiday that lasts eight nights. It begins on the 25th day of Kislev, a month in the Hebrew calendar, so its date on the calendar most of us use changes every year. Hanukkah remembers the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem long ago. A story tells how a small amount of oil lasted eight days. Many families light a menorah, which is sometimes called a hanukkiah. It has nine branches: one for each night and a helper candle, the shamash, which is used to light the others. One more candle is lit each night. Many people also play dreidel, a game with a spinning top.

WhenEight nights, starting on 25 Kislev (usually in November or December)
WhereCelebrated by Jewish communities worldwide
Key wordsmenorah, shamash, dreidel
Check your reading
  1. How many nights does Hanukkah last?
  2. What is the shamash?
  3. Why does the date of Hanukkah change from year to year on our usual calendar?

KwanzaaReading and questions
READING 3
of 5
Name Date

Kwanzaa is a celebration of African American culture, family and community. It lasts seven days, from December 26 to January 1. Maulana Karenga created it in 1966. The name comes from a Swahili phrase, matunda ya kwanza, which means “first fruits.” Each day focuses on one of seven principles, called the Nguzo Saba, and their names are Swahili words. Families and communities often light one candle each day on a candleholder called a kinara, which holds seven candles. Many people also share a community feast called Karamu. The table below lists the seven principles in order.

WhenSeven days, December 26 to January 1
WhereCelebrated by many African American families and communities
Key wordsprinciple, kinara, Swahili
DaySwahili nameMeaningIn other words
1UmojaUnityWorking together for unity in the family and the community
2KujichaguliaSelf-determinationDefining, naming and speaking for yourself
3UjimaCollective work and responsibilityBuilding the community and solving its problems together
4UjamaaCooperative economicsBuilding and sharing in community businesses together
5NiaPurposeHaving a purpose: building up the community
6KuumbaCreativityUsing creativity to leave the community better than you found it
7ImaniFaithBelieving in your people, your leaders and the success of the community’s goals

  1. Who created Kwanzaa, and in what year?
  2. How many principles are there, and what are they called together?
  3. What does the word Kuumba mean?

Las PosadasReading and questions
READING 4
of 5
Name Date

Las Posadas is a celebration of nine nights, held from December 16 to 24. It is most closely linked with Mexico, and it is also observed in other countries, including Guatemala, and by many families in the United States. The word posada means lodging, or inn. Each night, people walk in a procession from house to house, remembering Mary and Joseph’s search for a place to stay before the birth of Jesus. Groups often sing along the way, and each night ends with a gathering. Many gatherings include songs, a piñata and food to share. Customs differ from town to town and from family to family.

WhenNine nights, December 16 to 24
WhereMost closely linked with Mexico
Key wordsposada, procession, piñata
Check your reading
  1. How many nights does Las Posadas last?
  2. What does the word posada mean?
  3. What do people remember when they walk in the procession?

Lunar New YearReading and questions
READING 5
of 5
Name Date

Lunar New Year begins on a different date each year because it follows a calendar based on the moon. It always falls in late January or in February, which is later than the other celebrations in this pack. Lunar New Year is celebrated in many places and cultures, including China, Vietnam and Korea. In China, the festival lasts until the Lantern Festival on the 15th day. In many places, a lion dance is part of the festivities, and the dancers are often accompanied by drums, gongs and cymbals. Families have their own traditions, and they celebrate in many different ways.

WhenA new date each year, in late January or February
WhereChina, Vietnam, Korea and many other places
Key wordslunar calendar, lion dance, percussion
Check your reading
  1. Why does the date of Lunar New Year change each year?
  2. In which months does Lunar New Year always fall?
  3. Name one country where Lunar New Year is celebrated.
