How to display

  • Print the posters single-sided on US Letter. Post one to three a week, in order, at eye level.
  • Cut the term cards from pages 30–32 on the dashed lines. Sort them under the six group headings on the term index (page 3).
  • Pin the poster next to its card until the class knows the term.

What is inside

  • 26 posters, one term each (pages 4–29): a definition, a drawing, three facts and an example.
  • 26 word wall cards plus one blank card (pages 30–32).
  • Self-check and rubric (page 33), a 30-point quiz (pages 34–35) and its key (page 36).

Pacing: six short sessions

SessionTermsPages
1Setting up and keeping time: DAW, track, region, loop, BPM and quantize.Posters, pages 4–9
2MIDI and samples: MIDI, velocity, piano roll and sample.Posters, pages 10–13
3Digital audio and levels: waveform, frequency, sample rate, bit depth, headroom and clipping.Posters, pages 14–19
4Shaping sound: EQ, filter, compression and limiter.Posters, pages 20–23
5Space and the mix: reverb, delay, pan, automation, mixer, bounce and export.Posters, pages 24–29
6Review with the word wall and the self-check, then the quiz.Pages 30–32, 33, 34–35

Activities

  • Term of the day. Read the definition aloud, cover the drawing and ask students to sketch it. Then compare.
  • Match the card. Hand out cards. Students find the poster that matches, and say the example in their own words.
  • Check the numbers. Ask for seconds per beat at 90 and 150 BPM, or the output of a 4:1 compressor for a peak 12 dB over the threshold (it comes out 3 dB over).
  • Hear it in your DAW. Use any DAW: students find the control for each term and describe what changes. Posters name no software.
  • Teach a term. Each student teaches one term to a partner. Score it with the rubric (page 33).

Notes

The terms are DAW-neutral. Screens and menus differ between programs, so the drawings show ideas, not a particular window. Numbers on the posters are examples: typical sample rates are 44,100 Hz and 48,000 Hz, and typical bit depths are 16 and 24 bits. The activities support the Creating and Producing processes of the National Core Arts Standards for Music; match them to your own curriculum. No songs, lyrics or copyrighted characters are used.

Created with the help of digital and AI tools. Notation and answer keys are verified before release.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 1: DAW
TERM 1
of 26 · Project
DAW
digital audio workstation
A DAW, short for digital audio workstation, is the program or app you use to record, edit, arrange, mix and export music on a computer or tablet.
The main parts of a DAW window
Main partsTransport, tracks, timeline and mixer
Works withBoth audio and MIDI
ProjectOne file that holds all your tracks
In practiceYou open a DAW, add a drum track and a bass track, and build a song one layer at a time.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 2: Track
TERM 2
of 26 · Project
Track
one lane for one sound
A track is one lane in a project that holds one sound source, such as a vocal, a guitar or a drum pattern, so you can edit and mix it on its own.
Four tracks: two audio and two MIDI
Audio trackHolds recorded sound
MIDI trackHolds notes that play an instrument sound
M and SMute silences a track; solo plays only that track
In practicePut the lead vocal on its own track so you can turn it up without changing the drums.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 3: Region
TERM 3
of 26 · Project
Region
also called a clip
A region, also called a clip, is a block on a track that holds a piece of audio or a group of MIDI notes. You can move, trim, split and copy it along the timeline.
Regions on a timeline of 16 bars
MoveDrag it to a new place
TrimDrag an edge to change its length
SplitCut it into two regions at one point
In practiceCopy a 4-bar drum region three times to fill 16 bars.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 4: Loop
TERM 4
of 26 · Project
Loop
a pattern that repeats
A loop is a short musical pattern made to repeat without a gap. A DAW can also loop a chosen part of the timeline so you hear it again and again while you work.
Two kinds of loop
Loop lengthOften 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars
SeamlessThe end joins the start with no gap
Loop regionYou set its start and end on the timeline
In practiceLoop bars 5 to 8 while you adjust the bass sound.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 5: BPM
TERM 5
of 26 · Time and MIDI
BPM
beats per minute, or tempo
BPM means beats per minute. It is the tempo, or speed, of the music. A higher BPM means the beats come faster, so each beat is shorter.
Seconds per beat at four tempos
Seconds per beat60 divided by the BPM
120 BPM2 beats per second
120 BPM0.5 seconds per beat
In practiceAt 120 BPM a bar of four beats lasts 2 seconds, so an 8-bar loop lasts 16 seconds.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 6: Quantize
TERM 6
of 26 · Time and MIDI
Quantize
snap to the grid
Quantize moves notes or audio hits to the nearest line on a timing grid, such as the nearest eighth note, so the timing becomes exact.
The same eight notes before and after
Grid valueThe note length that notes snap to
StrengthHow far notes move toward the grid
TipFull strength sounds exact but can sound stiff
In practiceQuantize a hi-hat part that was played a little early or late to eighth notes to tighten it.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 7: MIDI
TERM 7
of 26 · Time and MIDI
MIDI
Musical Instrument Digital Interface
MIDI is a way to send performance information, such as which note, how hard and how long, between instruments, devices and software. MIDI is not sound: an instrument sound has to play it.
From a key press to a sound
ValuesMost MIDI values run from 0 to 127
Middle CNote number 60
File sizeSmall, because it stores instructions and not audio
In practicePlay a keyboard, record the MIDI, then change the piano sound to strings without playing it again.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 8: Velocity
TERM 8
of 26 · Time and MIDI
Velocity
how hard a note is played
Velocity is how hard a MIDI note is played, from 1 (softest) to 127 (hardest). It usually controls how loud the note is, and some instrument sounds also get brighter at high velocity.
Four notes with different velocities
Range1 to 127
Low numbersSoft notes
High numbersHard notes
In practiceLower the off-beat hi-hat velocity to 60 and keep the main beats near 100 so the groove feels alive.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 9: Piano roll
TERM 9
of 26 · Time and MIDI
Piano roll
the MIDI note grid
A piano roll is a grid for editing MIDI notes. Up and down is pitch, left to right is time, and each bar is a note whose length shows how long it lasts.
Four notes on a piano roll: C, E, G and C
Up and downPitch: higher notes sit higher
Left to rightTime, in beats
Bar lengthHow long the note lasts
In practiceClick in the grid to place C, E and G one after another, then drag the ends to change how long each note lasts.

Sample
a short recorded sound
A sample is a short recorded sound, such as a drum hit, a voice phrase or a single note, that you load and play again and again. It also has a second meaning, one measurement of a sound wave (see term 13, Sample rate).
One sample played on four beats
ExamplesA drum hit, a vocal phrase, one note
Play it fromPads, a keyboard or a region
Other meaningOne measurement of a wave (term 13)
In practiceLoad one recorded kick drum hit and play it on every beat.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 11: Waveform
TERM 11
of 26 · Digital audio
Waveform
a picture of sound
A waveform is a picture of sound over time. Across is time and up and down is the level. Loud parts look tall, quiet parts look short and silence is a flat line.
A waveform with a quiet start and two loud parts
PeakThe highest point of the wave
Zero lineThe flat line of silence
Use it toFind hits, spot clipping and edit by eye
In practiceZoom in on the waveform to find the exact start of a drum hit before you trim it.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 12: Frequency
TERM 12
of 26 · Digital audio
Frequency
how fast a wave vibrates
Frequency is how fast a sound wave vibrates, counted in hertz (Hz), which means cycles per second. A higher frequency sounds higher in pitch.
Three frequencies drawn over the same 10 milliseconds
UnitHertz (Hz): cycles per second
Human hearingAbout 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz
One octave upDouble the frequency: 440 Hz to 880 Hz
In practiceBass notes sit at low frequencies, often under 250 Hz, and cymbal shimmer sits at high frequencies, often above 5,000 Hz.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 13: Sample rate
TERM 13
of 26 · Digital audio
Sample rate
measurements per second
Sample rate is how many times each second digital audio measures the sound wave. It is counted in hertz (Hz). Common sample rates are 44,100 Hz and 48,000 Hz.
The same wave measured with fewer and more samples
Common rates44,100 Hz and 48,000 Hz
Highest frequency keptHalf the rate: 22,050 Hz or 24,000 Hz
Human hearingAbout 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz
In practiceSet the project to 48,000 Hz before you record, and keep your files at the same rate so nothing has to be converted.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 14: Bit depth
TERM 14
of 26 · Digital audio
Bit depth
detail in each measurement
Bit depth is how many bits are used to store each sample's level. More bits give more possible levels and a lower noise floor. Common bit depths are 16 bits (65,536 levels) and 24 bits (16,777,216 levels).
The same wave stored with 3 bits and with 4 bits
16 bits65,536 levels, about 96 dB of range
24 bits16,777,216 levels, about 144 dB of range
Rule of thumbEach extra bit adds about 6 dB of range
In practiceRecord at 24 bits for extra room above the noise floor, and share the finished song at 16 or 24 bits.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 15: Headroom
TERM 15
of 26 · Levels
Headroom
room left below the maximum
Headroom is the space between your loudest peak and the highest level the system can handle without distortion. Digital meters use dBFS, where 0 dBFS is the maximum, so every level below it is a negative number.
A level meter with the peak at -6 dBFS
Maximum0 dBFS
Peak at -6 dBFS6 dB of headroom
Peak at -18 dBFS18 dB of headroom
In practiceRecord so your loudest peaks stay well below 0 dBFS, for example around -12 dBFS, in case a louder note comes along.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 16: Clipping
TERM 16
of 26 · Levels
Clipping
distortion from too much level
Clipping is the harsh distortion you hear when a signal tries to go above 0 dBFS. The tops of the waveform are cut flat, and the lost detail cannot be restored later.
The same wave with and without clipping
Limit0 dBFS
SoundCrackly, harsh distortion
FixLower the level before it is recorded
In practiceIf the clip light on a track meter turns on, turn the level down and record the part again.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 17: EQ
TERM 17
of 26 · Effects
EQ
equalizer
EQ, short for equalizer, boosts or cuts parts of the frequency range. Low, mid and high bands let you turn down a boomy sound or turn up the clarity of a vocal.
An EQ curve with one cut and one boost
CutTurns a band down (negative dB)
BoostTurns a band up (positive dB)
Typical bandsLow 20-250 Hz, mid 250 Hz-4 kHz, high 4-20 kHz
In practiceCut 4 dB at 150 Hz on a boomy vocal and add 5 dB at 2,000 Hz so the words are clearer.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 18: Filter
TERM 18
of 26 · Effects
Filter
removes low or high sound
A filter removes frequencies below or above a cutoff frequency. A high-pass filter lets the highs pass and removes the lows. A low-pass filter lets the lows pass and removes the highs.
A high-pass and a low-pass filter
CutoffThe frequency where the level has dropped 3 dB
Slope12 dB per octave in these drawings
UsesHigh-pass for rumble, low-pass for hiss
In practicePut a high-pass filter at 100 Hz on a vocal to remove low rumble from the room.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 19: Compression
TERM 19
of 26 · Effects
Compression
evens out loud and quiet
Compression reduces the difference between loud and quiet parts. When the signal rises above the threshold, the compressor turns it down. The ratio sets how much: at 4:1, every 4 dB over the threshold comes out as 1 dB over.
Input level against output level at 4:1
ThresholdThe level where compression starts
Ratio4:1 means 4 dB over the threshold comes out 1 dB over
Makeup gainTurns the whole signal back up
In practiceCompress a lead vocal at 4:1 with a threshold of -20 dBFS so loud words sit closer to quiet ones.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 20: Limiter
TERM 20
of 26 · Effects
Limiter
a ceiling for the level
A limiter is a very strong compressor that stops the level from going above a ceiling you set. It is often the last effect on a finished mix, to keep loud peaks from clipping.
Input level against output level with a -3 dBFS ceiling
CeilingThe level nothing should pass
RatioVery high, like infinity to 1
ExampleCeiling -3 dBFS: no peak goes higher
In practicePut a limiter on the master track with the ceiling at -3 dBFS so no peak in the exported song goes above -3 dBFS.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 21: Reverb
TERM 21
of 26 · Effects
Reverb
the sound of a space
Reverb is the wash of many fast reflections that follow a sound in a room or hall. It adds a feeling of space. The decay time says how long the tail lasts.
A hit followed by two reverb tails
Decay timeHow long the tail lasts
RT60Time for the tail to fall by 60 dB
Wet and dryWet is the effect, dry is the original
In practiceAdd a short reverb to a snare for a roomy sound, or a long one to a pad for a floating feel.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 22: Delay
TERM 22
of 26 · Effects
Delay
echoes at a set time
Delay records a sound and plays it back after a set time, so you hear it repeat. Feedback controls how many repeats there are, and each repeat is quieter than the one before.
One sound and three repeats, 500 ms apart
Delay timeSet in milliseconds or in note values
FeedbackHow much returns to make more repeats
1 beat at 120 BPM500 ms
In practiceSet a delay to one beat at 120 BPM, which is 500 ms, so each echo lands on a beat.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 23: Pan
TERM 23
of 26 · Mixing
Pan
left and right
Pan places a sound between the left and right speakers. Full left sends it only to the left speaker, center sends it equally to both and full right sends it only to the right.
Three tracks at three pan settings
L 100All of the sound on the left
CEqual on both sides
R 100All of the sound on the right
In practicePan two guitars 50% left and 50% right to leave the center open for the vocal.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 24: Automation
TERM 24
of 26 · Mixing
Automation
controls that move by themselves
Automation records or draws changes to a control over time, such as volume, pan or an effect setting, so the DAW moves it for you every time the song plays.
Volume automation over 13 bars
BreakpointA dot that sets a value at one moment
Fade inThe level rises from silence
Fade outThe level falls to silence
In practiceDraw a volume line that fades the intro in over 2 bars and fades the ending out over 2 bars.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 25: Mixer
TERM 25
of 26 · Mixing
Mixer
balance every track
The mixer is where you balance all your tracks. Each track has a channel strip with a fader for volume, a pan control and a meter. A fader at 0 dB leaves the level unchanged, and moving it down turns the track down.
Three channel strips and the master
FaderSets a track's volume in dB
MeterShows the level while it plays
-6 dBAbout half the signal's amplitude
In practiceStart with every fader at the same level, then bring up the drums, the bass and the vocal one at a time.

Music Production VocabularyPoster 26: Bounce and export
TERM 26
of 26 · Mixing
Bounce and export
make the finished file
Bounce, or export, turns your finished project into one audio file that plays anywhere. The DAW plays the whole mix once and writes it to a single file.
Many tracks become one audio file
WAVUncompressed: full quality, large file
MP3Compressed: much smaller, some detail lost
SettingsChoose the sample rate and the bit depth
In practiceExport a 24-bit, 48,000 Hz WAV file as your master copy, and a small MP3 file to share.

QUIZ
30 points
NameDateClass

Part B: Short answer (15 points)

1. At 120 BPM, how long is one beat, and how long is a bar of four beats? (2 points)

2. A compressor has a threshold of -20 dBFS and a ratio of 4:1. A peak of -12 dBFS comes in. How many dB over the threshold is it, and what level comes out? (2 points)

3. Name one common sample rate. Which gives more levels in every sample, 16 bits or 24 bits? (2 points)

4. A recording's loudest peak is at -6 dBFS. How much headroom is that? What is it called when a peak tries to go above 0 dBFS? (2 points)

5. A vocal sounds boomy around 150 Hz. Which EQ band do you cut: low, mid or high? What does a high-pass filter do? (2 points)

6. In your own words, what is the difference between MIDI and audio? (2 points)

7. Explain how reverb and delay are different. (3 points)

Working

  • BPM: seconds per beat = 60 ÷ 120 = 0.5; a bar of 4 beats = 4 × 0.5 = 2 seconds.
  • Compression: -12 - (-20) = 8 dB over the threshold. 8 ÷ 4 = 2 dB over, so the output is -20 + 2 = -18 dBFS.
  • Levels: 216 = 65,536 and 224 = 16,777,216. Headroom = 0 - (-6) = 6 dB.
