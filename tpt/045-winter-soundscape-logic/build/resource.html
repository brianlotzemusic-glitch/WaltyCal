What this is ·

A 4-class-period sound design project for high school music technology. Each student designs a snowstorm soundscape from scratch in Logic Pro: no melody, no drums and no loops, only layers of wind, ice, noise and pads made with Alchemy, Quick Sampler and Retro Synth, shaped with automation of filter cutoff, volume, pan and reverb send, and placed with reverb, EQ and stereo width. The piece is 60 to 90 seconds and follows a hush, build, peak and fade. Students plan it, build and mix it, present it and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look through the scene brief (page 11) and the worked example (pages 9–10), and check the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–27), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

No audio, recordings, loops, lyrics, artists or albums are named or included, and there is no melody to chart. Students make every sound in their soundscapes themselves, from the instruments, samplers and plug-ins in Logic Pro.

OVERVIEW
Sound design project
The project in one line

Winter Soundscape · Sound design · Logic Pro
Design a snowstorm from scratch, with no melody.

What students make ·

One original soundscape, 60 to 90 seconds long, of a snowstorm that builds, peaks and fades. It has at least six layers (at least two built in Alchemy and one from noise), at least four automated parameters (filter cutoff, volume, pan and reverb send), reverb, EQ and stereo width, and a mix that does not clip. Students hand in a bounced .mp3 or .wav and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play it and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches sound design as a way of making music without a melody. Students learn that texture, movement and space, not only notes, hold a listener’s attention, and they practice building layers, drawing automation, and using reverb, EQ and stereo width in Logic Pro.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Design six or more layers of sound for a storm, each with its own job, from noise, pads and short samples
  • Plan a 60 to 90 second storm arc (hush, build, peak, fade) whose bars and seconds add up
  • Automate at least four parameters (filter cutoff, volume, pan, reverb send) so the storm moves and breathes
  • Place every layer in the mix with reverb, EQ and stereo width, and explain each choice

SETUP

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones. Speakers help for the final listen, because a rumble needs good low end.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The scene brief and palette cards, printed (pages 11–13), or shown on the projector.
  • The recipes, automation guide and space page, printed (pages 14–17).
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–27).
  • A microphone is optional: students can record a short sound for Quick Sampler, such as paper or a fingernail on a desk. A MIDI keyboard is optional too.

Before Class 1 ·

Decide which scenes you will offer (page 11).
Open Logic Pro on a student computer: can you add an Alchemy track, hear it through headphones and draw automation (press A)?
Check that the tools on pages 14 and 17 are there (Alchemy, Quick Sampler, Retro Synth, Auto Filter, ChromaVerb or Space Designer, Channel EQ).
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Students build layers that blur together, never build to a peak, click when they automate, or crush the peak. Pairs well with a listening day: play the finished soundscapes without saying the scene and let the class decide where the storm builds, peaks and fades. A storm the class can follow without a tune is a soundscape that works.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: what is this layer’s job? Where does the storm peak? What is moving here?
  • On Class 1, ask students to close their eyes while you describe a storm and raise a hand when it starts to build, peaks and fades. Compare the hands.
  • A student who is stuck on sound design can start from a Library noise or pad preset in Alchemy and change its filter and effects.

Choosing scenes ·

A secular, seasonal theme

The project is about winter weather and place, not a holiday. Nothing is sung, no carol is used and there is no sleigh, no character and no brand. Students who prefer another weather or place (a desert wind, a rainstorm) can use the same plan; approve their scene on Class 1.

Keep the volume moderate

Noise, bass and loud peaks are tiring in headphones. Tell students to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

Winter SoundscapeTeacher notes · the big idea
TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches sound design as a way of making music without a melody. Students learn that texture, movement and space, not only notes, hold a listener’s attention, and they practice building layers, drawing automation, and using reverb, EQ and stereo width in Logic Pro.

Common student struggles ·

  • Every layer sounds like one grey blur: give each layer its own range of pitch (page 17), cut the lows of every layer except the rumble with Channel EQ, and spread the layers with pan.
  • The storm never builds because every layer plays from start to finish: use the layer map (page 19) so layers enter and leave on purpose, and compare it with the worked example (pages 9–10).
  • The automation clicks or zig-zags: use fewer points and smooth curves (page 15), and move the control slowly. Wind rarely jumps from one level to another.
  • The peak is distorted, or the reverb washes everything out: lower the faders so the loudest moment keeps headroom, and use less reverb on the layers at the peak (page 17).

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Offer scene A, build only six layers from the palette cards, automate only filter cutoff and volume first, and add pan and reverb send afterwards. Students can start from a Library preset in Alchemy and change only its filter and effects.

More Advanced: Ask for a second scene change halfway, such as a storm that clears to a still, starry night, with a different set of layers in each half. Students can record their own sounds for Quick Sampler (rubbing paper, a zipper, a fingertip on a desk), automate Alchemy macro controls and plan a stereo width that grows toward the peak.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • A grey blur: the palette cards (pages 12–13) and the frequency map (page 17) give each layer its own range.
  • No build: the timeline planner (page 18), the layer map (page 19) and slide 7 show layers entering and leaving.
  • Clicky automation: the five curve shapes (page 15) and the worked example (page 10).
  • A distorted peak or wash: the space page (page 17) and slide 11.

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 24). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Layers & Sound Design (rubric row 1): open the project and count the tracks. Does each layer have its own job and its own range of pitch, and were at least two built in Alchemy?
  • Automation (rubric row 2): show the automation lanes on each track (press A) and compare them with the automation plan (page 20). Listen for clicks or jumps.
  • Storm Arc & Timing and Space & Mix (rubric rows 3 and 4): check the length is 60 to 90 seconds, that the bars and seconds on the timeline (page 18) add up, that the peak does not clip, and that layers are not covering each other.
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): the scene card, layer map, timeline and automation plan, the questions on the feedback planner and the Reflection page (page 27) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 27): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a Winter Soundscape is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 to 3 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

New project and tracksFile › New…, then add a Software Instrument track with Track › New Tracks… for each layer you play or draw. Name every track so your layer map matches.
Tempo as a rulerType the tempo in the control bar (60 to 120 BPM) and keep 4/4. A soundscape has no beat, but the tempo turns bars into seconds: seconds = bars × 240 ÷ BPM (page 18). The playhead position in the control bar can also be shown as time.
AlchemyClick the instrument slot on a channel strip and choose Alchemy. Browse the Library (Y) for noise, wind or pad sounds as a starting point, then change the source, the filter and the effects (recipes on page 14).
Quick SamplerChoose Quick Sampler as the instrument and record a short sound yourself, or use a short sound you recorded. Shorten it, raise its pitch, add its filter and play it from the keyboard or draw notes in the Piano Roll.
Retro Synth and Step FXRetro Synth makes simple low tones (a sine for the rumble). The Step FX plug-in, if your version has it, can make a pad stutter or sweep its filter in steps. If you are unsure, skip it.
Auto FilterInsert Auto Filter on a track. Choose a low-pass filter and set the cutoff and resonance; its LFO moves the cutoff in time. The same cutoff can also be automated (next row).
AutomationPress A to show automation. In the track header, pick a parameter such as Volume, Pan or a plug-in control, click the line to add a point and drag it. Or choose Touch, Latch or Write, play, and move the control by hand; Read plays it back.
Reverb: ChromaVerb and Space DesignerInsert ChromaVerb or Space Designer on a track, or on an aux channel strip that several tracks send to (the Send knob). Set the size and decay to taste and put more on layers that should sound far away.
Channel EQInsert Channel EQ. Turn on the lowest band as a high-pass filter and set its frequency (page 17); lower or raise a band to cut mud or add air.
Pan and stereo widthEach channel strip has a Pan knob (and can automate it). The Stereo Spread plug-in, if your version has it, widens a sound; keep very low sounds in the centre.
Mix and bouncePress X for the Mixer and keep the loudest moment below the red clipping mark. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

I will make a storm sound with some wind and some bells.

My scene is a mountain hut at night. My soundscape is 60 seconds at 120 BPM, so 30 bars of 2 seconds each. It has four sections: a hush of 4 bars, a build of 10 bars, a peak of 8 bars and a fade of 8 bars. I use eight layers: wind bed, frozen pad, glass chimes, gust swells, ice crackle, low rumble, whiteout hiss and shimmer. 6 · Checking the numbers

One bar of 4/4 at 120 BPM lasts 240 ÷ 120 = 2 seconds, so 30 bars = 60 seconds, inside 60 to 90. The sections are 4 + 10 + 8 + 8 bars = 30. Time at a bar = (bar − 1) × 2, so bar 15 starts at 0:28.

Winter SoundscapeSound palette · 1 of 2
STUDENT
Class 1

Every layer in a storm has a job and a zone, the range of pitch it needs (page 17). These cards describe the sounds in words, not presets: use them as recipes and make them your own. Cards 1 to 4 are the body of the storm. Use at least six cards across the two pages; at least two must be made in Alchemy.

1 · Wind bedZone 200 Hz–4 kHz

Its job. The constant body of the storm and the glue of the mix.

Make it. Alchemy with a noise source (pink or white noise). Low-pass or band-pass filter with the cutoff near 800 Hz and little resonance. A slow LFO on the cutoff, about one cycle every 6 to 10 seconds, so the wind breathes.

Automate it. Open the cutoff from the hush to the peak; close it again in the fade.

2 · Gust swellsZone 300 Hz–3 kHz

Its job. Gusts that come and go, so the wind is never flat.

Make it. The same noise as the wind bed on its own track, or one more Alchemy source. Draw volume and cutoff swells of 2 to 4 seconds, each followed by a fall. A longer, softer attack makes a gust sound like it arrives from far away.

Automate it. Draw a Swell curve (page 15) every few bars; make the gusts bigger toward the peak.

3 · Low rumbleZone 30–120 Hz

Its job. Weight under the storm; you feel it more than you hear it.

Make it. Retro Synth or Alchemy: a sine or the sub oscillator on one low note (about 45 Hz) with a trace of noise and a low-pass filter. A slow volume wobble helps. Keep it in the centre of the stereo picture.

Automate it. Bring it in at the middle of the build; take it out in the fade.

4 · Frozen padZone 120 Hz–3 kHz

Its job. A cold, held tone that gives the storm a pitch to sit on. It is a drone, not a tune.

Make it. Alchemy, two or three oscillators slightly detuned, on one low note and its fifth. Attack 3 s or more, release 6 s or more, a gentle low-pass filter. A slow chorus or Step FX filter movement keeps it alive.

Automate it. Automate reverb send: wetter in the hush and fade, drier at the peak.

Winter SoundscapeSound palette · 2 of 2
STUDENT
Class 1

Cards 5 to 8 are the details: the bright, high sounds that appear at the peak, and the sparse ones that show how quiet the hush is. Some layers should come and go: a layer that plays all the time cannot make the storm build. Tick the cards you will use and write the track name you give each.

5 · Ice crackleZone 3–12 kHz

Its job. Tiny, bright, irregular ticks, like frozen branches and blown snow on a window.

Make it. Quick Sampler with a very short sound (a click or a fingernail on a desk, recorded yourself) played with short notes of different velocities. Raise the pitch, shorten the end, add a high-pass filter. Alchemy’s granular source is another route.

Automate it. Automate pan so the ticks sweep from left to right.

6 · Whiteout hissZone 4–16 kHz

Its job. A wall of bright noise that only appears when the storm is at its worst.

Make it. White noise in Alchemy or Retro Synth through a high-pass filter near 3 kHz. A long attack so it swells in rather than starting.

Automate it. Bring it in at the start of the peak, swelling up over its first bar; remove it all at once at the start of the fade.

7 · Glass chimesZone 1–8 kHz

Its job. A few soft, high sounds that show how quiet the hush is.

Make it. A bell or pluck sound in Alchemy, played by hand in a handful of random notes from one scale, short and soft, with a long reverb. Leave them out at the peak. They are not a melody: no tune, no pattern.

Automate it. Send more to the reverb in the hush and the fade.

8 · ShimmerZone 2–12 kHz

Its job. A glassy halo that makes the peak feel wide and open.

Make it. The pad, or a copy of it, an octave higher, with a long, bright reverb and the high-pass filter at about 1.5 kHz. Play it only through the peak.

Automate it. Raise reverb send and volume together as the peak begins; lower both in the fade.

Winter SoundscapeAlchemy, Quick Sampler and Retro Synth recipes
STUDENT
Class 2

Four recipes, in words. Settings are starting points: change them by ear. The same ideas work in other synths, but the names here are from Logic Pro. Nothing is supplied: you make every sound. Build at least two layers in Alchemy.

Alchemy · The wind bed

Source. Choose a noise sound: a noise waveform from one of Alchemy’s sources, or a Library wind or noise preset to start from.

Filter. Low-pass or band-pass. Cutoff low (around 800 Hz), resonance low, so it sounds like air and not a whistle.

Motion. Add an LFO and route it to the cutoff, very slow (one cycle every 6 to 10 seconds) and shallow, so the wind breathes.

Effects. A little reverb and a gentle high-pass. Leave the volume low; the peak is made by automation, not by turning it up first.

Alchemy · The frozen pad

Source. Two sources of a simple, soft wave, detuned a few cents apart for a slow, cold beating.

Envelope. Attack 3 seconds or more, a long release (6 seconds or more), so notes swell in and melt away.

Filter. A low-pass filter with a gentle slope and the cutoff around 2 kHz, to take the edge off.

Play. Hold one low note and its fifth for the whole piece. No melody, and no change of note, is needed.

Quick Sampler · The ice crackle

Sound. Record a very short sound yourself (a fingernail on a desk, a pen click, crumpled paper). Nothing is supplied with this kit.

Shape. Shorten the end of the sample, raise the pitch, and add a high-pass filter so only the highest part remains.

Play. Play short, soft notes with different velocities and gaps, or draw them with the mouse. Irregular is better than steady.

Space. Pan the track and send it to a reverb. Automate the pan over the build (page 16).

Retro Synth · The low rumble

Source. A sine wave, or the sub oscillator if your version has one, with a trace of noise.

Pitch. One note around 45 Hz. Keep it mono.

Filter. Low-pass with the cutoff low; a slow tremolo or volume wobble is enough movement.

Mix. Keep it in the centre, and do not boost it: it takes up a great deal of room. Check it on small speakers too.

Three habits of a good sound designer

Slow: real wind moves slowly, so use slow LFOs and long envelopes. Small: a little modulation is enough. Different: two layers made the same way sound like one. Change the source, the filter or the speed.

Winter SoundscapeAutomation guide · 1 of 2
STUDENT
Class 3

Automation is a curve that moves a control over time. It is how a storm builds: the wind’s cutoff opens, a gust swells, the ice moves from left to right, and the reverb falls away at the peak. Everything you can turn in Logic can be automated.

How to draw it in Logic
  1. Press A to show automation on the track.
  2. In the track header, choose a parameter: Volume, Pan, a plug-in control such as a filter cutoff, or a send.
  3. Click the line to add a point; drag it up or down, or left or right. Use few points.
  4. Press play and listen. Move the points until the change is smooth.
Or record it by hand
Readplays the automation you drew back
Touchrecords while you hold a control, then returns to the drawn line
Latchrecords while you hold a control and stays at the last value
Writerecords from the start of playback, over everything
Try it: draw a gust (a Swell over 2 bars) and a build (a Ramp over 10 bars)
Rules for smooth automation
  • Fewer points make smoother curves. A gust needs three, not thirty.
  • Move slowly: 2 to 4 seconds for a gust, 20 seconds or more for a build.
  • Never jump a volume or a cutoff in one step unless you want a click.
Tempo turns bars into seconds

At 120 BPM one bar lasts 240 ÷ 120 = 2 seconds. A gust of 2 bars is 4 seconds; a build of 10 bars is 20 seconds. Use the timeline (page 18) to plan where each curve starts and ends.

Turn on the lowest band of Channel EQ as a high-pass filter and set its frequency. Everything below it is cut.

Reverb · distance

ChromaVerb or Space Designer on an aux that layers send to. More send, a bigger size and a longer decay mean farther away; less send means close and dry. Use more in the hush and fade and less at the peak.

Pan and stereo width

Pan puts a sound on one side; the Stereo Spread plug-in, if you have it, widens a layer. Pan the ice, widen the pad and shimmer, and keep the rumble in the centre.

Auto Filter · movement

A low-pass cutoff that opens makes a sound seem to come closer. Use its LFO for slow breathing, and automate the cutoff for the big build.

Winter Soundscape

Music Technology | Sound Design Project | 4 class periods

Overview ·

Design a snowstorm from scratch. You are the sound designer for a scene, and you have 60 to 90 seconds to make a storm that arrives, builds, peaks and fades away, using no melody at all. In Logic Pro you will build six or more layers (wind, ice, rumble, pads, noise textures) with Alchemy, Quick Sampler and Retro Synth, then shape them with automation of filter cutoff, volume, pan and reverb send, and place them with reverb, EQ and stereo width.

Goals ·

  • Design six or more layers of sound for a storm, each with its own job, from noise, pads and short samples
  • Plan a 60 to 90 second storm arc (hush, build, peak, fade) whose bars and seconds add up
  • Automate at least four parameters (filter cutoff, volume, pan, reverb send) so the storm moves and breathes
  • Place every layer in the mix with reverb, EQ and stereo width, and explain each choice

Key Vocabulary ·

SoundscapeA piece of sound design that paints a place or scene from layers of texture, with no tune required
LayerOne sound in a stack, such as wind or ice, with its own job, range of pitch and place in the mix
AutomationA drawn or recorded curve that changes a control, such as volume, pan or filter cutoff, over time
Filter cutoffWhere a filter starts to cut: a lower cutoff makes a sound darker and muffled, a higher one opens it up
ReverbThe sound of a space: fast echoes that blend into a tail; more reverb makes a sound seem farther away
Stereo widthHow far a sound spreads between the speakers; pan places it, width widens or narrows it

Tips for Success ·

  • A storm is not loud all the way through. Start quiet so the peak feels huge.
  • Give every layer its own range of pitch: low rumble, mid wind, high ice. Layers that overlap hide each other.
  • Add one layer at a time and listen before you add the next. Move the controls slowly: real wind rarely jumps.
  • Save a new version of your project before each big experiment, so you can always go back.
