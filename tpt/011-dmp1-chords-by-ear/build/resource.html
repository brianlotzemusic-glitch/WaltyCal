What this is ·

A complete 5-day chords unit for high school music technology, taught ear-first. On Day 1 students only listen and play: seven chords, two four-chord loops and a real song, described in their own words. Day 2 gives them the keyboard and the C major and A minor scales. Days 3 and 4 build chords from formulas and name them with Roman numerals (the KCSRN worksheet and a Reference Card). Then they build four chords over a bassline in Logic Pro and present on Day 5.

This is Unit 3 of my Digital Music Production 1 course. All students work in C major or A minor, white keys only. No music reading is needed. Unit 4, Bass & Melody Writing, is the next unit and a separate resource.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the overview and pacing (pages 3–6) and the teacher notes (pages 10–13).
  2. Make the Part 3 Logic file: a 4-measure bassline (10 minutes, recipe on page 4).
  3. Cue up Twist and Shout and Let It Be (The Beatles) on YouTube or Spotify.
  4. Practice the opening activity once (page 10).
  5. Print the student pages (pages 14–31): the packet, KCSRN and cards as one set per student.

Chords by EarUnit overview
OVERVIEW
DMP1 · Unit 3

What students make ·

A Chord Feel Log in their own words, the Scary Stuff packet (keyboard, scales, chords, Roman numerals), the KCSRN worksheet and a Reference Card for their key. Then a short Logic project: four chords built over a provided bassline, one per measure, each labeled with its Roman numeral, plus a chord function table that says what each chord feels like.

What this unit is really about ·

What a chord sounds like and feels like. The I chord feels like home. The V chord feels like homesick. The vi chord feels like something tender or melancholy. Major sounds bright; minor sounds dark. Chords are just notes from the scale stacked together. Roman numerals are names for feelings, not abstract theory.

Feel it before you name it: students play I–IV–V–I and describe how it feels before they know what those symbols mean. The KCSRN worksheet then arrives as “now name what you already heard,” not as a cold fill-in exercise.

Mixing concept: balance ·

When you layer parts, the background has to step back. When chords and melody are the same volume, the melody disappears. Turning the chords down is not a mixing trick; it is a musical decision about what the listener should focus on.

Goals ·

  • Describe what a chord feels like in plain language before naming it
  • Name every white key and tell a half step from a whole step
  • Build the C major and A natural minor scales from their formulas
  • Build major, minor and diminished chords with R-4-3, R-3-4 and R-3-3
  • Use Roman numerals to describe chord function in C major or A minor
  • Build and label four chords over a bassline in Logic

Before Day 1 ·

Open Logic with a piano sound and test the class speakers.
Practice the opening activity (page 10) and cue up the songs.

Make the Part 3 Logic file (10 minutes)

I give students a Logic file with the bassline already written. Logic projects are not included here, so build your own once. My recommended setup:

  • Same key as the scales work (C major or A minor)
  • Bassline clearly following chord roots so students can infer the progression from the bass
  • Arrangement markers with chord labels visible
  • No chord track — leave that blank for students to build

Recipe:

  1. New project, one Software Instrument track with a bass sound (Library: Bass). Tempo between 80 and 100 BPM is comfortable.
  2. Draw a 4-measure MIDI region. In the piano roll, add one whole note per measure, the root of each chord. C major example: C – A – F – G (low octave). A minor example: A – F – C – G.
  3. Turn on Cycle over the 4 measures so it loops.
  4. Optional, as in my file: arrangement markers with chord labels visible.
  5. Leave the chord track empty: students build it. Save it as a template or share the project with your class.

The examples use the same four chords in both keys: I–vi–IV–V in C major and i–VI–III–VII in A minor (worked example on page 34). Any four chords from the Reference Card work.

What comes next

Unit 4, Bass & Melody Writing, picks up here: the rule behind every bassline, and a short melody over the chords students just built.

Everything students need in Logic for this unit, in one table. On Day 3 demonstrate one chord (slide 15), then let students build the rest.

New instrument trackTrack › New Software Instrument Track (Option-Command-S). Open the Library (Y) and pick a piano or electric piano for chords, a bass sound for the bassline.
Play without a keyboardWindow › Show Musical Typing (Command-K) turns the computer keys into a piano. Z and X move the octave.
Empty MIDI regionWith the Pencil tool, click in the track’s lane, or Control-click the lane and choose Create MIDI Region. Drag its right edge to make it one measure long.
Piano rollDouble-click the region, or press P. With the Pencil tool, click to add a note; drag its right edge to set the length.
Stack a chordAll three notes start at the same position and last the same length. Select the notes and press Q to quantize them to the grid.
Copy a chordOption-drag a region to copy it; then drag its notes up or down in the piano roll to make the next chord.
Name a regionSelect it and type the Roman numeral in the Region inspector’s name field (or press Shift-N).
Loop itPress C for Cycle and drag the cycle area over the four measures.
BalanceOpen the Mixer (X). Pull the chord track’s fader down until the bass is clear. Solo (S) a track to hear it alone.
ExportFile › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx project and choose Compress.
Chord check in the piano roll

Three notes stacked in one column, same start, same length. Count the rows between them: 4 then 3 half steps is major, 3 then 4 is minor, 3 then 3 is diminished. Every grey row (a black key) counts.

Feel it before you name it ·

This is the single most important fix in my course sequence: theory used to arrive as a cold worksheet. Taught ear-first, every later project has real harmonic understanding behind it. The most common failure mode is rushing to the worksheet. Spend the full first day just playing chords and describing feelings in plain language. Those descriptions are music theory; the Roman numerals just give them shorter names.

Day 1: the opening activity (first 10 minutes) ·

Ten minutes of playing notes and chords before any theory framing plants an emotional reference point that the packet then labels. Do not skip it, even if it feels like lost time.

  1. 1
    Open Logic. Load a piano sound. Make sure students can hear.
  2. 2
    Play a single C. Ask: what does that feel like? (Empty. Waiting.) Good.
  3. 3
    Play C major (C-E-G). Ask: what changed? Let them answer freely. Happy, full, finished.
  4. 4
    Play A minor (A-C-E). Ask: what is different? Dark, heavy, sad.
  5. 5
    Play I-IV-V-I slowly. Ask: what happened at the end? Most will say it resolved. Why?
  6. 6
    Tell them: the Scary Stuff packet gives names to everything you just felt. That is all music theory is.

Roman numerals: the key framing ·

Part 3 has two distinct moments. The first is mechanical: students learn to build chords from formulas and enter them correctly in the piano roll. The second is conceptual: Roman numerals are introduced as a way to describe chord function rather than just chord names. The second moment is more important and should not be rushed.

A chord’s letter name tells you what notes it contains. Its Roman numeral tells you how it functions: what emotional role it plays in the key. The I chord always feels like home regardless of whether the key is C major or G major or Bb major. That principle transfers to every key students will ever work in.

On Day 4, play I–IV–V–I together: home, away, very away, home. Play i–VI–III–VII: how is that different? Name the feelings before naming the numerals. Then bring back the class’s words from Day 1: the KCSRN worksheet is “now name what you already heard.”

The Reference Card is a living document ·

Students will use it again in the next unit, in the EDM project and beyond. Make clear that filling it in carefully now is an investment. The completed C major and A minor cards (pages 23–24) are for checking; the blank card (page 25) is the one students fill in.

About the Logic file ·

The Part 3 file contains a bassline already written; students add only the chord layer. The bass follows the chord roots so students can infer the progression from the bass. Recipe on page 4.

Mixing concept: balance (Day 5) ·

Solo the chords, solo the bass, then play both. Ask which one the listener should follow. Students pull the chord fader down until the bass is clear. It is not graded; it is the habit the next unit needs when the melody arrives.

3 of 4

Common student struggles ·

Rushing to the worksheet: The most common failure mode in this unit. Spend the full first day just playing chords and describing feelings in plain language. ‘That one sounds like something good is about to happen.’ ‘That one sounds unfinished.’ Those descriptions are music theory. The Roman numerals just give them shorter names.

Finding C on the MPK Mini: The MPK Mini keys are small and not labeled. Show students the pattern: C always sits just to the left of the group of 2 black keys. Have every student find C before they do anything else.

Counting half steps from the root incorrectly: The single most common chord construction error. Students count the root as step 1. Demonstrate explicitly: for C major (R-4-3), start from C# as step 1 — C#(1), D(2), D#(3), E(4). Then count 3 from E: F(1), F#(2), G(3). Walk around and ask every student to verbally count their first chord before they enter it in Logic.

Chords entered as melody notes: Students sometimes enter chord notes one at a time horizontally instead of stacked vertically. Show the piano roll with a correct chord: all three notes start at the same position and have the same length. Any chord that looks like a melody line is wrong.

Roman numerals feeling abstract: Students who built their chords mechanically and did not play them will struggle to connect Roman numerals to feelings. Return to the opening activity: play I and V on the class speaker and ask what the difference feels like. Then explain: I is labeled I because it is the home chord, built on the first note of the scale. That is all the Roman numeral tells you.

Rushing through the packet: The half/whole step exercises are not optional. They build the mechanical understanding that makes chord construction possible. Check completion before students open Logic.

Suggested variations ·

More accessible: Pre-fill the chord spellings in the Scary Stuff packet for the key students are working in. Ask them to verify each chord in Logic rather than calculate it from scratch.

More advanced: Ask students to add a second chord layer with a different instrument sound and slightly varied rhythm. Introduce the concept of harmonic rhythm (how often the chords change) and ask students to experiment with half-measure chord changes.

Grading tips ·

  • Three categories, each scored 2–5, for a total out of 15 (page 31).
  • Chord Construction: open the piano roll and count. The answer keys (pages 32–34) list every chord in both keys.
  • Harmonic Understanding: the chord function table. ‘Sounds good’ is a 3; ‘wants to go back home’ is the kind of specific answer the 5 asks for.
  • The quickest evidence: point to your I chord. Play it. Does it feel like home? That is the test.

Roman Numerals — Names for Chord Function ·

Every chord in a key has a Roman numeral based on its position in the scale. The Roman numeral tells you something the letter name cannot — it tells you how the chord functions. The I chord always feels like home. The V chord always feels like it needs to resolve. These feelings are the same in every key.

Uppercase = major chord. Lowercase = minor chord. ° = diminished. In the last column, write the word the class used for that feeling on Day 1.
