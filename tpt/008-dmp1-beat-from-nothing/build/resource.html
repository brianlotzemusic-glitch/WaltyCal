What this is ·

A complete 4-day drum-programming unit for high school music technology. Students build an original drum beat from nothing in Logic Pro’s Drum Machine Designer: no loops, no pre-made grooves. They name the kit, hear how loops work, copy one famous pattern, then build and mix their own 16-measure beat in AABA form and present it.

This is the first unit of my Digital Music Production 1 course, the one I teach every September. It works just as well on its own.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the overview and pacing (pages 3–5) and the teacher notes (pages 8–9).
  2. Check the setup list (page 4) on one student computer.
  3. Program the three listening examples into Logic from the key (page 19): about 10 minutes.
  4. Open the intro deck and cue up Billie Jean (Michael Jackson) on YouTube or Spotify. No audio is included.
  5. Print or post the student pages (pages 10–18).

The intro deck ·

You present it: slides 1–8 on Day 1 (hook, what a loop is, beats and measures, the kit, the grid) and slides 9–15 to launch the project on Day 2 (worked example, Logic steps, the project, deliverables, rubric). Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint shows a different font, install them or use the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

Beat From NothingUnit overview
OVERVIEW
DMP1 · Unit 1

What students make ·

An original drum beat built entirely from scratch in Drum Machine Designer: no loops, no pre-made grooves. Students copy one famous pattern, then build an original one inspired by it: 16 measures in AABA form, mixed with volume and panning.

What students learn ·

Kick, snare, and hi-hat by name, sound, and function. Subdivision: eighth notes, sixteenth notes, and why they feel different. Why repetition is a musical tool, not laziness. The satisfaction of making something from nothing.

Why it comes first ·

This is the hook. But instead of ‘drag a loop and feel like a producer,’ the hook is authorship: ‘I made that sound myself and I understand why it works.’ Students who build a beat understand what is inside every loop they will meet for the rest of the year. Constraints force musical decisions.

Mixing concept: volume & panning ·

The first mixing lesson is simple: not everything should be the same volume. The kick should feel grounded, the hi-hat should sit above it, the snare should cut through. Turning something down to make something else feel louder is the most transferable mixing lesson there is.

Goals ·

  • Identify the kick, snare, and hi-hat by name, sound, and function
  • Build an original drum beat using Logic Pro’s Drum Machine Designer
  • Understand how volume and panning affect the feel of a mix
  • Experience the creative process of building something musical from scratch

At a glance ·

Grades9–12, beginners (no music reading needed)
Length4 class periods of about 53 minutes: 2 learning, 1 work, 1 presenting
TypeStructured project
SoftwareLogic Pro: Drum Machine Designer, pattern regions, Step Sequencer (16 and 64 steps), volume and pan
Product16-measure AABA drum beat, MP3 + compressed project, presented with three feedback questions
Assessment5-category rubric, /25 (page 18)

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and organizing musical ideas (MU:Cr1, MU:Cr2), refining work through feedback (MU:Cr3), presenting (MU:Pr6) and analyzing what is heard (MU:Re7).

A note from me

Some students will be frustrated that they can’t just drag a loop. Hold the line. By the end of this unit they will feel more pride in four bars they built than in four minutes they assembled. That pride is the hook, and it lasts longer.

  • The free Learning Music beat-making lessons at learningmusic.ableton.com for students who want more.

Before Day 1 ·

Open Logic on one student computer and make a pattern region (checks the version and the kits).

Logic quick reference ·

New drum trackCreate a Software Instrument track and choose any kit under Electronic Drum Kit in the Library (Y). This loads Drum Machine Designer.
Pattern regionControl-click (right-click) in the track at bar 1 and choose Create Pattern Region. Double-click it to open the Step Sequencer.
Place hitsClick squares to turn hits on. Each row is one sound; add rows for more sounds. Pattern length goes up to 64 steps.
CyclePress C, then set the cycle area over bar 1 so it repeats while you work.
TempoDouble-click the tempo in the control bar and type the BPM.
TracksDuplicate a track with Track › New Track With Duplicate Setting (Command-D). Change a sound from the Library.
RepeatOption-drag a region to copy it, or select it and press Command-R to repeat it.
Volume and panOpen the Mixer (X). Click the disclosure triangle on the Drum Machine Designer track to show each drum’s own fader and pan knob.
ExportFile › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx project and choose Compress.

Menu names are from current Logic Pro versions; older versions may differ slightly. Logic Pro is a trademark of Apple Inc.; it is named here only to describe what the unit needs. This resource is not affiliated with Apple.

Beat From NothingTeacher notes · what to expect
TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this unit reinforces ·

This is the entry point for the course. It sets the tone by establishing authorship over assembly as the hook: students who build a beat from scratch understand what is inside every loop they will encounter for the rest of the year. The first two days are deliberately slow: students name the kit, learn what a loop actually is, and count beats and measures by ear before they are asked to build anything. The AABA form requirement plants the first formal seed: they meet the term on the project sheet and hear it in their own work before they can define it. The mixing concept (volume and panning) is introduced here in the simplest possible context: three sounds competing for space is the most accessible mixing problem you can give a beginner.

The Famous Drum Patterns activity on Day 2 is critical. Copying Billie Jean is not busywork: it trains the ear to recognize what ‘correct’ feels like at 116 BPM with a specific kick-snare-hi-hat relationship. Students who complete it properly have a reference point for everything they build afterward.

Common student struggles ·

The blank page problem: Some students will stare at the step sequencer and not know where to start. The Day 2 sequence solves this: let them mess around in a pattern region with no requirements first, then copy a famous pattern, then delete it and build something new. Having done it twice removes the paralysis. Do not skip the copying step even if students are impatient to build their own.

Over-complicating the B section: Students tend to want the B section to be completely different; they start over and lose their groove. Redirect them: remove one element, add one new sound, or change the hi-hat rhythm. Less is almost always more. The best B sections are 80% the same as A.

Volume at maximum: Students default to pushing every fader to the top. Address this on Day 3 with a direct demonstration: play a mix with everything at max, then pull individual elements down and let students hear the difference. The cardinal rule (turn other things down rather than turning one thing up) should be stated explicitly and repeated.

Random hi-hat placement: Students who do not yet understand subdivision will scatter hi-hats without a pattern. Spend 5 minutes at the Day 2 launch explaining the grid: 16 squares = 16 sixteenth notes. Every other square = eighth notes (the default groove feel). Every square = sixteenth notes (busier, more energy).

Opening the piano roll instead of creating a pattern region: This is the most common technical error on Day 2. Watch for students who double-click on a blank space and open an empty piano roll. The correct workflow is right-click (or Control-click), then Create Pattern Region.

TEACHER NOTES

Suggested variations ·

More accessible: Remove the AABA structure requirement. Have students focus on building one strong 4-measure A section they are proud of. Introduce AABA in the presentation discussion as a concept to explore next time, not a requirement they failed.

More advanced: Require the B section to be built in 64-step mode (rather than 16). Challenge these students to use note repeat on the hi-hat to create a triplet or 32nd-note accent. Ask them to include in their feedback questions: ‘What specific choice makes my B section different, and why does it work?’

Grading tips ·

  • Each category is scored 2–5, for a total out of 25. Students self-score first; you score in the second column (page 18).
  • Listen once for groove (Composition & Structure), once for contrast between A and B (Creativity), and once with the mixer open (Volume & Balance).
  • Technical Skill: open the project. Pattern regions on a clean grid, or 64-step / note repeat used with control, earn the 5.

Resources ·

  • The intro deck (.pptx and PDF), included: Day 1 loops lesson feat. Billie Jean, and the Day 2 launch.
  • A short “how to identify parts of a drum set” video, and a Logic Step Sequencer tutorial (YouTube; choose ones you like).
  • learningmusic.ableton.com/make-beats: a useful conceptual supplement for advanced students.

Project sheet

Digital Music Production 1 · Unit 1 · Structured Project · Drum Machine Designer

Overview ·

You’re going to build an original drum beat from nothing: no loops, no pre-made grooves. Starting with the three building blocks of a drum kit (kick, snare, hi-hat), you’ll explore rhythm, pattern, and what makes a beat feel right. You’ll also analyze a famous drum beat to understand how real producers think about groove. By the end, you’ll have made something entirely your own, and you’ll understand exactly why it works.

Goals ·

  • Identify the kick, snare, and hi-hat by name, sound, and function
  • Build an original drum beat using Logic Pro’s Drum Machine Designer
  • Understand how volume and panning affect the feel of a mix
  • Experience the creative process of building something musical from scratch

Requirements ·

Built entirely in Drum Machine Designer: no Apple Loops
Must include: kick drum, snare drum, closed hi-hat, and at least one additional element (open hi-hat, clap, rim, shaker, etc.)
Minimum 16 measures, structured in AABA form (A = main beat, B = variation, A = main beat returns)
Volume adjusted thoughtfully on all tracks: nothing at default
Panning applied intentionally to at least 2 tracks
Project named correctly: Your Name – Beat From Nothing
Exported as MP3 and .zip (compressed .logicx file) and submitted to your class page before presenting
Your beat’s shape

4 measures each · 16 measures · B = A with one change

Key vocabulary

14 terms on page 16: kick, snare, hi-hat, loop, beat, measure, step sequencer, subdivision, tempo, pattern region, cycle, AABA, volume, panning.

STUDENT
Project · 2 of 2

Steps / process ·

  1. 1
    Day 1 · Watch & LabelWatch a short parts-of-the-drum-set video. Label the Parts of the Drum Set worksheet using the word bank. Do not move on until you can name each piece of the kit without looking.
  2. 2
    Day 1 · Loops & ListeningLearn what a loop is using Billie Jean, then follow along with the Drumbeat Listening Examples as a class. Notice where the kick lands, where the snare hits, what the hi-hat is doing. Count. Listen. Discuss.
  3. 3
    Day 2 · Copy a Famous PatternOpen the Famous Drum Patterns page. Choose ONE pattern and recreate it exactly in Drum Machine Designer; tempo must match. Call over your teacher when you’re done.
  4. 4
    Day 3 · Build Your A SectionDelete the famous pattern. Now build your own. Start with kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, hi-hat in eighth notes. Then make it yours: adjust, add, subtract until it feels right. This is your A section.
  5. 5
    Day 3 · Build Your B SectionCreate a variation on your A section. You don’t need to start over: try removing one element, adding one new sound, or changing the hi-hat rhythm. It should feel related to A but noticeably different.
  6. 6
    Day 3 · Structure in AABAArrange your pattern regions in AABA form: A section, A section, B section, A section. Each section should be 4 measures long for a 16-measure total.
  7. 7
    Day 3 · MixAdjust the volume of each track so every element has its own space in the mix. Pan at least two elements slightly left or right. Ask yourself: can I clearly hear each drum sound?
  8. 8
    Day 4 · PresentPost three feedback questions to the class feedback board before class begins. Share your beat with the class. Respond to at least two of your classmates’ projects with genuine, specific feedback.

Tips for success ·

  • Start simple. A groove that locks in is more powerful than a complicated pattern that doesn’t feel right.
  • Listen while you build. If you wouldn’t nod your head to it, it isn’t there yet.
  • Your B section doesn’t need to be completely different: one small change is often enough to give the listener something new.
  • Turn things down before you turn things up. Your mix will almost always sound better when every element has space.
  • The hi-hat is what makes a beat breathe. Try eighth notes vs. sixteenth notes and hear how the energy changes.
  • When you copy the famous pattern, count carefully. Accuracy here trains your ear to recognize what ‘correct’ feels like.

EXAMPLE
Teacher

Use this to model the build on Days 2–3 or as a reference while you circulate. It is one possible answer, not the answer: every student’s beat should sound different.

1 · Starting point

Kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, hi-hat in eighths. Every hit lands on the grid, so it locks in. Now change one thing at a time and listen.

2 · My A section

Extra kick on the “and” of 2 (2.3); clap with the snare on 4; open hat on 4.3, where the closed hat drops out.

3 · My B section (one change)

Same beat, closed hi-hat in sixteenths: more energy, still clearly related to A.

4 · Arrange

4 measures each · 16 measures · B = A with one change

5 · A sample mix
TrackVolumePanWhy
Kick0 dB (loudest)CenterThe anchor: everything else sits around it.
Snareabout −3 dBCenterCuts through on 2 and 4.
Clapabout −6 dBSlightly left (L10)Doubles the snare on beat 4; tucked under it.
Closed hi-hatabout −9 dBSlightly right (R15)Sits above the kick; never louder than the snare.
Open hi-hatabout −11 dBSlightly left (L15)A quick lift into the next bar.

Numbers are starting points for a demo, not targets. Trust your ears: if you can’t hear a sound clearly, turn something else down first.

What a 5 sounds like

A groove you nod your head to within a bar, a B section you notice without being told, every drum sound clear, width from panning that doesn’t distract, and a student who can say why they made each choice.
