Teacher Notes

February Music Sub Plans K–5 · 8 no-prep, no-tech days · Black History Month musicians, Valentine’s Day, Lunar New Year

Why February matters

February is a short month with a lot to remember. Every February the United States observes Black History Month, which grew from Negro History Week, started by historian Carter G. Woodson in February 1926. This bundle shares short facts about five musicians: Scott Joplin, Duke Ellington, Ella Fitzgerald, Louis Armstrong and Florence Price. February also brings Valentine’s Day on February 14, which the bundle uses to talk about the heartbeat as a steady beat; Lunar New Year, a celebration based on the Moon’s calendar that falls between January 21 and February 20, with the drums, cymbals and gong of the lion dance; and Groundhog Day on February 2. The music stays simple: clapping, patting and saying words. Nothing asks a student to take part in a religious practice.

What it is

Eight complete music lessons a substitute can teach with no tech, no instruments and no music background. Each day has a step-by-step plan with words to say and timed steps, and two worksheets: one for grades K–2 and one for grades 3–5.

The eight days

  1. Musician Spotlight Reader: reading about February musicians.
  2. Name Beats: speech rhythm: clap the syllables.
  3. Heart Drum Design Lab: design a heart drum.
  4. Heartbeat Freeze: move to a tapped beat and freeze.
  5. Lantern Word Puzzles: February vocabulary puzzles.
  6. Music Job Spotlight: music jobs.
  7. Lantern Chant Book: compose a four-beat chant.
  8. February Bingo Showdown: review game day.

Use them in any order. The welcome sheet has a “Day #” column so the sub knows which day each class gets next.

Set up the sub tub (about 15 minutes, once)

  1. Fill in the welcome sheet (page 3).
  2. Print the plans, the quick start, the word-rhythm chart, the facts page and the sub report; copy each worksheet for your largest class.
  3. Cut out the folder labels (page 45), put each day in its own folder, and add the answer keys.

Why subs can teach it

Rhythm work is speech rhythm: clap the syllables of words, with the claps printed as dots. Nobody has to read music or make music with an instrument. Games use only voices, hands and a pencil.

Standards

Supports relating music to varied contexts (MU:Cn11.0) and generating musical ideas (MU:Cr1.1), National Core Arts Standards, Music, grades K–5.

Kind Word RoundEach student says one kind word, like “friend,” while the class pats a steady beat.any
Mirror MovesPairs face each other. One moves slowly, the other copies like a mirror; then swap.4
Tips for a calm class ·
  • Use the teacher’s attention signal from the welcome sheet (page 3), or clap two times and have the class clap back two.
  • “Strong voice, not shouting” keeps every game safe and happy.
  • If a game gets too loud, switch to a worksheet or to Quiet Countdown.
  • Let students take turns as the leader. Keep turns short.
  • Students may know other musicians or celebrate other traditions. Thank them and move on.
Not sure which game to pick? Use Quiet Countdown to settle a class, Name Clap to wake it up, and Kind Word Round to finish with something positive.

Plain facts for answering questions. Students may know more, and may celebrate other traditions. Thank them for sharing.
TopicWhenA few facts
Black History MonthEvery FebruaryObserved in the United States each February. It grew from Negro History Week, which historian Carter G. Woodson started in February 1926. In 1976 President Gerald Ford asked Americans to honor it.
Scott JoplinAbout 1868–1917Composer and pianist, called the “King of Ragtime.” His Maple Leaf Rag was published in 1899. Sources differ on his exact birth date, so the pages say “about 1868.”
Duke EllingtonApril 29, 1899 – May 24, 1974Born in Washington, D.C. Pianist, composer and bandleader who led his own orchestra from 1924 until his death, about fifty years.
Ella FitzgeraldApril 25, 1917 – June 15, 1996Jazz singer famous for scat, using the voice like an instrument. Her nickname was “Lady Ella.”
Louis Armstrong1901 – July 6, 1971Born in New Orleans, Louisiana. Jazz trumpeter and singer, nicknamed “Satchmo.” He often said he was born in 1900, but records point to 1901.
Florence Price1887 – June 3, 1953Born in Little Rock, Arkansas (a few sources say 1888). Composer, pianist and organist. In 1933 the Chicago Symphony Orchestra performed her Symphony No. 1. She is known as the first African American woman to have a symphony performed by a major U.S. orchestra.
Valentine’s DayFebruary 14A day when many people share cards and kind words. In this bundle, the heart is a steady beat: it speeds up when you run and slows down when you rest.
Lunar New YearBetween January 21 and February 20A celebration based on the Moon’s calendar, so its date changes every year. Many families in China, other Asian countries and communities around the world celebrate it. Lanterns are a common decoration, and the lion dance uses drums, cymbals and a gong.
Groundhog DayFebruary 2A tradition in which people watch to see whether a groundhog sees its shadow. The best-known event is in Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, where official celebrations date back to 1887.
Presidents’ DayThird Monday of FebruaryThe federal holiday officially named Washington’s Birthday, often called Presidents’ Day. It always falls between February 15 and 21.
If a student asks something you do not know: say “That is a good question. Let’s ask your classroom teacher.” You never need to explain more than what is on the page. The days share these facts and never ask students to take part in a religious practice.

Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Welcome and echo
    Say: “Good morning, musicians! I am your guest teacher today. Echo me!” Say each line and let the class repeat it: Ears are on. Eyes are up. Hands are still. Let’s start! Then say it once in a whisper.
  2. 5 minStep 2

    Read aloud

Every February, the United States observes Black History Month. It grew from Negro History Week, which historian Carter G. Woodson started in February 1926. Music is one way to meet people who shaped American culture. Scott Joplin, a composer and pianist, became known as the King of Ragtime, and his Maple Leaf Rag was published in 1899. Duke Ellington was born in Washington, D.C., in 1899. He was a pianist, composer and bandleader who led his own orchestra from 1924 until 1974. Name BeatsDay 2 · Speech rhythm: clap the syllables · no tech, no prep
DAY 2
Sub plan
GoalStudents clap the syllables of musicians’ names and February words and use them to build a four-beat chant.
You needPencils, crayons, the worksheets and the word-rhythm chart (page 7). No music reading needed: every word shows its claps.
CopiesK–2: page 14
3–5: page 15
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Steady pat
    Pat your legs slowly and evenly, like a heartbeat. Say: “Copy me!” Students pat with you. Say: “This steady pulse is called the beat.”
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Clap the words
    Say a word, then say it again while clapping once for each syllable: Price (1 clap), Jop-lin (2 claps), El-ling-ton (3 claps). Students echo. Try five or six words from page 7.
  3. 5 minStep 3
    Chart echoes
    Show page 7. Read a line out loud, one box per beat, while clapping the dots. One dot = ta, two dots in one box = ti-ti, shh = a silent beat (open hands). Students echo. K–2 use lines 1–4; 3–5 use all 8.
  4. 15 minStep 4
    Worksheet
    K–2 page 14, 3–5 page 15. Read Part A aloud and clap each word with the class before they color or write. Answers are on page 41.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Chant parade
    Volunteers read their chant aloud while the class pats a steady beat. Everyone claps at the end.
Extra time? Name Clap from the quick games (page 6), with the names of students. Short class? Do only Part A and Part B.

Heart Drum Design LabDay 3 · Design a heart drum · no tech, no prep
DAY 3
Sub plan
GoalStudents design a decorated drum, label its parts, and connect it to a steady beat.
You needPencils, crayons and the worksheets. Nothing else.
CopiesK–2: page 17
3–5: page 18
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    What is a drum?
    Say: “A drum makes sound when something taps it. Drums are used in many kinds of music, and a big drum helps keep the beat in the Lunar New Year lion dance.” Ask: What patterns or colors could decorate a drum? Take three answers.
  2. 5 minStep 2
    Find your heartbeat
    Say: Put a hand on your chest and find your heartbeat. Then say: “Your heart keeps a steady beat all day. Pat the same beat on your leg.” Pat slowly together for ten seconds.
  3. 20 minStep 3
    Design the drum
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 17, 3–5 page 18. Say: “Design a heart drum. Make it bright and clear, and give your drum a name.” Read the directions aloud.
  4. 5 minStep 4
    Gallery walk
    Students stand beside their desks with their drums. Walk the room and ask three students: What is your drum called?
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Put it away
    Collect the sheets for the teacher. Ask: Whose drum would you like to tap?
Extra time? Kind Word Round or Quiet Countdown from the quick games (page 6). Short class? Skip the gallery walk.

Heartbeat FreezeDay 4 · Move to a tapped beat and freeze · no tech, no prep
DAY 4
Sub plan
GoalStudents move to a steady tap, change speed with the tap, and freeze when it stops.
You needPencils, the worksheets, a clear space to move, and the picture cards on page 22 (point at them or cut them out).
CopiesK–2: page 20
3–5: page 21
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Rules of the freeze
    Say: “When I tap, you move. When I stop, you freeze like a statue. Keep your hands to yourself.” Practice once: tap a pencil on a desk for five seconds, stop, and wait for stillness.
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Heartbeat walk
    Say: “Your heartbeat is a steady beat. It is faster when you run and slower when you rest.” Show the heart card on page 22. Tap slowly: students walk slowly. Tap fast: they jog in place. Stop: freeze. Repeat with three different speeds.
  3. 10 minStep 3
    Picture cards
    Hold up (or point at) one card at a time. Students move as the card says while you tap. Use at least six cards. The words under each card tell you what to say.
  4. 10 minStep 4
    Reflection sheet
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 20, 3–5 page 21. Students sit and fill it in. Read the directions aloud.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Statue gallery
    Tap a few beats, stop, and let the class freeze in a favorite pose. Walk around and name two poses you notice.
Extra time? Mirror Moves from the quick games (page 6). Short class? Use only four cards.

Jobs and What They DoRead, match and write
DAY 6
Grades 3–5
NameDate
Every piece of music starts somewhere, and many different jobs help it reach listeners. A composer writes and arranges original music. Scott Joplin and Florence Price were both composers. A conductor, also called a music director, leads an orchestra or another group so that the musicians stay together. Duke Ellington led his own orchestra for about fifty years, so he was a bandleader as well as a composer. A piano tuner adjusts the tension of a piano’s strings so that the instrument sounds in tune. A radio host broadcasts music, news and weather, interviews guests, and often specializes in one kind of music. A music teacher helps students learn how music works and gives them chances to try it. All of these jobs need careful listening, and most of them need teamwork. Imagine that you lead a brand new orchestra. Which job from this list would you most want to add first, and why?
