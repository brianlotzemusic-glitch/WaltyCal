What this is ·

A complete 4-day bass and melody unit for high school music technology. Days 1 and 2: students use a scale to write a short, singable melody over a chord progression and bassline they already have. At the end of Day 2 they look hard at that bassline and discover the rule behind every bassline. Day 3: they choose four chords with Roman numerals and write their own bassline. Day 4: they present both.

This is Unit 4 of my Digital Music Production 1 course. It picks up where Unit 3, Chords by Ear (a separate resource), ends: students continue in their Unit 3 project. Students who did not do Unit 3 use your chords-and-bassline file (recipe on page 4) and the Reference Cards in this kit. C major or A minor, white keys only. No music reading is needed.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the overview and pacing (pages 3–6) and the teacher notes (pages 9–12).
  2. Check who has a Unit 3 project; for everyone else make the chords-and-bassline file once (10 minutes, page 4).
  3. Cue up Seven Nation Army (The White Stripes) and Stand by Me (Ben E. King) on YouTube or Spotify.
  4. Print the student pages (pages 13–26), one set per student.

Every slide has a day: Day 1 (slides 1–10), Day 2 (slides 11–15), Day 3 (slides 16–20) and Day 4 (slides 21–22). The day plans name the slide for each block. Every slide has speaker notes.

Bass & Melody WritingUnit overview
OVERVIEW
DMP1 · Unit 4

What students make ·

A bassline and short melody written over a chord progression in Logic: the foundational skills a full-song or EDM project will require. Two short projects: a 2- or 4-measure melody over the chords and bassline from Unit 3 (or your file), then an original bassline over four chords each student chooses from the Reference Card.

What students learn ·

Basslines are monophonic — one note at a time. The root of each chord is the safest and most powerful bass note. Melody is short, repetitive, and singable. Quantize exists and it is not cheating. The scale gives you a guaranteed set of notes that will sound right. Four methods of writing melody: play in, draw a rhythm first, click one note and move it, use the step sequencer.

Why this unit lands here ·

Unit 3 gave students the harmonic landscape. This unit teaches them how to move through it — one line at a time. Bass anchors the bottom, melody defines the top. These are the two skills the EDM project requires but currently cannot assume. Teaching them here, with no other pressures, means students can focus entirely on the musical challenge.

Mixing concept: frequency separation ·

Bass and melody stacked together sound muddy. A brief EQ introduction gives students the tool to solve the problem they just created: high-pass the melody, roll off the extreme highs of the bass.

Goals ·

  • Use a scale as the ingredient list for a melody
  • Write a short, singable melody that starts or ends on the root
  • Enter notes by playing in (and quantizing) and by clicking in
  • Discover and state the bassline rule: the bass changes to the root of the new chord
  • Choose four chords with Roman numerals and explain the feeling they create
  • Write a monophonic bassline with at least two rhythmic ideas
  • Give bass and melody their own frequency homes with EQ

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro and headphones for each student; a MIDI keyboard (an MPK Mini or similar) if you have one.
  • A piano sound on the class speakers for the Day 1 scales and demos.
  • Projector for the deck; two songs from your own YouTube or Spotify access: Seven Nation Army (The White Stripes) and Stand by Me (Ben E. King).

  • A chords-and-bassline Logic file for every student (right).
  • A place to submit an MP3 and a .zip, and a class feedback board for Day 4 (I use Padlet).

Before Day 1 ·

List who has a Unit 3 project; make and share your file for everyone else.
Test the class speakers; cue up the two songs.
Check that students can play notes: MPK Mini (find OCT +), or Musical Typing (Command-K).
Print the student pages; set up the submission spot and the feedback board.
The Logic file: continue from Unit 3

Students who did Unit 3 already have it: their Part 3 project is a bassline plus four chords. On Day 1 they open it and save a copy as “Your Name — Building Blocks Part 2”, then add the melody on a new track. On Day 2 the bassline in the same file is the one they analyze.

No Unit 3 project? Make the file once (10 minutes)

My recommended setup for the Part 2 file:

  • C major or A minor key — white keys only for accessibility
  • 4 chords, one per measure (the recipe below)
  • Bassline using root notes of each chord
  • Arrangement markers labeling each section
  • No melody track — leave that blank for students to fill in

Recipe (one chord per measure, as in the Unit 3 project):

  1. New project, a Software Instrument track with a bass sound (Library: Bass). A tempo between 80 and 100 BPM is comfortable.
  2. Draw a 4-measure MIDI region. In the piano roll add one whole note per measure, the root of each chord, low on the keyboard. C major: C – A – F – G. A minor: A – F – C – G.
  3. A second track with a piano or pad sound: the four chords, three notes stacked per measure. C major: I C - E - G, vi A - C - E, IV F - A - C, V G - B - D. A minor: i, VI, III, VII (spellings on the A minor card).
  4. Turn on Cycle over the 4 measures; add arrangement markers labeling the chords.
  5. Leave the melody track out: students add it. Share the project with your class.

Worked examples on pages 28–29. Logic sessions are not included in this kit.

Part one: a melody (Days 1–2) ·

Part 2 makes the first musical demand: write something. The scale gives students a safe set of notes, the Logic file removes the blank-page problem, and the constraint of 2 or 4 measures prevents the endless-tinkering failure mode. By the end students should have a short, singable melody and a physical understanding of what a scale is for.

The key insight to deliver: a scale is not an exercise. It is the ingredient list for a key. Every note in a melody should come from the scale. If it does, it will sound like it belongs. That is the only rule.

My course plan adds four ways to write a melody (page 19): play it in, draw a rhythm first, click one note and move it, use the step sequencer. And the most important thing you can say in this unit: “Simple and repetitive will always beat complicated and forgettable.”

Why a standalone unit ·

This is a new standalone unit carved from content currently buried inside EDM project videos. Students practice bassline and melody writing before they need it in a high-stakes project. The 'start on the root of each chord' rule is introduced explicitly and repeated.

How this kit differs from my classroom version
  • My Building Blocks material gives Part 2 (melody) 2 days and Part 4 (basslines) 2 days plus presentations, labeled Unit 3. This kit follows the 4 days of my course plan (intro, two work days, present) and is labeled Unit 4. Part 4’s analysis and reveal move to the end of Day 2 (12 + 6 minutes instead of 15 + 10). Project sheets say Unit 4 and the days.
  • Students write the melody in their Unit 3 project (it holds a bassline and four chords) or in your file, and analyze the bassline in that same file, so the basslines post says “Open the Logic file from your melody project” instead of “Download today’s Logic file”. The melody post asks for Part 2 of the packet only (Part 1 is in Unit 3).
  • My LMS is written as “your class page” and my Padlet steps as “the class feedback board”.
  • Fixes to my text: the A minor Reference Card’s closing line names its own chords (i, v, VII, VI), not I, V, vi; basslines post step 4 says “V to I feels resolved” (I wrote “I to V”); the setup uses one chord per measure, as in Unit 3 and every example (I wrote “4 chords, each lasting 4 measures”).

Part two: basslines (Days 2–4) ·

Part 4 has a reveal at its center. Students have been given basslines in every Logic file so far. Now they are asked to look carefully at one and figure out the rule themselves before it is stated. The analysis section of the packet is the discovery activity. The rule box in the packet confirms what they found. This sequence — discover, confirm, apply — is more memorable than being told the rule directly.

The second half of Part 4 connects everything. Students choose a chord progression using Roman numerals from their Reference Card and write a bassline that follows those roots. For the first time, they are making a compositional decision — not just executing an exercise. The written explanation of why they chose their progression is the assessment moment that shows you whether the Roman numeral instruction actually worked.

The reveal moment: how to frame it ·

After students have completed the analysis questions in the packet and identified that the bass note always follows the chord root, bring the class back together and ask: what did you find? Let two or three students answer. Then write the rule on the board:

When the chord changes, the bass note changes to the root of the new chord.

Then say: every bassline you have ever heard follows this rule. Another One Bites the Dust. Billie Jean. Your favorite song. The rule is always the same. Everything else is rhythm and decoration on top of this one idea.

The presentation question that matters ·

Every student should be asked this during their presentation: why did you choose this chord progression? What did you want it to feel like?

A student who answers with Roman numeral language — ‘I wanted it to feel unresolved so I ended on the V’ or ‘I used the vi because I wanted it to feel more emotional’ — has internalized the unit. A student who says ‘it sounded good’ has not. Both are valid at this stage, but the difference tells you what to reinforce in Unit 5 and beyond.

In class I also quote my tutorial line: “Your bassline loves to sit on the root of the chord. That is the safest and most powerful note you can play.”

Common student struggles · melody ·

Blank page paralysis: Enforce the loop-and-play rule: set a 2-measure loop over the biggest part of the progression and just play. Record anything. Students who resist starting are almost always overthinking. The prompt: play one note in a rhythm you like. Then move one note up or down.

Melodies that go nowhere: Students who try to use all 7 scale notes end up with aimless lines. Redirect them: use only 3 notes. Go up, then come back down. That is a melody.

Not quantizing: Play-in melodies with bad timing are discouraging. Show the Q shortcut immediately after the first recording attempt. Make it automatic.

Wrong octave: Students who do not shift the MPK Mini up an octave will record a melody that sits in the bass register. Show the OCT button on the keyboard before recording begins.

Common student struggles · basslines ·

Not changing the bass note at chord changes: Walk around during working time and ask every student: point to where your chord changes. Does your bass note change there? This is the most common error and the most important one to catch early.

Polyphonic basslines: Students leave previous notes sustaining when new ones start. Zoom into the piano roll and look for any notes that overlap. Any overlap in the bass is an error.

Chord progressions chosen randomly: Students who pick chords without thinking about function will have nothing to say during the presentation. Push back before they start building: tell me what you want this to feel like. Then ask: which Roman numerals would create that feeling?

Rhythmic basslines that are too busy: A bassline that plays sixteenth notes throughout is exhausting. Redirect with a simple rule: your bassline should breathe. Give some notes room. Less is almost always more.

Suggested variations (melody) ·

More accessible: Restrict students to just 3 notes from the scale. C, E, G for C Major or A, C, E for A Minor. A melody built from only 3 notes is still a real melody and removes the decision paralysis entirely.

More advanced: Ask students to write a second melody that acts as a response to the first — call and response. Or ask them to change the last measure of the melody so it ends differently on the final repetition.

Mixing concept: frequency separation (Day 2) ·

Frequency separation is introduced here because students immediately encounter the problem — their bassline and melody sound muddy when stacked. A brief introduction to EQ: high-pass the melody to remove low frequencies it does not need, roll off the extreme highs of the bass. Students do not need to understand parametric EQ yet. They need to understand that instruments have frequency homes and mixing means giving each one space to live.

Grading tips ·

  • Two rubrics, each with three categories scored 2–5, for /15 each (pages 22 and 25).
  • Melody: open the piano roll. In key? 2 or 4 measures? First or last note C or A? Quantized?
  • Bassline: point to each chord change. Is the root there? Any overlapping notes? Two rhythm ideas? Then read the why answer.
  • Worked examples and answer keys: pages 27–29.

Bass & Melody WritingFour ways to write a melody
STUDENT
Day 1
NameDate

There is more than one way to write a melody. Try at least two of these today. Whichever you use, the rules are the same: 2 or 4 measures, notes from your scale, start or end on the root.

1
Play it in

Loop 2 measures with Cycle (C). Press R, play on the white keys, stop. Select the notes and press Q to quantize.

Good when you can already hear an idea in your head.

2
Draw a rhythm first

With the Pencil, click notes in a rhythm you like, all on the root. Play it back. Then drag some notes up or down to other scale notes.

Good when you have a groove but no tune yet.

3
Click one note, move it

Click in one note. Listen. Drag it up or down until it fits the chord. Then click the next one.

Good when you want to build slowly, one decision at a time.

4
Use the step sequencer

Control-click the track lane and choose Create Pattern Region. Turn steps on to place notes; each row is one pitch.

Good when you want a short, repeating, rhythmic hook.

Stuck?

Use only 3 notes. C major: C, E, G · A minor: A, C, E. Go up, then come back down. That is a melody.

Remember

Simple and repetitive will always beat complicated and forgettable.

How to Write a Melody ·

Keep it short2 or 4 measures only. Once you have something you like, copy and paste it across the progression. Short melodies are easier to remember and easier to build on.
White keys onlyIn C Major or A Minor, every white key is a safe note. You cannot play a wrong note if you stay on the white keys.
Give it shapeA good melody goes up for a bit, then comes down — or starts high and descends. Avoid playing the same note over and over, or jumping randomly in both directions.
Start or end on the rootAnchor your melody to C (for C Major) or A (for A Minor). This creates a sense of resolution.
Simple and repetitive winsA two-note melody with a strong rhythm is more powerful than eight random notes. Write something you could hum after hearing it once.

Read each word and its meaning. These are the words you should own by the end of the unit.

WordMeaning
Scalean ordered set of notes built with a formula of whole and half steps. The ingredient list for a key.
Keythe home a piece of music settles on: its home note, its scale and its chords (this unit: C major or A minor).
Rootthe note a chord is built on, and the home note of a key (C in C major, A in A minor).
Melodythe tune: one note at a time, on top. Short, repetitive and singable.
Contourthe shape of a melody: up for a bit, then down, or high and then descending.
Play-inrecording a part by playing it on a MIDI keyboard (R), then quantizing it (Q).
Click-inentering notes one at a time with the Pencil tool in the piano roll.
Quantizesnapping notes to the grid. It fixes timing, not notes. It is not cheating.
Basslinethe lowest part. It follows the roots of the chords and gives the music its rhythmic foundation.
Monophonicone note at a time, with no overlapping notes. Every bassline in this unit is monophonic.
Chord progressionthe order the chords come in, written as Roman numerals (I–V–vi–IV) or chord names.
Frequency separationgiving each part its own frequency home so bass and melody do not turn to mud. A high-pass filter on the melody is the first tool.
