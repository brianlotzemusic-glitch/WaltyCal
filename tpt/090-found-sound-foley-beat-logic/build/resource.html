What this is ·

A 3-class-period found sound project for high school music technology. Every student records 6 to 8 sounds from safe household objects (wrapping paper, tape, boxes, a tube, a small bell, or any paper and boxes), trims each one, gives it a drum role, plays a 4-bar pattern on a 16-step grid, tries swing and humanize (if their version has them) and arranges and mixes 16 bars. Students plan the timing with simple arithmetic, present the result and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look at the timing page, the sounds page, the role table, the step grid and the swing page (pages 11–15) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–24), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–8), Class 2 (slides 9–12) and Class 3 (slides 13–18). The slide about the three classes carries the badge for all three. Every slide has speaker notes.

The project in one line

Gift Wrap Foley Beat · Found sounds, roles, step grid and mix · Logic Pro
Six to eight sounds you record yourself, one beat built from them.

What students make ·

A beat of 16 bars, built in Logic Pro from 6 to 8 found sounds that students record themselves: wrapping paper, tape, a box, a tube, scissors on paper and a small bell, or any paper and boxes. Each sound is trimmed and given a role: kick, snare, hat or percussion. A 4-bar pattern plays them on a 16-step grid, swing and humanize are tried (if the version has them), and the sounds become the layers of an arrangement with an intro, a build, a groove and an outro. Students hand in a bounced file and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play the beat and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a sample-based beat is made from found sounds. Students learn to record clean one-shots at a safe level, to trim them, to give each a drum role, to play them on a 16-step grid, to move them off the grid on purpose and to arrange and mix the layers, all in Logic Pro.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Plan a beat with timing math: one bar of 4/4 = 240 ÷ BPM seconds and one 16th-note step = 15 ÷ BPM seconds (0.15 s at 100 BPM)
  • Record 6 to 8 clean found sounds of your own at a safe level, trim each to its start, and give each a role: kick, snare, hat or percussion
  • Build a 4-bar, 16-step pattern, draw it on a step grid and count its hits per bar and in all
  • Add swing and humanize (if your version has them), layer a 16-bar arrangement and mix it, starting with small moves

About Foley and the theme ·

Foley is the reproduction of everyday sound effects that are added to films, videos and other media in post-production, after the picture has been shot. It is named after sound-effects artist Jack Foley. In this project you are the Foley artist for a beat: the sounds are everyday ones, and you record them yourself.

Foley artists make the sound of footsteps, rustling and door handles with everyday objects. This project borrows that idea for music: paper, tape, boxes and bells become the drum kit of a beat. Students record their own found sounds, so there is no existing song, sample or recording anywhere in the project.

Not every family wraps gifts, and that is fine. The project is about the sounds of paper, tape, boxes and bells: any paper and boxes will do, such as newspaper, a paper bag, a cereal box, a shoebox or a cardboard tube. Nothing needs to be bought.

SETUP

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones.
  • A microphone, shared if you must. A quiet corner with soft surfaces beats a loud room. A laptop’s built-in microphone will do for practice.
  • Safe household objects: paper, tape, cardboard boxes and tubes, small bells. Students may bring their own with permission; keep a small box of spares in the room.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav files, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–24).

Before Class 1 ·

Read the timing page (page 11) so you can answer ‘how long is one step at 100 BPM?’ and ‘how long are 16 bars?’
Test a microphone in Logic Pro on one computer, and tap a box near it while watching the meter (page 12).
Open Logic Pro on a student computer and check which tools your version has: Drum Machine Designer, Quick Sampler, the Step Sequencer, an EQ with a high-pass filter (see page 8).
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Watch for a recording that clips or is full of room noise, a sound trimmed too late so that its start is cut off, hits that sit off the grid, and a mix so crowded that the sounds disappear. A good listening day: play each beat and ask the class two questions. Which found sound did you recognize first? Where did the build begin? If a layer is hard to hear, find it on the arrangement map together. This kit includes no recordings and names no songs or artists.

Safety and permission
  • Students ask permission before using an object, and an adult helps with anything beyond paper, tape, cardboard and small bells.
  • No glass, no knives, no blades. Ordinary scissors only on paper, closed slowly, or use a pen click instead.
  • Tap gently, and keep headphone volume low.

Not every family wraps gifts ·

Not every family wraps gifts, and that is fine. The project is about the sounds of paper, tape, boxes and bells: any paper and boxes will do, such as newspaper, a paper bag, a cereal box, a shoebox or a cardboard tube. Nothing needs to be bought.

Their own recordings only

Students record their OWN found sounds. They do not sample songs, videos, games or any commercial recording. Every object is used with permission, and an adult helps with anything beyond paper, tape, cardboard and small bells. No glass, no knives, no blades, and the volume stays low.

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a sample-based beat is made from found sounds. Students learn to record clean one-shots at a safe level, to trim them, to give each a drum role, to play them on a 16-step grid, to move them off the grid on purpose and to arrange and mix the layers, all in Logic Pro.

Common student struggles ·

  • A recording is noisy or distorted: record again with the input turned down and the object a little farther from the microphone. A recording that clipped cannot be repaired (page 12).
  • A sound clicks or has a gap at the start: trim just before the first sharp part of the sound, the transient, and not after it (page 13).
  • The hits feel off the beat: check the project tempo and the grid, and count the steps on the step grid (pages 11 and 14).
  • The mix sounds muddy or boomy: use a high-pass filter on the sounds that are not meant to be low, starting with a small move, and leave the kick alone (page 17).

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Keep the tempo at 100 BPM, where a step lasts 0.15 seconds and a bar lasts 2.4 seconds (page 11); record 6 sounds, use the model pattern on page 14 and build only the first 8 bars of the arrangement.

More Advanced: Record 8 sounds, write a different 4-bar pattern for the groove section and compare two swing settings by ear (page 15). Or build a 32-bar arrangement with a second pattern.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Noisy or clipped recordings: the sounds page (page 12) has a signal-chain drawing, a headroom checklist and a take log.
  • Clicks and gaps: the role table page (page 13) has a trim checklist.
  • Hits off the beat: the timing page (page 11) gives the length of a step at six tempos, and the step grid (page 14) shows every hit.
  • A muddy mix: the mix page (page 17) has a high-pass step, and the worked example (pages 9–10) shows a full set of choices.

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 21). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Sounds & Recording (rubric row 1): listen for noise and clipping, check the take log and ask where each sound came from (page 12).
  • Roles & Pattern (rubric row 2): check the role table and compare the step grid with what plays (pages 13 and 14).
  • Swing & Feel (rubric row 3): ask the student to play a straight and a swung version and say what changed (page 15).
  • Arrangement & Mix (rubric row 4): play the whole beat and find the intro, build, groove and outro on the arrangement map; check the mix checklist (pages 16 and 17).
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): the timing page, the role table, the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 24) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 24): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

Gift Wrap Foley BeatLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a Gift Wrap Foley Beat is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 and 2 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

Project and tempoFile › New…, then an Audio track to record the sounds. Set the project tempo in the control bar to the BPM on your timing page and keep the time signature at 4/4. Set the snap or grid value to 16th notes (if your version shows one) so that every hit lands on a step.
Record a soundOn the Audio track, choose the microphone or interface as the input and click the record-enable button (R). Set the gain on the interface, not the fader, and leave plenty of headroom: record the loudest tap you plan to make and keep the meter well below the top. Leave a moment of silence before each hit and record several takes. Re-record a take that clips.
Trim a recordingSelect the region and shorten its start so that it begins just before the first sharp part of the sound, the transient, and its end after the sound has died away. Look for a fade option on the region (if your version has one) to remove a click at the end. Rename each region after its sound.
Load the soundsAdd a Software Instrument track and choose Drum Machine Designer (if your version has it), then drop each trimmed recording onto its own pad. Quick Sampler (if your version has it) can hold one sound at a time. Name each pad after its sound and role.
Play the patternPlay the pads from a keyboard or pad controller, or draw notes in the Piano Roll with the grid set to 16th notes: 16 steps to a bar, for 4 bars. The Step Sequencer (if your version has it) shows one row for each sound and a column for each step, and works with Drum Machine Designer. Quantize to 16th notes if hits are late.
VelocityVelocity is a number that says how hard a note or pad is hit (many programs use a scale up to 127). Look for a velocity value for each step or note (if your version shows one). Change the velocities a little so that not every hit is equally loud.
Swing and humanizeLook for a swing control in the Step Sequencer or in the region or track settings, and for a humanize or randomize option for timing and velocity (each if your version has them). The scales and names differ between versions, so listen and compare a straight and a swung version.
EQ and the high-pass filterAdd an EQ plug-in on a track, such as Channel EQ (if your version has it), and look for a high-pass filter, which lets higher sounds through and cuts the lows. Start with a small move, listen, and stop as soon as the sound starts to feel thin.
Mixer and bounceOpen the Mixer (the key command is X by default, if your version has it) and set levels and pan. Keep the master peak below 0 dBFS with room to spare. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV, for the whole beat. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

Step 1: plan the beat. In 4/4 there are 4 beats in a bar, and each beat holds four 16th-note steps, so a bar has 16 steps. These formulas turn a tempo into seconds. Every number below is computed, so you can check your own arithmetic against it.

The formulas (4/4)

Seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM

Seconds per 16th-note step = 15 ÷ BPM

Seconds per bar = 240 ÷ BPM (16 steps, 4 beats)

A hit on grid step s of bar b is at ((b − 1) × 16 + (s − 1)) × 15 ÷ BPM seconds

A piece of b bars lasts b × 240 ÷ BPM seconds

Example: 100 BPM: 15 ÷ 100 = 0.15 s per step, and 240 ÷ 100 = 2.4 s per bar.

Rounding rules

Seconds per beat, bar and section to 3 decimals and seconds per step to 4; a 5 rounds up. So 15 ÷ 128 = 0.1172 seconds (the exact value is 0.1171875). The tables for 80, 100 and 120 BPM are exact.

Going the other way: how many bars fit in a time?

Bars = seconds × BPM ÷ 240. Check: 38.4 seconds at 100 BPM is 38.4 × 100 ÷ 240 = 16 bars. Try it: 24 seconds at 120 BPM = 24 × 120 ÷ 240 = 12 bars, and 19.2 seconds at 100 BPM = 19.2 × 100 ÷ 240 = 8 bars.

Step 2: collect the sounds. Choose 6 to 8 sounds from safe household objects and record each one yourself, as a one-shot: one clean hit with a little silence before it. Think like a Foley artist, who records everyday sounds to add to a film. Your recordings are the only sounds in your beat.

Safety and permission
Ask permission before you use an object, and tell an adult where you are recording.
Use only safe household objects that cannot break: paper, tape, cardboard, small bells. No glass, no knives, no blades.
Scissors: ordinary scissors on paper only, with an adult’s help, closed slowly and pointed away from everyone. Skip this sound if you are unsure; a pen click works instead.
Safety, continued
Tap gently. Do not hit, drop or throw anything, and do not make loud sounds near anyone’s ears.
Keep your headphone volume low, and turn it down before you play a sound back.

Reading the grid

A hit on grid step s of bar b starts at ((b − 1) × 16 + (s − 1)) × 15 ÷ BPM seconds. At 100 BPM, bar 2, grid step 14 (the tube tap) is at (16 + 13) × 0.15 = 4.35 s.

The box tap plays on grid steps 1, 7 and 11 and the tape pull on 5 and 13, which are beats 2 and 4. Velocity sets how hard each hit is, if your version shows one for each step.

Step 5: swing and humanize. Swing delays every second step of a pair, so the pair is long and short instead of even. Humanize makes tiny random changes to timing and velocity, so a pattern sounds less like a machine. Look for a swing control and a humanize option (each if your version has them); their scales differ, so listen and compare.

Two steps make a pair. Split long : short = r : 1, the second step starts at 2r ÷ (r + 1) steps instead of 1, so it is late by (r − 1) ÷ (r + 1) of a step. For 2 : 1 that is 1/3 of a step: 0.15 ÷ 3 = 0.05 s at 100 BPM.

Which hits does swing move?

A 16th-note swing moves the hits on even steps (2, 4, 6 and so on). In the model, 7 of the 65 hits are on even steps: the scissor snips and two tube taps. The crinkles are on odd steps, so they stay put.

One common description of swing says the first note of a pair is often about twice as long as the second. Try a little first.

Music Technology | Found Sound Beat | 3 class periods

Overview ·

Build a beat in Logic Pro from sounds you record yourself. Students choose 6 to 8 household sounds (wrapping paper, tape, boxes, a tube, scissors and bells, or any paper and boxes), record each one at a safe level, trim and clean it, and give it a drum-machine job: kick, snare, hat or percussion. They play a 4-bar pattern on a 16-step grid, add swing and humanize if their version has them, layer the sounds into a 16-bar arrangement and mix it. Timing math drives every choice, and nobody needs to read music.

Goals ·

  • Plan a beat with timing math: one bar of 4/4 = 240 ÷ BPM seconds and one 16th-note step = 15 ÷ BPM seconds (0.15 s at 100 BPM)
  • Record 6 to 8 clean found sounds of your own at a safe level, trim each to its start, and give each a role: kick, snare, hat or percussion
  • Build a 4-bar, 16-step pattern, draw it on a step grid and count its hits per bar and in all
  • Add swing and humanize (if your version has them), layer a 16-bar arrangement and mix it, starting with small moves

Tips for Success ·

  • Record each sound several times and keep the cleanest. A good recording makes every later step easier.
  • Leave plenty of headroom: record quietly. You can raise a quiet sound later, but you cannot repair one that clipped.
  • Start with a simple pattern and add one change at a time. Listen after each change.
  • Save a new version of your project at the end of each class, so you can always go back.
