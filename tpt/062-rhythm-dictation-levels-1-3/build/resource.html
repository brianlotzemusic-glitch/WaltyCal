Teacher Notes

Rhythm Dictation · Levels 1–3 · 30 exercises · grades 3–8

What this is. Thirty rhythm dictation exercises in three levels. Students hear a rhythm, echo it, hear it in their heads and then write it in notation. Each exercise has a teacher script with the meter, tempo, count-in and the exact syllables and counts to perform, a student page with blank bars, and an answer key. No audio is included. You perform each rhythm yourself by clapping, tapping or playing it on one pitch.

Tempo and count-in

Every script gives a tempo in beats per minute (BPM) and a count-in. Count in aloud before every play: four beats in 4/4, three beats in 3/4 and, in 6/8, the two big beats twice (1 2 1 2). In 6/8 the BPM is the pulse of the dotted quarter note.

Scoring and Assessment

Score every bar, then add up the exercise

Score each bar separately, so a student who writes one bar wrongly still earns credit for the others. Every bar is worth 2 points. An exercise is worth 2 points for each of its bars: 4 points for two bars, 6 for three bars and 8 for four bars.

PointsWhat the bar looks like
2Exactly right. Every note and rest value is correct and in the right order, stems and beams are correct, and the bar adds up to the time signature.
1Almost. The bar adds up to the time signature but one note or rest value is wrong, or the rhythm is right but has one notation slip such as a missing beam or a stem on the wrong side.
0The bar does not add up to the time signature, or it is blank.

How to assess quickly

  • Check the sum first. Have students count each bar aloud against the time signature. A bar that does not add up scores 0 and is easy to spot.
  • Then check the pattern. Compare the bar with the answer key beat by beat. Look first at the rests, then at the held notes and then at the beams.
  • Name the error. Write one word beside a lost point: sum, rest, value, beam or stem. Students fix the bar and clap it back for full credit.
  • Peer check. Partners swap papers, clap each other's bars from the notation and mark them with the answer key before you collect the pages.

Example

A student writes Exercise 4 (two bars, 4 points). Bar 1 is exactly right and earns 2 points. Bar 2 adds up to four beats but has a quarter note where the quarter rest belongs, so it earns 1 point. The exercise score is 3 out of 4.

About Rhythm Dictation

Hear it, say it, write it

In rhythm dictation you listen to a rhythm and write it in notation. Every exercise has blank bars with the barlines and the time signature already printed. Follow the same five steps each time.

1

Listen. Your teacher performs the rhythm. Keep a steady beat and notice the time signature and the number of bars.

2

Echo. Clap or say the rhythm back with the syllables so you know what you heard.

3

Audiate. Hear the rhythm silently in your head while your finger keeps the beat. This is called audiating, and it is the step that makes writing easier.

4

Write. Write the notes and rests bar by bar. Make every bar add up to the time signature.

5

Check. Clap what you wrote, count each bar and compare it with the answer key.

Worked example

You hear: ta ti-ti ta shh.

Add it up: ta is 1 beat, ti-ti is 1 beat, ta is 1 beat and shh (a quarter rest) is 1 beat. That makes 4 beats, which fills one bar of 4/4.

Your turn

Your teacher performs a two-bar rhythm in 4/4. Listen, echo, audiate, then write it here.

Remember

  • The top number of a time signature tells you how many beats fill a bar.
  • The bottom number names the note that gets one beat.
  • A rest is silence, but it still counts and it still needs to be written.

Reading the Count column. A number is a note that starts on that beat, a plus is the half of the beat, and e and a are the other quarters. A dash means a held note is still sounding, and a number in parentheses is a rest. Every row is shown starting on beat 1.

  • Clap every note. Clap where a note starts. A rest is silence, so say "shh" with your hands apart. Hold a long note by keeping your hands together while you say the syllable.
  • Beams. Eighth notes joined by a beam share one beat, so each is half a beat. Four sixteenth notes with two beams share one beat, so each is a quarter of a beat.
  • Dots. A dot adds half of the note's value. A dotted half note lasts 3 beats. A dotted quarter note lasts 1 and a half beats.

Chart: 6/8 and the Three Meters

Compound meter and how each meter counts
NotationNameLengthSayCountLevel
Dotted quarter note1 beatta1 – –Level 3
Three eighth notes, joined by a beam1 beatti-ti-ti1 la liLevel 3
Quarter note, then an eighth note1 beatta-ti1 – liLevel 3
Eighth note, then a quarter note1 beatti-ta1 la –Level 3

Reading 6/8. In 6/8 every eighth note gets a count, 1 la li 2 la li, and each big beat is a dotted quarter note. The rows above show one big beat starting on count 1.

The three meters

4/4. Four beats in a bar, and a quarter note gets one beat.

3/4. Three beats in a bar, and a quarter note gets one beat.

6/8. Six eighth notes in a bar, felt as two big beats of three eighths each.

  • Compound meter. 6/8 is a compound meter because each beat divides into three. Eighth notes are beamed in groups of three, one group for each big beat.
  • Tempo in 6/8. The scripts give the BPM of the dotted quarter note, the big beat.

Self-assessment

Level 1: I can

  • write quarter notes and quarter rests
  • write two eighth notes joined by a beam
  • write half notes
  • make each bar add up to 4 beats
  • echo a rhythm before I write it

Level 2: I can

  • write 3/4 bars that add up to 3 beats
  • write whole notes and dotted half notes
  • write eighth-quarter-eighth syncopation
  • keep three or four bars in my head
  • check my bars against the beat

Level 3: I can

  • write groups of four sixteenth notes
  • write a dotted quarter note and an eighth note
  • write 6/8 with eighths in groups of three
  • write four bars in 4/4, 3/4 or 6/8
  • find and fix my own mistakes
