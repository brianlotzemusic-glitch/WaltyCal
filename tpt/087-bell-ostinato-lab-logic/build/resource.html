What this is ·

A 3-class-period ostinato project for high school music technology. Every student enters the same four-note motif in Logic Pro, repeats it as an ostinato in 3/4, layers it at several octaves and sounds over a 16-bar form, answers it in a second key and shapes the piece from pp to ff with volume automation (if their version has it). Students plan the timing with simple arithmetic, present the result and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look at the timing page, the note chart, the layer map and the dynamics ramp (pages 11–16) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–24), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–8), Class 2 (slides 9–11) and Class 3 (slides 12–17). The slide about the three classes carries the badge for all three. Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

OVERVIEW
Ostinato project
The project in one line

Bell Ostinato Lab · Motif, layers, dynamics and automation · Logic Pro
One four-note motif, five layers, a piece that grows from pp to ff.

What students make ·

A piece of 16 bars of 3/4 built in Logic Pro from one four-note motif. The motif is entered in the Piano Roll and repeated as an ostinato. It is layered at other octaves and with other sounds, answered in a second key in the bell’s rests, and shaped with a volume automation line that builds from pp to ff. Students hand in a bounced file and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play the piece and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a loop-based piece is built from one short pattern. Students enter a motif exactly, name its intervals, repeat it as an ostinato in 3/4, layer it at several octaves and sounds, answer it in a second key and shape the result with dynamics and volume automation, all in Logic Pro.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Plan a 16-bar form in 3/4 with tempo math: one bar = 180 ÷ BPM seconds (2 seconds at 90 BPM), so 16 bars last 32 seconds at 90 BPM
  • Enter the four-note motif exactly (Bb4, A4, Bb4, G4) and name its intervals: −1, +1 and −3 semitones
  • Layer the ostinato at several octaves and sounds, and write a call-and-response answer in E minor, 3 semitones lower
  • Shape the piece from pp to ff with a volume automation line (if your version has it) and mix the layers so each can be heard

About the motif ·

An ostinato is a short pattern that repeats, and bells are a good sound for it. This project starts from the four-note motif of the Ukrainian choral piece Shchedryk, which Mykola Leontovych (1877–1921) wrote and revised over several years and which was first performed in December 1916. In English-speaking countries the tune is known as “Carol of the Bells”. Only the four-note motif is used. There are no words, and students build their own original layers, answers and dynamics around it.

The skills here carry over to every loop-based piece: enter a pattern exactly, repeat it, layer it in different registers and sounds, let a second voice answer it, and shape the whole thing with dynamics and automation. The math is the same too: beats, bars, seconds and semitones.

SETUP
Teacher

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones. Nothing is recorded: every sound comes from software instruments.
  • A keyboard is optional. Students can draw the notes in the Piano Roll, so a MIDI keyboard is a convenience, not a requirement.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav files, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The timing page, note chart, layer map and dynamics ramp, printed (pages 11–16), or shown on the projector.
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–24).

Before Class 1 ·

Read the timing page (page 11) so you can answer ‘how long is one bar at 100 BPM?’ and ‘how long is a 16-bar piece?’
Read the note chart (page 12) and play the motif once: Bb, A, Bb, G.
Open Logic Pro on a student computer and check which tools your version has: a bell or celesta sound, the Piano Roll, automation (see page 8).
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Watch for a motif with a wrong note or the wrong length, a repeat that has a gap at the barline, a layer that hides the ostinato, and an automation line that jumps instead of rising. A good listening day: play each piece and ask the class two questions. Which layer joined last? Where did you first hear the answer? If a layer is hard to hear, find it on the layer map together. This kit includes no recordings and prints no words.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: which layer is this, and which bars does it play?
  • Use only the four-note motif and the sounds that come with Logic. No other existing songs, videos or recordings.
  • A student who is stuck can build the bell, one higher copy and a rising volume line: those already make a piece.

The motif and copyright ·

The motif comes from Shchedryk, a choral piece by Mykola Leontovych (1877–1921) that was first performed in December 1916. The music is old enough to be in the public domain. The English words and the 1936 choral arrangement that made it famous as “Carol of the Bells” are a separate work: this kit does not use them, and students must not copy any later arrangement. Students build their own original layers.

Keep the volume moderate

Students hear a short pattern over and over in headphones, and the finale is the loudest part. Tell them to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a loop-based piece is built from one short pattern. Students enter a motif exactly, name its intervals, repeat it as an ostinato in 3/4, layer it at several octaves and sounds, answer it in a second key and shape the result with dynamics and volume automation, all in Logic Pro.

Common student struggles ·

  • The motif sounds wrong: compare each note with the note chart note by note, and check the octave and the lengths (page 12). Software may label octaves differently, so compare the note letters and the distance in semitones.
  • The repeat has a gap or a hiccup: check that the region is exactly one bar of 3/4 long, and that the last note ends on the barline (page 13).
  • The layers fight each other: take layers away until the motif is clear, then bring them back one at a time, and make the quiet ones quieter (page 14).
  • The crescendo jumps instead of growing: check that the automation points are joined by straight lines, and that each point is higher than the one before it (page 16).

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Keep the tempo at 90 BPM, where a bar lasts exactly 2 seconds and 16 bars last 32 seconds (page 11); use only the original key, enter the motif once and loop it, and build only the bell, the celesta and the pad.

More Advanced: Write an original answer phrase of your own in the bell’s rests, not only the motif in a second key, or build a 32-bar form with a second round of call and response (page 14).

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • A wrong motif: the note chart (page 12) shows every note by name, number and length, in two keys.
  • A repeat with a gap: the entry page (page 13) has a checklist for the region length and the barline.
  • Crowded layers: the layer map (page 14) shows which of 5 layers plays in each bar, and the worked example (pages 9–10) shows a full set of choices.
  • A jumpy crescendo: the dynamics page (page 16) gives the level at the start of every bar.

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 21). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Motif & Ostinato (rubric row 1): check the entry log and the intervals against the note chart, and listen for a clean repeat (pages 12 and 13).
  • Layers & Form (rubric row 2): compare the student’s layer map with what plays, bar by bar (page 14).
  • Dynamics & Automation (rubric row 3): ask the student to show the automation line and read the level at bars 1, 9, 13 and 16 (page 16).
  • Answer & Mix (rubric row 4): play bars 9 to 12 and find the call and the answer; then check the mix checklist (pages 15 and 17).
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): the timing page, the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 24) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 24): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a Bell Ostinato Lab is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 and 2 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

Project, tempo and meterCreate a project with a Software Instrument track. Set the project tempo in the control bar to the BPM on your timing page, and set the time signature to 3/4 (look in the control bar or the project settings; the place differs between versions).
Choose a bell soundOpen the Software Instrument library and look for a bell, glockenspiel, celesta or mallet sound (if your version has one). Synths such as Alchemy (if your version has it) can make a bell sound from scratch.
Enter the motifOpen the region in the Piano Roll and set the grid to eighth notes. Draw Bb, A, Bb, G with the lengths on the note chart: a quarter note, two eighth notes and a quarter note, so the four notes fill exactly one bar. Octave numbers differ between versions, so compare the note letters and the distance in semitones.
Check the pitchesClick or play each note and read its name from the Piano Roll or a help tag (if your version shows one), or compare the position of the notes on the piano keys. The distance between notes is what matters: down 1, up 1, down 3 semitones.
Repeat the regionMake the one-bar region repeat for 4 bars with the loop option on the region, or copy it end to end (the loop option is in the region’s settings, if your version has it). A tiny gap or overlap at the barline shows up as a hiccup, so check the region ends exactly at the end of the bar.
Layer and transposeCopy the region to new tracks and give each its own sound. To move a layer by an octave, transpose its notes by +12 or −12 semitones in the Piano Roll or with a transpose control (if your version has one). For the answer, transpose a copy by −3 semitones.
VelocityIn the Piano Roll look for the velocity display (if your version has it). Velocity runs from 1 to 127 and controls how hard a note is played; the dynamics table uses it as a starting point: pp 32 up to ff 112.
Volume automationOn a track, show its automation (look for an Automation button or menu in your version) and choose Volume as the parameter. Click on the line to add points, drag them to the levels on your plan and check that straight lines join them. Reading and writing modes (if your version has them) record fader moves instead.
Mixer and bounceOpen the Mixer (the key command is X by default, if your version has it) and set levels and pan. Keep the master peak below 0 dBFS with room to spare. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV, for the whole piece. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

EXAMPLE
Teacher

An invented plan on paper: a piece at 90 BPM from the four-note motif. No audio is included. Student pieces will differ. Page 1 of 2: the plan, the timing and the layer map.

1 · A weak plan

I will enter some bell notes, repeat them, add more sounds and make it louder at the end, and it will sound cool.

It names no tempo, no length, no key, no layers and no dynamics.

2 · A clear plan

My piece is in 3/4 at 90 BPM. One beat lasts 60 ÷ 90 = 0.667 seconds and one bar lasts 180 ÷ 90 = 2 seconds. My form is 16 bars = 32 seconds: a solo bell in bars 1 to 4, the celesta and the pad joining in bars 5 to 8, the bell and an answer taking turns in bars 9 to 12, and all of the layers except the answer playing in bars 13 to 16. I use 5 layers in all. My motif is Bb4, A4, Bb4, G4 in G minor, and my answer is G4, F#4, G4, E4 in E minor, 3 semitones lower. My volume line starts at pp (32), reaches mp (64) at bar 9 and mf (80) at bar 13, and rises to ff (112) at the end of bar 16.

Step 1: plan the form. In 3/4 there are 3 beats in a bar, each beat is a quarter note, and a bar holds 6 eighth notes. These formulas turn a tempo into seconds. Every number below is computed, so you can check your own arithmetic against it.

The formulas (3/4)

Seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM

Seconds per eighth note = 30 ÷ BPM

Seconds per bar = 180 ÷ BPM = 3 beats = 6 eighth notes

A section or piece of b bars lasts b × 180 ÷ BPM seconds

Bar b starts at (b − 1) × 180 ÷ BPM seconds

Example: 90 BPM: 60 ÷ 90 = 0.667 s per beat, and 180 ÷ 90 = 2 s per bar.

Rounding rules

Seconds per beat, bar and section to 3 decimals and seconds per eighth note to 4; a 5 rounds up. So 30 ÷ 72 = 0.4167 seconds (the exact value is 0.41666…). The form-length table is exact at these three tempos.

Step 3: build the ostinato. An ostinato is a short pattern that repeats in the same voice. Your first job is to enter the four notes exactly, then repeat them. Take your time: every layer you add later is a copy of this one bar. The quick reference is on page 8.

Set up the project
The tempo is the BPM on my timing page, and the time signature is 3/4.
I have a Software Instrument track with a bell, glockenspiel or celesta sound, if my version has one.
My headphones are on and the volume is low.
The Piano Roll grid is set to eighth notes.
Enter the motif
I draw 4 notes in one bar: Bb, A, Bb, G.
The lengths are a quarter note, an eighth note, an eighth note and a quarter note.
The first note starts on beat 1 and the last note ends at the end of the bar.
I compare every note with the note chart, letter by letter.
Repeat it
The region is exactly one bar of 3/4 long.
I repeat it for 4 bars, with the loop option or by copying, with no gap or overlap at the barlines.
I listen to all 4 bars with my eyes closed: the pattern is steady.
I save a new version of the project.
If it sounds wrong

Check the notes one at a time. If every note sounds right but the pattern sounds odd, check the lengths. If the pattern sounds right but too high or too low, check the octave; the shape and the distances are what matter.

Use only the motif. Do not copy any other melody or arrangement into your project.

Step 6: shape the sound. Dynamics are how loud or soft the music is. A crescendo is a gradual increase in loudness. In Logic Pro you can draw one as a volume automation line: points on a track’s volume that you place and drag (if your version has automation). The plan is an example on a 0 to 127 scale, like MIDI velocity, not a rule.

Reading the line

Between two points the level changes by the same amount each bar: (end level − start level) ÷ number of bars. Bars 13 to 16: (112 − 80) ÷ 4 = 8 levels per bar, so the line is at 80 at bar 13 and 104 at bar 16.

Velocity sets how hard each note is played; volume automation sets the level of the whole track. Try both and listen to the difference.

Bell Ostinato Lab

Music Technology | Bell Ostinato Lab | 3 class periods

Overview ·

Build a short piece in Logic Pro from one four-note motif. Students enter the motif in the Piano Roll (Bb4, A4, Bb4, G4: quarter, eighth, eighth, quarter, one bar of 3/4), repeat it as an ostinato, and layer it at different octaves and with different bell, celesta and pad sounds over a 16-bar form. They write an answer in a second key, E minor, 3 semitones lower, and shape the piece from pp to ff with volume automation. Tempo math drives every choice, and nobody has to read notation.

Goals ·

  • Plan a 16-bar form in 3/4 with tempo math: one bar = 180 ÷ BPM seconds (2 seconds at 90 BPM), so 16 bars last 32 seconds at 90 BPM
  • Enter the four-note motif exactly (Bb4, A4, Bb4, G4) and name its intervals: −1, +1 and −3 semitones
  • Layer the ostinato at several octaves and sounds, and write a call-and-response answer in E minor, 3 semitones lower
  • Shape the piece from pp to ff with a volume automation line (if your version has it) and mix the layers so each can be heard

Key Vocabulary ·

Tips for Success ·

  • Enter the motif once, check it note by note against the chart, and only then copy it. A wrong note repeated 16 times is 16 wrong notes.
  • Keep the ostinato steady and let the layers change around it. The listener hears the pattern most clearly when it does not move.
  • Add one layer at a time and listen after each. If you cannot hear a layer, make another one quieter instead of making it louder.
  • Save a new version of your project at the end of each class, so you can always go back.
