What this is ·

A complete 6-day drum unit for high school music technology. Students write drums for music that already exists: a stem, a recording with the drums taken out. In Part 1, Foundation, they write two contrasting patterns, A and B, and place them where the sections change in a 16-measure stem. In Part 2, Full Song, they map every section change in a longer stem and announce each one with a fill, a breakdown, a cut or a build. The mixing concept is gain staging: nothing in the red.

This is Unit 2 of my Digital Music Production 1 course, after Unit 1, Beat From Nothing (a separate resource), where students learned Drum Machine Designer and the Step Sequencer.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the overview and pacing (pages 3 and 8) and the teacher notes (pages 12–14).
  2. Get your stems (pages 5–6): a few 16-measure Foundation stems and a few Full Song stems. None are included.
  3. Program the four example transitions (page 26) and cue up In the Air Tonight (Phil Collins) on YouTube or Spotify.
  4. Print the student pages (pages 15–24), one set per student.

Stems & GroovesUnit overview
OVERVIEW
DMP1 · Unit 2

What students make ·

Two drum tracks written to fit stems the teacher supplies. Foundation: an A pattern and a contrasting B pattern, at least 3 layers each, placed where the sections change in a 16-measure stem. Full Song: drums for a full-length stem with a deliberate transition at every section change, at least two types, mixed so the drums serve the stem.

What students learn ·

AABA form through doing — not naming. How drums change when a section changes. Why musical continuity matters. How to listen to a piece of music and respond to it. That mixing decisions (volume, panning) are musical decisions, not technical ones.

Why this unit lands here ·

The stems project is the most musically sophisticated thing in the early curriculum. It removes the blank-page problem while requiring genuine musical thinking. Students hear what a complete track sounds like and have to fit into it — that is a listening and responding skill that transfers to every project for the rest of the year.

Mixing concept: gain staging ·

The stem tracks have varying levels, and students notice at once when their drums are too loud or too quiet. The rule: “Never push anything into the red. It is almost always better to turn other things down.”

Goals ·

  • Listen to a piece of music and identify where its sections change
  • Write two contrasting drum patterns that serve the music they accompany
  • Use transitions (fills, breakdowns, cuts, builds) to signal section changes
  • Apply gain staging so the drum track sits correctly in the overall mix
  • Experience what it means to respond musically to something that already exists

What is (and is not) in this kit

No stems, audio or Logic sessions are included in this kit. My own classroom stems come from other artists, so I cannot include them.

Where to get stems ·

1. Make your own (safest). Build a short backing track in Logic with no drums and bounce it to an audio file. The recipe on page 6 makes one Foundation stem and one Full Song stem in about 30 minutes. Students can never tell you made it.

2. A multitrack library that allows classroom use. I source new tracks from the multitrack library at cambridge-mt.com/ms/mtk. Read the terms for each track, leave out (or mute) the drum tracks, and bounce the rest into one stem.

3. Your own recordings: your school band, a colleague, or you, playing without a drummer.

4. Royalty-free music whose license allows you to edit it and share it with students, if you can get a version without drums.

Only use audio you have the right to use and share in class. Do not hand out stems pulled from commercial songs.

What makes a good stem ·

  • No drums. Everything else can stay: bass, chords, melody, vocals.
  • A steady tempo you know. Put the BPM in the file name so students can set Logic to match.
  • Clear sections. Energy that changes where the sections change: instruments in or out, louder or softer.
  • Starts on the downbeat of bar 1, with no pickup or silence before it, so the grid lines up.
  • Foundation: 16 measures, sections of 4 measures (A or B), as in my Day 1 post.
  • Full Song: 2 to 4 minutes with an intro, verses, choruses and ideally a bridge.
  • WAV or MP3, one file per stem.

Stems & GroovesMake your own stems in Logic
STEMS
Recipe · 30 min

Two original stems, C major, white keys only. Every chord is one of the I, IV, V and vi chords students meet in Unit 3. Change the chords, sounds or tempo to make more; keep the section lengths.

  1. New project, tempo 96 BPM, 4/4. Software Instrument tracks: a piano, a bass, a pad and a simple synth. No drum track.
  2. Turn on Cycle over 4 measures. With the Pencil in the piano roll, enter one chord per measure: three notes stacked, all white keys (the chord names below). The bass plays each chord’s root, low, as a whole note.
  3. Copy the 4-measure progression (Option-drag) to build each section; mute or delete instruments so each section has the instruments in the table. More instruments = more energy.
  4. Add arrangement markers for your own answer key. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section, WAV or MP3, starting at bar 1.
  5. Name the file with its tempo, for example “Foundation 1 - 96 BPM”, and post it in the right folder.

Stems & GroovesLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher

Everything students need in Logic for this unit, in one table. Demonstrate once (slides 9 and 15), then let students work.

Open a stemNew empty project. Set the tempo (double-click it in the control bar) to the stem’s BPM. Drag the audio file into the Tracks area at bar 1.
New drum trackTrack › New Software Instrument Track; in the Library (Y) choose any kit under Electronic Drum Kit. This loads Drum Machine Designer.
Arrangement markersTurn on Global Tracks (G). In the Arrangement track, click + to add a marker, drag its edges to fit the section, and rename it (A, B, Verse…).
Pattern regionControl-click (right-click) in the drum track at the start of a section and choose Create Pattern Region. Double-click it to open the Step Sequencer.
Place hitsClick steps to turn hits on. Each row is one sound; add rows for more sounds.
64-step modeIn the Step Sequencer, set the pattern length to 64 steps: four measures in one pattern, so a transition can sit in its last measure.
Copy acrossOption-drag a region to copy it, or select it and press Command-R to repeat it.
CyclePress C and drag the cycle area over a section to loop it while you work.
Volume and panOpen the Mixer (X). Click the disclosure triangle on the Drum Machine Designer track to show each drum’s own fader and pan knob.
ClippingThe peak display above each fader turns red when that track clips. Turn something down, then click the display to reset it.
ExportFile › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx project and choose Compress.
The order that works

Listen twice, markers, then drums. Groove first (kick, snare, closed hi-hat), then layers, then transitions, then the mix.

What to listen for at every transition
  • A transition that works feels inevitable in hindsight. If it surprises you in a good way, it will surprise the listener too.
  • The cut is the most powerful transition and the easiest to execute badly. Silence only works if the music earns it. Listen carefully before and after.
  • Only the measure before the change moves; the groove around it stays. Drums in songs tend to remain relatively consistent. That is what ties everything together and makes it sound like one song.

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 3

What this unit reinforces ·

This unit introduces the most important listening skill in the curriculum: responding to music that already exists. Students are not building in a vacuum — they have to hear what a professional recording is doing and fit into it. That constraint forces musical decisions that free-form building never does.

Foundation plants AABA thinking without naming it yet. Students are writing two contrasting patterns and placing them based on what they hear. This is form comprehension through action. Full Song introduces the concept of musical transitions — that the seam between sections is as compositionally significant as the sections themselves. This idea will echo through the EDM project in Unit 8 and every project after it.

The mixing concept introduced here is gain staging — the foundation of every mix decision students will make for the rest of the year. Frame it simply: nothing in the red, everything audible, the drums serve the stem. Students encounter this as a practical problem immediately because the stem tracks have varying levels and their drums often overwhelm them.

How this kit differs from my classroom version
  • My course plan describes an older version of this unit (Group 1: drums in AABA over a 16-bar stem; Group 2: full-song drums with volume, panning and reverb). This kit follows my newest project sheet, Foundation and Full Song. AABA lives in the 16-measure Foundation stem (sections of 4), and the reverb bus is an optional, ungraded extension (slide 17, page 17).
  • My stems and my example Logic session are not included (third-party audio). Pages 5–6 replace them. The Day 1 post’s “Check the example!” link becomes “Your teacher will play an example of each.”
  • My LMS is written as “your class page”. Padlet is kept: it is the board I use; any feedback board works.

TEACHER NOTES
2 of 3

Common student struggles ·

Skipping the listening step: The most common failure is students who open Drum Machine Designer before they have listened to their stem. Enforce the pre-listening requirement. Walk around Day 1 and ask every student to tell you where their sections are before they create a single pattern region. If they cannot answer, they are not ready to build.

A and B patterns that are barely different: Students will often change one hi-hat hit and call it a B section. Push back during working days with a simple test: play A, then immediately play B. Can you feel the difference without looking? If not, it needs more contrast. A useful prompt: if A is busy and driving, what would the opposite feel like?

Transitions that are just louder versions of the pattern: Students who are not yet confident with drum fills will often just double the hi-hat and call it a transition. Show examples of all four transition types on Day 3 before they start Full Song. The cut and breakdown are the most underused and often the most effective.

Drums that drown out the stem: Students push faders up instinctively. Address this with a direct demonstration: play a student project with drums too loud, then pull the drum bus down until the stem is clearly the lead. Ask: which version sounds more like a real song? The answer is usually obvious. Introduce the rule here: if you can clearly hear every drum hit and the stem is getting lost, your mix is backwards.

Not knowing where sections are: Some stems have less obvious section changes. For students who are struggling, add arrangement markers to their Logic file before they download it so the sections are pre-labeled. This is appropriate scaffolding at this stage of the year.

Suggested variations ·

More accessible: Pre-label all section changes in the stem file before distributing. Allow students to complete Foundation only and skip Full Song. Accept one transition type (fill only) rather than requiring two different types.

More advanced: Require all four transition types to be used at least once. Challenge students to write a third pattern (a C section) for any bridge or breakdown in the stem. Ask them to write a one-sentence rationale for every transition choice on their Padlet post.

Mixing concept: gain staging (Day 2) ·

Gain staging lands here because students encounter it naturally — the stem tracks have varying levels and students immediately notice when their drums are too loud or too quiet relative to the music. Introduce the concept directly: 'Never push anything into the red. It is almost always better to turn other things down than to turn one thing up.' This principle will echo through every project for the rest of the year.

Grading tips ·

  • Five categories, each scored 2–5, /25 (page 23). Foundations and Technical Skill come from the Foundation project; Full Song from the second; Gain Staging and Documentation from both.
  • Play A, then B, without looking: is the contrast obvious? Then play every section change of the full song: is there a deliberate transition at each, two types or more?
  • Watch the meters on the loudest section: any red? Is the stem still the lead?
  • Worked examples and answer keys: pages 25–27.

Stems & GroovesClassroom post
STUDENT
Day 1

Stems & Grooves — Day 1

Digital Music Production 1 · Unit 2

Today you are starting Part 1 of this project: Foundation. Follow this sequence in order.

Do not open Drum Machine Designer until you have completed Steps 1 and 2.

  1. 1
    Download · Open your class page and download one stem track from the Foundation folder. Open it in Logic Pro.
    Pick something that catches your ear. You are going to spend a lot of time with this track.
  2. 2
    Listen — twice · Play the full 16 measures from beginning to end. Do not touch anything. Then play it again.
    What is this music doing? Where does the energy change? Where does a new section feel like it starts?
  3. 3
    Map your sections · Add arrangement markers in Logic to label every section change you hear: A, B, A, B, etc. Each section should be 4 measures long.
    You cannot write drums for music you have not listened to. This step is not optional.
  4. 4
    Build your A pattern · Create a pattern region at an A section. Build a groove that fits the feel of that section. Start with kick, snare, and hi-hat — then make it yours.
    Does this groove fit the music? Play it alongside the stem and listen.
  5. 5
    Start your B pattern · Once your A pattern feels solid, create a new pattern region at a B section and start building something that contrasts with A.
    A and B should feel different. If you have to look at the screen to tell them apart, they are not different enough yet.
The four transition types — you will use these in Part 2:

Drum fill — a rhythmic variation in the last 1–2 measures that signals a change is coming

Breakdown — strip to one element (kick only, hi-hat only) before the new section hits

Cut — complete silence for 1–2 beats before the next section drops

Build — add elements progressively in the last measure to increase energy into the change

Your teacher will play an example of each.

The ruleNever push anything into the red. It is almost always better to turn other things down.

Gain staging — setting all track volumes so nothing clips (goes into the red) and everything is audible - the foundation of a clean mix.

Gain staging in 5 steps ·

  1. Start with the stem: it is the lead. Leave it where it sits.
  2. Bring the drums up until they support the stem: you can hear every drum, and the stem is still the star.
  3. Play the loudest section and watch the meters, including the master (Stereo Out).
  4. Anything in the red? Turn other things down. Do not turn one thing up.
  5. Balance the drums against each other: kick and snare first, hi-hats just above them.

Check your mix ·

Nothing clips: no red anywhere, in the loudest section too
Every drum is audible
Nothing in my drums is louder than the loudest part of the stem
It sounds like one song, not drums on top of a song
Going further (optional, not graded): a reverb bus

In the Mixer (X), click a Send slot on the drums and choose a bus. Logic adds an Aux channel strip: put ChromaVerb in its Audio FX slot, then turn the Send knob up slowly. A little shared reverb glues the drums to the stem. Unit 6 teaches buses properly.

Stems & Grooves

Digital Music Production 1 · Unit 2 · Structured Project · 6 Days · Drum Machine Designer + Stems

Overview ·

This project has two parts. In Foundation, you will write two contrasting drum patterns and place them correctly as sections change in a real professional recording. In Full Song, you will take those patterns and focus on the moments between sections — the transitions. How do you signal to a listener that something is about to change? By the end, you will have a complete drum track that feels like it belongs to the music, not just on top of it.

Goals ·

  • Listen to a piece of music and identify where its sections change
  • Write two contrasting drum patterns that serve the music they accompany
  • Use transitions (fills, breakdowns, cuts, builds) to signal section changes
  • Apply gain staging so the drum track sits correctly in the overall mix
  • Experience what it means to respond musically to something that already exists

Key Vocabulary ·

Stem tracka single isolated instrument or group from a real recording - in this project, everything except the drums
Sectiona distinct part of a song - verse, chorus, bridge, intro, outro - that has its own feel or energy
A section / B sectionthe two main drum patterns in this project: A for one type of section, B for another
Transitionthe moment between two sections where the drum pattern signals a change is coming
Drum filla rhythmic variation - usually one or two measures - that breaks the pattern and announces a new section
Breakdownstripping the beat down to one element (just kick, just hi-hat) before a new section hits

Steps / Process ·

PART 1 — FOUNDATION
  1. 1
    Listen before you build

    Download your stem track and listen to the whole thing twice before you open Drum Machine Designer. Count measures. Notice where the energy changes. Label sections in the arrangement markers: A, B, A, B, etc. You cannot write drums for music you have not listened to.

  2. 2
    Build your A pattern

    Create a pattern region at an A section. Build a drum groove that fits the feel of that section. At minimum: kick, snare, closed hi-hat. Aim for 3–5 total layers. Use 64-step mode if you want more control over individual measures.

  3. 3
    Build your B pattern

    Create a new, contrasting pattern region at a B section. It should feel different from A. If A is busy, B might be stripped back. If A is laid back, B might add energy. The listener should clearly feel the difference.

  4. 4
    Place and check

    Copy your patterns across all the correct sections. Play the full track back from beginning to end. Ask yourself: do my drums follow the music, or does the music follow my drums? It should always be the former.

PART 2 — FULL SONG
  1. 1
    Map every section change (Label the sections with letters (a,b,c…))

    Go through the full track and mark every section boundary in your arrangement markers. You should know exactly how many transitions exist before you write a single one.

  2. 2
    Choose a transition type for each boundary

    For every section change, decide which of the four transition types fits best:

    • Drum fill — a rhythmic variation in the last 1–2 measures that signals the change
    • Breakdown — strip to one element (kick only, hi-hat only) before the new section
    • Cut — complete silence for 1–2 beats before the next section drops
    • Build — add elements progressively in the last measure to increase energy
  3. 3
    Build each transition

    Work through the section changes one at a time. Use the 64-step sequencer to control exactly what happens in the transition measure. Listen after each one before moving to the next.

  4. 4
    Play it all the way through

    Listen to the complete track from start to finish. Every section change should feel intentional. If a transition does not add anything, it is not doing its job. Adjust until every seam in the music is earned.

  5. 5
    Mix and submit

    Adjust all volume levels so the drums sit correctly in the mix. Nothing should overpower the stems. Name the project correctly, export MP3 and .zip, and submit via your class page before your presentation.

Tips for Success ·

  • Listen first. Always. You cannot write drum parts for music you have not deeply listened to. Play it twice before you touch anything.
  • Your A and B patterns should be obviously different. If you have to look at the screen to tell them apart, they are not different enough.
  • A transition that works feels inevitable in hindsight. If it surprises you in a good way, it will surprise the listener too.
  • The cut is the most powerful transition and the easiest to execute badly. Silence only works if the music earns it. Listen carefully before and after.
  • When mixing, the stem is the star. Your drums should support it, not compete with it. If anything in your drum track is louder than the loudest element in the stem, pull it back.
  • You do not need to fill every measure with activity. Space is musical. A simple groove that serves the track is worth more than a complicated one that fights it.

SectionMeasuresEnergyGrooveTransition into the next section, and why
Intro (i)1–41none, or hi-hat onlyBuild in m. 4: the music grows from the pad into the verse
Verse (a)5–122ADrum fill in m. 12: announce the first chorus
Chorus (b)13–204BBreakdown in m. 20: back down to the smaller verse
Verse (a)21–282ADrum fill in m. 28: the same move as before: listeners expect it now
Chorus (b)29–364BCut in m. 36: the bridge is quieter: silence sets it up
Bridge (c)37–442A, half the kicksBuild in m. 44: a build before the big last chorus
Chorus (b)45–524BBreakdown in m. 52: thin out into the outro
Outro (o)53–561noneEnd: let the last chord ring

Energy = the number of instruments in the stem in that section. Types used: Drum fill, Breakdown, Cut, Build (4; the project needs at least two).

Check the math

4 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 8 + 4 = 56 measures. At 96 BPM one measure of 4/4 lasts 2.5 seconds, so the stem runs 56 × 2.5 = 140 seconds (2:20). Each transition sits in the last measure of its section: measures 4, 12, 20, 28, 36, 44, 52.

My rule of thumb

“A cut before a quiet verse, a build before a big chorus.” Here every build leads into more energy and the cut leads into less.

Grading the Full Song

Count the section changes in the student’s stem, then play each one: a deliberate transition at every change, two types or more, each fitting its moment.
