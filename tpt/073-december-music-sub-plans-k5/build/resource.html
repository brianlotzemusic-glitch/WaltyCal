Teacher Notes

December Music Sub Plans K–5 · 8 no-prep, no-tech days · winter holidays around the world

Why December matters

December is a month of many celebrations, and your students may celebrate some, all or none of them. In the Northern Hemisphere the winter solstice, around December 21, brings the least daylight of the year. Hanukkah lasts eight nights, Las Posadas nine, and Kwanzaa runs from December 26 to January 1. Christmas is on December 25, and many people count down to midnight on December 31. This bundle shares short, plain facts about each and keeps the music simple: clapping, patting and saying words. Nothing asks a student to take part in a religious practice.

What it is

Eight complete music lessons a substitute can teach with no tech, no instruments and no music background. Each day has a step-by-step plan with words to say and timed steps, and two worksheets: one for grades K–2 and one for grades 3–5.

The eight days

  1. Winter Lights Reader: reading about winter holidays.
  2. Snowflake Syllables: speech rhythm: clap the syllables.
  3. Winter Sound Maker: draw and design an instrument.
  4. Snowfall and Freeze: move to a tapped beat and freeze.
  5. Winter Word Games: winter vocabulary puzzles.
  6. Jobs Behind the Concert: music jobs.
  7. December Chant Book: compose a four-beat chant.
  8. Winter Word Bingo: review game day.

Use them in any order. The welcome sheet has a “Day #” column so the sub knows which day each class gets next.

Set up the sub tub (about 15 minutes, once)

  1. Fill in the welcome sheet (page 3).
  2. Print the plans, the quick start, the chant chart, the holidays page and the sub report; copy each worksheet for your largest class.
  3. Cut out the folder labels (page 45), put each day in its own folder, and add the answer keys.

Why subs can teach it

Rhythm work is speech rhythm: clap the syllables of words, with the claps printed as dots. Nobody has to read music or make music with an instrument. Games use only voices, hands and a pencil.

Standards

Supports relating music to varied contexts (MU:Cn11.0) and generating musical ideas (MU:Cr1.1), National Core Arts Standards, Music, grades K–5.

Tips for a calm class ·
  • Use the teacher’s attention signal from the welcome sheet (page 3), or clap two times and have the class clap back two.
  • “Strong voice, not shouting” keeps every game safe and happy.
  • If a game gets too loud, switch to a worksheet or to Quiet Countdown.
  • Let students take turns as the leader. Keep turns short.
  • Everyone celebrates differently. If students share how their family celebrates, thank them and move on.

Facts and dates
Plain facts for answering questions. Students may celebrate some, all or none of these. Every celebration is welcome in music class.
CelebrationWhenHow longA few facts
Winter solsticeDecember 21 or 22 (Northern Hemisphere)1 dayThe day with the least daylight and the longest night of the year in the Northern Hemisphere, because of the tilt of Earth. In the Southern Hemisphere it is the summer solstice.
HanukkahStarts on the 25th of Kislev (Hebrew calendar); the date on the usual calendar changes each year8 nightsRemembers the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem. A nine-branch hanukkiah (menorah) holds eight candles plus a helper candle; one more is lit each night. Dreidel is a spinning-top game.
Las PosadasUsually December 16 to 249 nightsMainly in Mexico and other Spanish-speaking communities. “Posada” means lodging or inn. Neighbors walk in a procession from house to house. Piñatas and a shared meal are part of it.
ChristmasDecember 25 (some churches use January 7)1 dayChristians celebrate the birth of Jesus. Many other people take part in some of the customs. Customs vary by place and family.
KwanzaaDecember 26 to January 17 daysCreated in 1966 by Maulana Karenga. The name comes from the Swahili phrase for “first fruits.” One of seven principles is honored each day, and a candle is lit in a kinara. Karamu is a community feast.
New Year’s EveDecember 31an eveningThe last day of the Gregorian calendar year. Many people count down to midnight. Because of time zones, some Pacific islands are among the first to welcome the new year. Customs vary widely.
The seven principles of Kwanzaa ·
Umoja (unity), Kujichagulia (self-determination), Ujima (collective work and responsibility), Ujamaa (cooperative economics), Nia (purpose), Kuumba (creativity), Imani (faith).
If a student asks something you do not know: say “That is a good question. Let’s ask your classroom teacher.” You never need to explain a belief; the days only share what is on the page.

In December, people around the world have winter celebrations. Many of them have candles or lights. In Hanukkah, families light one more candle each night for eight nights. Winter Around the WorldRead, answer and draw
DAY 1
Grades 3–5
NameDate
Read the passage. Underline one fact that was new to you. Then answer the questions in complete sentences.
December is full of celebrations, and many happen around the same time. The winter solstice falls on December 21 or 22 in the Northern Hemisphere. It is the day with the least daylight and the longest night of the year, because of the tilt of Earth. Hanukkah lasts eight nights. It begins on the 25th of Kislev in the Hebrew calendar, and families light one more candle each night. Las Posadas is a nine-night celebration, usually December 16 to 24, mainly in Mexico and in other Spanish-speaking communities. Neighbors walk from house to house in a procession, and there are piñatas and a shared meal. Christmas is celebrated on December 25 by Christians, and many other people take part in some of its customs. Kwanzaa runs from December 26 to January 1. It was created in 1966 by Maulana Karenga, and its name comes from a Swahili phrase meaning “first fruits.” Each day honors one of seven principles, and a candle is lit in a kinara. On December 31, New Year’s Eve, many people count down to midnight. Because of time zones, some Pacific islands are among the first places to welcome the new year.

Snowflake SyllablesDay 2 · Speech rhythm: clap the syllables · no tech, no prep
DAY 2
Sub plan
GoalStudents clap the syllables of winter words and use them to build a four-beat chant.
You needPencils, crayons, the worksheets and the chant chart (page 7). No music reading needed: every word shows its claps.
CopiesK–2: page 14
3–5: page 15
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Steady pat
    Pat your legs slowly and evenly. Say: “Copy me!” Students pat with you. Say: “This steady pulse is called the beat.”
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Clap the words
    Say a word, then say it again while clapping once for each syllable: snow (1 clap), can-dle (2 claps), Han-uk-kah (3 claps). Students echo. Try five or six words from page 7.
  3. 5 minStep 3
    Chart echoes
    Show page 7. Read a line out loud, one box per beat, while clapping the dots. One dot = ta, two dots in one box = ti-ti, shh = a silent beat (open hands). Students echo. K–2 use lines 1–4; 3–5 use all 8.
  4. 15 minStep 4
    Worksheet
    K–2 page 14, 3–5 page 15. Read Part A aloud and clap each word with the class before they color or write. Answers are on page 41.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Chant parade
    Volunteers read their chant aloud while the class pats a steady beat. Everyone claps at the end.
Extra time? Syllable Stomp from the game bank (page 6), with names of the students. Short class? Do only Part A and Part B.

Winter Sound MakerDay 3 · Draw and design an instrument · no tech, no prep
DAY 3
Sub plan
GoalStudents design an instrument for a winter celebration and explain how it makes sound.
You needPencils, crayons and the worksheets. A few classroom objects to look at: a pencil box, a cup, a book.
CopiesK–2: page 17
3–5: page 18
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    How sound is made
    Say: “Instruments make sound when something shakes, is tapped, blows air, or is plucked.” Show it: tap a book, shake a pencil box, tap a cup, blow gently over your hand.
  2. 5 minStep 2
    Sort the sounds
    Ask: Is a tap on the desk a shake, a tap, a blow or a pluck? Students answer with a body signal: shake hands, tap fingers, puff cheeks, pinch fingers.
  3. 20 minStep 3
    Design it
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 17, 3–5 page 18. Say: “Invent a sound maker for a winter celebration. It can be any shape and any material, even imaginary.” Read the directions aloud.
  4. 5 minStep 4
    Gallery walk
    Students stand beside their desks with their drawings. Walk the room and ask three students: How does it make its sound?
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Put it away
    Collect the sheets for the teacher. Ask: Which instrument would you like to see built?
Extra time? Mirror Moves or Quiet Countdown from the game bank (page 6). Short class? Skip the gallery walk.

Snowfall and FreezeDay 4 · Move to a tapped beat and freeze · no tech, no prep
DAY 4
Sub plan
GoalStudents move to a steady tap, change speed with the tap, and freeze when it stops.
You needPencils, the worksheets, a clear space to move, and the picture cards on page 22 (point at them or cut them out).
CopiesK–2: page 20
3–5: page 21
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Rules of the freeze
    Say: “When I tap, you move. When I stop, you freeze like ice. Keep your hands to yourself.” Practice once: tap a pencil on a desk for five seconds, stop, and wait for stillness.
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Snowfall
    Show the snowflake card on page 22. Tap slowly: students float like snowflakes. Tap fast: a snowstorm. Stop: freeze. Repeat with three different speeds.
  3. 10 minStep 3
    Picture cards
    Hold up (or point at) one card at a time. Students move as the card says while you tap. Use at least six cards. The words under each card tell you what to say.
  4. 10 minStep 4
    Reflection sheet
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 20, 3–5 page 21. Students sit and fill it in. Read the directions aloud.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Statue gallery
    Tap a few beats, stop, and let the class freeze in a favorite pose. Walk around and name two poses you notice.
Extra time? Mirror Moves from the game bank (page 6). Short class? Use only four cards.

Jobs Behind the ConcertRead, match and write
DAY 6
Grades 3–5
NameDate
Behind every winter concert are people whose jobs you may not see. A conductor, also called a music director, leads an orchestra or another group of musicians so that everyone stays together. An instrument maker builds or repairs instruments. A luthier, for example, makes and restores stringed instruments such as violins and guitars. A sound engineer balances and adjusts sounds so that a performance can be heard clearly. A radio host broadcasts music, news and weather, interviews guests, and often specializes in one kind of music. A music teacher helps students learn how music works and gives them chances to try it. All of these jobs need careful listening, and most of them need teamwork. When December comes, many schools and communities hold concerts, so people with these jobs may be especially busy.
