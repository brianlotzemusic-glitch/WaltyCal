Teacher Notes

Band and Orchestra Emergency Sub Plans · 5 no-instrument lessons · grades 6–12

What it is

Five complete lessons for the day you cannot be there. A substitute with no music background can run each one: every plan page has timed steps, words to say and a list of materials, and every lesson ends with a printed answer key. No lesson needs an instrument, a recording or a screen, so the plans work in a band room, an orchestra room, a mixed class or a regular classroom.

The five lessons

  1. Instrument Families and Care: instrument families, how sound is made, and caring for your instrument.
  2. Rhythm and Note-Value Math: note values, time signatures and beats as math.
  3. Composers and Music History: a reading about four composers, with comprehension questions.
  4. Practice Journal & Reflection: practice strategies, goal setting and honest reflection.
  5. Seating and Terminology Puzzle: where the sections sit, and the Italian terms in your music.

Each lesson fills one 40–45 minute class. Use them in any order; each stands alone. If you will be out several days, use the welcome sheet’s “Lesson #” column so the sub gives each class a new lesson.

Set up the sub folder (about 15 minutes, once)

  1. Fill in the welcome sheet (page 3).
  2. Print the plan and student pages for the lessons you want, one copy of each plan for the sub and a class set of each student page.
  3. Keep the answer keys and the sub report in the folder.

Levels

Grades 6–8: read aloud together. Grades 9–12: students can read silently and work alone.

About the music content

Rhythms are in 4/4 and use whole, half and quarter notes, paired eighth notes and quarter rests, engraved with a professional music font. Every engraved rhythm was read back from its drawing and checked against the data, and every answer in the keys was computed from the same data.

The history reading uses only well-documented facts, and the seating charts show the common layout (your ensemble may differ).

Standards

Supports reading and counting rhythm (MU:Pr4.2), evaluating and refining personal and ensemble performance (MU:Pr5.1) and connecting music to history and culture (MU:Cn11.0), National Core Arts Standards, Music (ensemble strand), grades 6–12.

Prints well in black and white. Nothing is dated or seasonal.

All lessons, readings, games, puzzles and art are original. No songs, lyrics or copyrighted characters are used.

Instrument FamiliesReading · The four instrument families
LESSON 1
Reading page
NameDate
Instruments are grouped into families by how they make sound. A concert band is mostly woodwinds, brass and percussion. An orchestra has all four families, and its string section is the largest.
Woodwinds ·

A flute player blows air across an edge. A clarinet or saxophone player makes a single reed vibrate. An oboe or bassoon player uses a double reed, two reeds tied together that vibrate against each other. Many woodwinds were once made of wood, but not all are today. A saxophone is made of brass, yet it is a woodwind because it uses a reed.

Brass ·

Brass players buzz their lips inside a cup-shaped mouthpiece, and the tubing makes the buzz louder and gives it a color. A trumpet, French horn or tuba player changes notes by pressing valves; a trombone player moves a slide. A tuba is the largest and lowest of the common brass instruments.

Percussion ·

Percussion instruments are struck, shaken or scraped. Some play a definite pitch, such as the timpani (kettledrums, which can be tuned) and the xylophone. Others, such as the snare drum and cymbals, make sounds with no clear pitch.

Strings ·

Violins, violas, cellos and double basses make sound when a bow is drawn across the strings or when the strings are plucked. They are built in the same shape but in different sizes: the smaller the instrument, the higher it sounds. The violin is the smallest and highest; the double bass is the largest and lowest.

How to look after your instrument
  1. Keep your instrument in its case, latched, when you are not playing. Never leave it on a chair or on the floor.
  2. Swab or wipe out a woodwind after you play, to remove moisture.
  3. Keep reeds in a reed case or holder. Use your own mouthpiece and do not share it.
  4. Put rosin on the bow hair, not on the strings, and loosen the bow hair when you put the bow away. Do not touch the bow hair with your fingers.
  5. Use valve oil or slide grease only as your director shows you, and never force a stuck valve or slide.
  6. Keep instruments away from heat and cold. A parked car can get far too hot or too cold.
Did you know? Most bands and orchestras tune to a single note. Instruments in different families sound different because their sound starts differently: a buzz, a reed, an edge, a string or a strike.

Rhythm and Note-Value MathLesson 2 · Note values, time signatures and beats as math
LESSON 2
Sub plan · no instruments
GoalStudents add note values, find missing beats and compare rhythms in 4/4, and use beats per minute to work out time.
You needPencils and the three worksheets (page 9, page 10, page 11). Students may clap on their desks or knees; no instruments.
Timeabout 43 min
for band or orchestra
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Clap a steady beat
    Say: “Music is math you can hear. Pat your knees in a slow, steady beat with me: 1, 2, 3, 4.” Count four steady beats aloud, then ask the class to clap on 1 only and rest on 2, 3 and 4. Repeat twice.
  2. 8 minStep 2
    Learn the note values
    Hand out the first worksheet (page 9) and read the box at the top aloud. Say: “A whole note lasts four beats. A half note lasts two. A quarter note lasts one. Two eighth notes joined by a beam together last one beat.” Clap each one: a whole note is one clap, then wait four beats.
  3. 15 minStep 3
    Worksheet A
    Students complete the first worksheet alone. Remind them: in 4/4 every full measure adds up to 4 beats. Rests count too, even though they are silent.
  4. 10 minStep 4
    Worksheets B and C
    Hand out the next two worksheets (page 10, page 11). Part C on the first looks like a puzzle: compare what is on each side, just as in math. Parts F and G on the last are a good stretch for fast finishers.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Check
    Read the answers from page 25. Students check each other’s papers. Ask: Which question was the trickiest?
Extra time? Students write their own 4-beat rhythms in part G of the last worksheet, or use the early finishers page (page 23). Short class? Do steps 1 to 3 with the first worksheet only.

ComposersReading · Four composers
LESSON 3
Reading page
NameDate
Bands and orchestras play music from many centuries. Here are four composers whose work you may meet. The years are the years each composer lived.
Johann Sebastian Bach
1685–1750
Baroque era (roughly 1600–1750)

Bach was born in Eisenach, Germany, into a family of musicians. He worked as an organist and a music director, and from 1723 until he died he led the music at St. Thomas Church in Leipzig. He wrote six Brandenburg Concertos, which he sent to a nobleman in 1721, and a huge amount of music for organ, keyboard, voices and orchestra.

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart
1756–1791
Classical era (roughly 1750–1820)

Mozart was born in Salzburg, in today’s Austria. As a child he played and wrote music, and he toured Europe as a young performer. He lived in Vienna as an adult, wrote more than 600 works, including symphonies, concertos and operas, and died there at age 35.

Ludwig van Beethoven
1770–1827
Between the Classical and Romantic eras

Beethoven was born in Bonn, Germany, and spent most of his adult life in Vienna. In his late twenties he began to lose his hearing, but he kept composing. He wrote nine symphonies; his Symphony No. 9, first performed in Vienna in 1824, ends with a chorus and four solo singers.

John Philip Sousa
1854–1932
Wind-band composer

Sousa was born in Washington, D.C. He led the U.S. Marine Band from 1880 to 1892, then formed his own Sousa Band. He wrote more than 100 marches and is called “The March King.” His march “The Stars and Stripes Forever” (1896) became the national march of the United States by act of Congress in 1987. The sousaphone, a tuba that wraps around the player, is named for him.

Era dates are rough: historians draw the lines in slightly different places.

Practice SmarterReading · Five practice strategies
LESSON 4
Reading + match
NameDate
Practice is not the same as playing through your music. Good practice is short, focused and has a plan. Here are five strategies that many band and orchestra directors teach.
  1. Isolate the hard spot. Do not play the whole piece again and again. Find the one or two measures that go wrong and repeat just those, slowly.
  2. Use a steady beat. A metronome or a steady foot tap shows you when you rush or drag. Start slow and speed up a little at a time.
  3. Work in small chunks. Break a piece into short sections of one to four measures. Make one chunk right, then join it to the next.
  4. Mark your music. Circle trouble spots in pencil, and write in counts or reminders, so you know where to start tomorrow.
  5. Set one goal. Before you start, decide what you will fix today. A clear goal keeps your practice from drifting.
