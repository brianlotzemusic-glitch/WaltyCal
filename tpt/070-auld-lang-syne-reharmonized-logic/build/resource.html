What this is ·

A 3-class-period reharmonizing project for high school music technology. Every student enters the traditional tune of “Auld Lang Syne” in Logic Pro from a note chart, puts new chords under it in a pop, ballad or EDM style, builds a groove, arranges 40 bars, mixes, presents and reflects. It is an original Hudson Beat project, and the melody is in the public domain.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look at the tempo table, the note charts and the chord tables (pages 11–15) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–25), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–6), Class 2 (slides 7–9) and Class 3 (slides 10–14). Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

One melody, three styles, your chords.

What students make ·

A 40-bar track in Logic Pro: a 4-bar intro, the 16-bar tune twice and a 4-bar outro. The melody is entered from the note chart in C major or G major. The chords come from the pop, ballad or EDM table, or are the student’s own, and every chord holds the melody note on its downbeat. Students name each chord by Roman numeral and by letter, build a groove, mix, and hand in a bounced file and the zipped .logicx project, plus their chord table and a reflection, then play the track and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

This project teaches what chords do. Students learn that a melody does not belong to one chord, that a chord that holds the melody’s downbeat note will fit, how Roman numerals name chords in any key, how pop, ballad and EDM styles use different chords and grooves, and how to turn a tempo into bars and seconds, all in Logic Pro.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Enter a 16-bar public-domain melody into Logic Pro’s Piano Roll from a note chart, in C major or G major, with the right pitches and lengths
  • Choose chords for each bar and check that the melody note on every downbeat is a chord tone, naming each chord by Roman numeral and by letter in the key
  • Build a pop, ballad or EDM groove around the chords, with a bass line and, for EDM, a sidechain pump from the kick
  • Use one bar = 240 ÷ BPM seconds to plan a 40-bar arrangement in four sections, then mix, bounce and reflect on the choices

About the tune ·

“Auld Lang Syne” is a Scottish song that people around the world sing as one year turns into the next. Its title is Scots for “old long since”, often put into English as “days gone by”, and its chorus line is often rendered “for the sake of old times”. It is a song of remembrance and friendship: it asks us to keep old friends and good memories close. This project borrows that tune and asks a music-technology question: what happens to one melody when the chords underneath it change?

The poem was written by Robert Burns in 1788. The tune students use is a traditional Scottish melody; its first documented use with the words is from 1799. Both are far older than the 1930 cutoff, so the tune is in the public domain worldwide. The poem’s words are not printed in this kit.

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav files, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The tempo table, note charts and chord tables, printed (pages 11–15), or shown on the projector.
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–25).
  • A MIDI keyboard is optional: the notes can be drawn in the Piano Roll. Nobody has to read music, only the note chart.

Before Class 1 ·

Read the note chart (pages 12–13) and enter its first four bars in Logic so you know how long it takes.
Open Logic Pro on a student computer and check which tools your version has: Alchemy, Drum Machine Designer, Step Sequencer, Chord Track, Session Players, Compressor side chain (see page 8).
Check that you can add a marker and draw volume automation.
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Watch for melodies entered an octave off, chords that do not hold the downbeat note, Roman numerals copied from the wrong key, a bass line that fights the kick and a melody buried under the chords. A good listening day: play each student’s track without saying the style and let the class name it and the chords they hear. Ask which chord changed the mood most. This kit names no songs or artists, and the melody is public domain.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: is that downbeat note in the chord? What would a different chord do to the mood?
  • Say the theme plainly: the song is about remembering friends and good times, and the project is about how chords change a tune’s feeling.
  • A student who is stuck can enter the melody once and use the pop table in C major: that already works.

The tune ·

The traditional Scottish tune is in the public domain worldwide. The poem’s words are not printed, so students work from the melody alone. If a student wants to use another tune, check its first publication date: in 2026 it must be 1930 or earlier.

Keep the volume moderate

A loud kick and a pumping pad can be tiring in headphones. Tell students to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

What this project reinforces ·

This project teaches what chords do. Students learn that a melody does not belong to one chord, that a chord that holds the melody’s downbeat note will fit, how Roman numerals name chords in any key, how pop, ballad and EDM styles use different chords and grooves, and how to turn a tempo into bars and seconds, all in Logic Pro.

Common student struggles ·

  • The chords sound wrong: play the melody’s downbeat note alone, then the chord. If the note is not in the chord, choose another chord from the table or move one note of the chord (page 15).
  • The Roman numerals and letter names do not match: write the key’s scale on a staff of seven boxes, count the chord’s root in it, and use the chord key on page 15.
  • The melody is entered in the wrong octave: Logic calls middle C “C3” by default and the chart calls it C4, so subtract one from the chart’s octave number (page 12).
  • The EDM pump does not move: the Compressor must have the kick track chosen as its side chain, and the kick must play on every beat (page 16).

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Use the pop table in C major, one chord a bar, with the groove card as given (pages 14 and 16). Skip the second pass’s octave doubling, and ask for 2 questions at the presentation.

More Advanced: Write your own chords for bars 9 to 16 and justify each downbeat, add a key change into the second pass (move it up two semitones with Transpose in the Region inspector), or make a second version in another style and compare the two (page 17).

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Chords that clash: the chord tables (pages 14–15) list the melody’s downbeat note, the chord tones and any passing notes for every bar.
  • Numerals and names: the chord key on page 15 gives every chord in C and in G with its Roman numeral and notes.
  • The octave: the note chart (pages 12–13) lists every note in both keys, and the Logic quick reference (page 8) explains Logic’s octave names.
  • The EDM pump: the groove cards (page 16) draw the kick and the ducking, and the quick reference (page 8) names the side chain setting.

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 22). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Melody & Chart (rubric row 1): play the melody alone and compare it with the chart, bar by bar (pages 12–13).
  • Chords & Harmony (rubric row 2): for each bar, check that the downbeat note is in the chord, and that the numeral and letter name are right (page 15).
  • Style & Groove (rubric row 3): listen for the style’s drum pattern and a bass line that follows the chords (page 16).
  • Arrangement & Mix (rubric row 4): check the markers against the map, listen for the melody over the chords and check the meter for clipping (pages 17 and 18).
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): the chord table, the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 25) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 25): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

Notes / observations ·

A good listening day: play each student’s track without saying the style and let the class name it and the chords they hear. Ask which chord changed the mood most. This kit names no songs or artists, and the melody is public domain.

SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a reharmonizing project is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 and 2 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

Project and tempoFile › New…, then a Software Instrument track to start. Set the project tempo in the control bar to the tempo you chose and keep the time signature at 4/4. Smart Tempo, if your version has it, can detect the tempo of a recording; for this project set the tempo yourself so that the bar arithmetic on the tempo table holds.
Enter the melodyOpen the Piano Roll and draw the notes with the pencil tool on a grid of 1/16 or 1/8. Pitch names run down the left. Logic names middle C “C3” by default; the chart calls it C4, so subtract one from the chart’s octave number. The pickup is one note on beat 4 of the bar before bar 1. Transpose in the Region inspector moves a region by semitones: 7 semitones up turns C major into G major.
ChordsPut the chord notes on a second Software Instrument track in the Piano Roll (a chord is three or four notes at the same start). The Chord Track, if your version has it, can show the chord symbols above the tracks; it only labels what you choose, and you must still check each downbeat against the chart.
Session PlayersKeyboard Player, Bass Player and Drummer, if your version has them, can play chords and a groove for you. Choose the chords yourself first and set the player to follow them; you still need to check the melody against every chord. Session Players are optional.
DrumsUse Drum Machine Designer with a kick, a snare or clap and a hat on three pads. In the Piano Roll set the grid to 1/16 and click in the hits from your groove card, or draw them with the Step Sequencer if your version has it. Lower the velocity of the hats a little so the kick and the snare stand out.
Bass and chords soundsUse Alchemy for a pad, a pluck or an EDM bass (browse its presets by name), or a Retro Synth patch. Keep the bass to one note at a time in a low octave, on each chord’s root. Roll the chords on keys or a pad, and keep them below the melody’s register.
Sidechain for EDMOn the pad or the bass track, add a Compressor and, in its Side Chain section, choose the kick track as the source, if your version’s Compressor has a side chain menu. Lower the threshold until the pad ducks on every kick. If your Compressor has no side chain, draw volume automation that dips on every beat and recovers before the next.
Sections with markersShow the Marker global track (the Global Tracks controls or the View menu). Add a marker with the plus (+) button in that track, or with a Create Marker key command if your version has one, then drag or type it to the bars on your arrangement map. Name them Intro, Pass 1, Pass 2 and Outro.
Mixer, automation and bouncePress X for the Mixer and set levels and pan. Press A to show automation. Keep the melody clear above the chords, and keep the master out of the red. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV, for the whole track. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

1 · A weak plan

I will put nice chords under the tune and make it sound sad and slow.

It names no key, no tempo, no chords and no checks.

2 · A clear plan

My track is a ballad in G major at 72 BPM in 4/4. One bar lasts 240 ÷ 72 = 3.333 seconds, so one 16-bar pass lasts 53.33 seconds and the 40-bar track lasts 133.33 seconds. The intro is bars 1 to 4, Pass 1 is bars 5 to 20, Pass 2 is bars 21 to 36 and the outro is bars 37 to 40. The melody’s pickup is on beat 4 of bar 4. I use the ballad table: Imaj7, Vsus4, vi7 and IVmaj7, then ii7 and iii7 in the second half. Every downbeat note is a chord tone: the A in bar 2 is the fifth of Dsus4, and the E in bar 4 is the third of Cmaj7.

Why these chords suit a ballad

Added 7ths (Gmaj7, Em7, Cmaj7) soften each chord, and the suspended chords (Dsus4 and D7sus4) lean toward the chord that follows. The harmony changes once a bar, so the melody’s long notes have room, and the move from Cmaj7 back to Gmaj7 in bars 4 and 5 sounds like coming home.

Try it yourself

Swap the ballad chord in bar 10 (Am7, ii7) for the pop chord D in G major (the V chord). Is the melody note on beat 1 still a chord tone? Then say how the mood changes.

6 · The mix

The pad and the piano stay under the melody; the bass holds one root a bar and is cut below 40 Hz with a Channel EQ, if I have it; the hat is very quiet; in Pass 2 a high pad doubles the melody an octave up; the reverb on the last chord rings out over bars 39 and 40.

7 · Three feedback questions (invented)

1. Which chord changed the mood the most, and why? 2. Did the melody always sound like it belonged? Which bar felt strange, if any? 3. Did the second pass feel bigger than the first? What made it feel that way?

  • Pass 2 starts at bar 21, 66.67 seconds in, with the melody doubled an octave up, so the class can hear the same notes grow.

Step 1: choose style and tempo. In 4/4 there are 4 beats in a bar. These formulas turn a tempo into seconds and bars. Every number below is computed, so you can check your own arithmetic against it. The track is 40 bars: an intro of 4, two 16-bar passes of the tune and an outro of 4.

The formulas (4/4)

Seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM

Seconds per bar = 240 ÷ BPM

One pass (16 bars) = 16 × 240 ÷ BPM seconds; the track (40 bars) = 40 × 240 ÷ BPM

A section starts at (bars gone by) × 240 ÷ BPM seconds

Example: 100 BPM: 240 ÷ 100 = 2.4 s per bar, so 40 bars = 96 s.

Rounding rules

Seconds per beat and per bar to 3 decimals, and seconds for passes, section starts and the track to 2; a 5 rounds up. So 240 ÷ 72 = 3.333 seconds per bar, and the 4 bars before Pass 1 at 72 BPM last 13.33 seconds (13.333… rounds down). Every start time is rounded from its exact value.

Each row gives the melody note on beat 1 in C major and in G major, the chord in both keys, its Roman numeral, the chord tones, and which part of the chord the beat 1 note is. How to check a chord

1. Find the melody note on beat 1 (page 12 or 13). 2. List the chord’s notes (the key above). 3. Is the note in the list? If yes, the chord fits. If not, change the chord. 4. Name it: count the root in the key’s scale (the numeral), then write the letter name.

Reading the names

A capital numeral is a major chord and lower case is minor. 7 adds the seventh, maj7 the major seventh, sus4 swaps the third for the fourth and add9 adds the ninth, a step above the root.

Step 4: build the groove. Each card is a one-bar drum pattern (16 steps, 4 to a beat; shaded steps are the beats). Click it into a Drum Machine Designer track (or draw it in the Piano Roll with the grid on 1/16) and repeat it for the whole track. The side stick or soft snare and the clap sit on the snare lane.

Pop groove · 96 to 120 BPM

Drums. Kick on beat 1, the “and” of 2 and beat 3; snare on beats 2 and 4; hat on every eighth note.

Bass and chords. Bass plays the chord root on beat 1, then repeats it on the eighths. Keys or guitar strum each chord once or twice a bar.

Try. Make the last bar of every four a little busier, such as an extra snare hit on the “and” of 4.

Ballad groove · 60 to 80 BPM

Drums. Kick on beats 1 and 3; one soft snare or side stick on beat 3; hat or shaker on every quarter note.

Bass and chords. Bass holds each chord root for the whole bar. Piano or pad plays the chord as an arpeggio or with a slow roll.

Try. Leave space: if the melody has a long note, let the chords hold underneath it.

EDM groove · 120 to 128 BPM

Drums. Four on the floor: a kick on every beat; clap on beats 2 and 4; hat on the off-beats (the “and” of each beat).

Bass and chords. Bass plays the chord root on the off-beats, so it sits between the kicks. A synth pad or pluck plays each chord and gets a sidechain pump from the kick.

Try. Sidechain: the kick turns the pad and bass down for an instant on every beat, then they swell back, so the track seems to breathe.

EDM: the sidechain pump

Add a Compressor to the pad or the bass and choose the kick as its side chain (see page 8). Lower the threshold until the pad ducks on every kick. No side chain menu? Draw volume automation that dips on each beat and recovers before the next.

Auld Lang Syne Reharmonized

Music Technology | Reharmonizing Project | 3 class periods

Overview ·

Re-harmonize a public-domain melody in Logic Pro. Enter the traditional tune of “Auld Lang Syne” from a note chart (16 bars in 4/4 with a one-beat pickup, in C major or G major), then choose new chords under it in one of three styles: a pop loop, a slow ballad with added 7ths and suspended chords, or an EDM track with four on the floor and a sidechain pump. Every chord must hold the melody note on its downbeat. Students name each chord by Roman numeral and letter, build a groove, arrange 40 bars in four sections and mix. Nobody needs to compose a melody.

Goals ·

  • Enter a 16-bar public-domain melody into Logic Pro’s Piano Roll from a note chart, in C major or G major, with the right pitches and lengths
  • Choose chords for each bar and check that the melody note on every downbeat is a chord tone, naming each chord by Roman numeral and by letter in the key
  • Build a pop, ballad or EDM groove around the chords, with a bass line and, for EDM, a sidechain pump from the kick
  • Use one bar = 240 ÷ BPM seconds to plan a 40-bar arrangement in four sections, then mix, bounce and reflect on the choices

Key Vocabulary ·

ReharmonizeTo keep a melody the same and write new chords under it, so that the same notes sound happy, sad, calm or driving
Chord toneA note that belongs to the chord that is playing. A melody note on a strong beat usually should be one; a note that is not is a passing tone
Roman numeralA number for a chord’s place in the key: I is the chord on the first note of the scale, V on the fifth. Capitals are major chords and lower case are minor
SidechainUsing one track to control another. A kick that turns the pad and bass down on every beat makes the pumping EDM sound
Suspended chord (sus)A chord whose third is replaced by the fourth (a sus4 chord), so it sounds open and leans toward the next chord

Tips for Success ·

  • Play the melody over a chord before you commit to it. If it sounds wrong, look at the melody note on the downbeat first: is it in the chord?
  • Change chords less often for a calmer feel and more often for a busier feel. One chord a bar is a good start.
  • Let the melody be the loudest part in the first pass. Let the arrangement grow in the second.
  • Save a new version of your project at the end of each class, so you can always go back.
