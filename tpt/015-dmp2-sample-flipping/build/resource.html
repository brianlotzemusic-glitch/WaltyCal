What this is ·

A 7-class-period project for high school music technology. Each student takes one sample and flips it into an original track in Logic Pro: they chop, pitch, reverse and rearrange it in Quick Sampler, program their own drums, layer instruments and mix. It is an open-ended project from my Digital Music Production 2 course (focus: sampling and Quick Sampler), and the projects in that course can be taught in any order.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 6) and my teacher notes (pages 7–8).
  2. Build your cleared sample set (page 4): no samples are included.
  3. Print the student pages (pages 12–20), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–6), Class 2 (slides 7–10), Class 3 (slide 11), Class 4 (slide 12), Class 6 (slides 13–15) and Class 7 (slides 16–17). Class 5 is a working day with no new slide. Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

In one sentence

Students pick a cleared sample, chop and pitch it in Quick Sampler, build their own drums around it, layer, mix and present.

What students make ·

One Logic Pro project: an original track built around one sample. The sample is chopped, pitched, reversed or stretched in Quick Sampler, set against drum patterns the student programmed, layered with instruments that fit the style, and mixed with EQ and plug-ins. They hand in a bounced .mp3 or .wav, the zipped .logicx project, and a Padlet post with three feedback questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Builds Quick Sampler proficiency, drum programming, and creative transformation skills. Natural entry point for discussing sampling ethics and copyright.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): developing and refining original work (MU:Cr2.1, MU:Cr3.1), presenting it (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Learn to use Quick Sampler for creative sample manipulation
  • Understand the art and ethics of sample-based production
  • Develop drum programming skills with self-created patterns
  • Build a complete, original track from a single source sample

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student (a current version; Quick Sampler and the Step Sequencer need Logic Pro 10.5 or later), and headphones.
  • A set of cleared samples: short audio files (a few seconds each) your students may use. Nothing is included. Build it from the options on the right.
  • A place to share the set with your students and to submit an .mp3 or .wav and a zipped .logicx project (your class page).
  • A feedback board for the three questions (my sheets use Padlet).
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 12–20).

Before Class 1 ·

Build and test the sample set; write each sample’s source and license in a list for yourself.
Name each file with its key and tempo where you know them (for example Warm Pad_keyAmin_90bpm).
Make a 30-second flip of one sample for slide 2.
Print the student pages; set up the submission spot and the feedback board.

Record your own. ·

My class set is 50 short vocal loops and phrases, and it is not part of this kit. A sample is someone’s recording, so use sounds you have the right to hand to students:

  • Record your own. You, your colleagues or willing students sing, play or tap short phrases into Logic. The performers agree to class use.
  • Public domain and CC0 sounds. Sound libraries label these. Save the page that shows the license.
  • Licensed libraries. If your school or department owns a sample library, read its license: does it allow classroom use and student remixes?
  • Logic’s own sounds. Apple Loops that come with Logic are meant for making music; read Apple’s license before you hand a set out.
A cleared sample is…
  • One whose source and license you can name
  • Short (2 to 12 seconds) and clean, with one clear idea to chop
  • Free of any famous recording or song
Posting online?

A class assignment kept on your class page is one thing; posting a flip publicly is another. Check each license first, and credit the source if it asks.

Sample FlippingSampling, ethics and copyright
TEACHER
Class 1

My teacher notes call this project “a natural entry point for discussing sampling ethics and copyright.” This page gives you the facts to start with and questions for Class 1. It is a discussion starter, not legal advice.

Four ideas for students ·

  • Two kinds of ownership. A recorded song generally carries two sets of rights: the recording itself and the music and words underneath it. Using a piece of someone’s record in a new track usually means asking about both.
  • Clearing a sample means getting permission, often for a fee or a share of the earnings.
  • Some sounds are free to use. Recordings you make yourself, public-domain sounds and CC0 sounds need no permission. Some licenses ask you to credit the source.
  • Transforming is not the same as clearing. Chopping and pitching a sample makes it sound different; it does not make it yours.

Why this class uses cleared samples ·

Every student can flip freely because the set was chosen so that nobody has to ask permission. The Sample log (page 12) asks where the sample came from, so the habit sticks.

Discussion questions ·

  1. Why would a producer build a track from someone else’s sound instead of recording a new one?
  2. The artist made the original recording. What does the producer owe them?
  3. When does a flip become a new work, and when is it still the original?
  4. Would you feel differently if the sample was a stranger’s voice, a friend’s, or yours?
  5. A producer flips a sample so much that nobody could recognize it. Do they still need permission?
  6. What would you want if someone sampled your music?
Class 1 in two minutes

Pick two questions, take answers aloud, then tell students: our samples are cleared, and I would like you to write down where yours came from.

Is it cleared? A five-minute sort ·

Read each source aloud and have students vote: use it, or ask first?

TEACHER NOTES

What this project reinforces ·

Builds Quick Sampler proficiency, drum programming, and creative transformation skills. Natural entry point for discussing sampling ethics and copyright.

Common student struggles ·

  • Students use the sample without transforming it — push them to actually chop, pitch, or manipulate.
  • Quick Sampler modes are confusing. Demo Classic, One Shot, and Slice modes early.
  • Drums feel generic or disconnected from the sample.
  • Students give up quickly if the sample doesn’t immediately sound cool.

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Pre-slice a few samples and provide them as ready-to-play Quick Sampler instruments.

More Advanced: Require 2 samples in the same track, or multiple Quick Sampler instruments from a single source.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Using the sample without transforming it: the Chop map (page 13) makes students plan their cuts and their flip, and the worked example (page 11) shows chop, reverse and pitch.
  • Confusing modes: slide 8 and the Logic reference (page 10).
  • Generic drums: slide 10 and the drum plan (page 14), which asks where the chop lands.
  • Giving up early: the Sample log (page 12) has students try several samples first.

Sample FlippingLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Everything students need in Logic for this project, in one table. Demonstrate the three modes (slide 8) and the drums (slide 10), then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions.

Make a Quick SamplerTrack › New Software Instrument Track. In the Library (Y), click the instrument slot and choose Quick Sampler (under Sampler).
Load a sampleDrag the audio file from Finder onto the Quick Sampler’s waveform area. The sample appears as a waveform; play the keys to hear it.
Classic modeThe sample plays across the keyboard like a sampler: the key you play changes the pitch, and the pitch changes the speed. Good for melodies and bass.
One Shot modeA key plays the whole sample from start to end, however long you hold it. Good for hits, stabs and single sounds.
Slice modeThe sample is cut into slices and each slice goes to its own key. Move or add slice markers on the waveform so each cut starts at the beginning of a sound.
Reverse and pitchUse the Reverse button for the sample or a slice, and the pitch control to move it up or down in semitones (12 = one octave).
Record the chopsRecord-enable the track and play the slices from a keyboard, or draw notes in the Piano Roll. Quantize if you want them tight.
DrumsNew Software Instrument Track, Electronic Drum Kit (Drum Machine Designer). Control-click in the track, Create Pattern Region, double-click to open the Step Sequencer.
EQ, pan, bounceMixer (X): double-click the EQ display for Channel EQ; turn the Pan knob. File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

Sample Flipping

Digital Music Production | Open-Ended Project | 7 class periods

Overview ·

Take one of the provided samples and flip it into something entirely your own. Using Logic’s Quick Sampler, you’ll chop, stretch, pitch, and manipulate the sample to build an original track around it. This is how some of the biggest producers in hip-hop, R&B, and electronic music work.

Goals ·

  • Learn to use Quick Sampler for creative sample manipulation
  • Understand the art and ethics of sample-based production
  • Develop drum programming skills with self-created patterns
  • Build a complete, original track from a single source sample

Key Vocabulary ·

SampleA recorded piece of audio used as raw material in a new composition
Quick SamplerA Logic Pro instrument that lets you import, slice, and play back audio samples
FlippingTransforming a sample into something new through chopping, pitching, or reversing
ChoppingCutting a sample into smaller pieces to rearrange and replay

  2. 2

  3. 3
    Build your beat — Program your own drum pattern that fits the style (page 14)
  4. 4
    Layer and arrange — Add bass, chords, melodies, and additional elements
  5. 5

  6. 6

  7. 7

Tips for Success ·

  • Spend real time experimenting in Quick Sampler before building the full track.
  • Try reversing, pitching down, or isolating just one small section of the sample.
  • Your drums set the tone. Match the drum style to whatever genre your flip is heading toward.
  • Listen to classic sample-based producers (J Dilla, Kanye, Madlib) for inspiration.
