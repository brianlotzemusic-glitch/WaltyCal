Teacher Notes

March Music Sub Plans K–5 · 8 no-prep, no-tech days · Music In Our Schools Month, women composers, Irish music, spring

Why March matters

March is a busy month for music teachers. Every March, schools in the United States celebrate Music In Our Schools Month, a program led by the National Association for Music Education that began as a one-day event in New York in 1973 and became a month-long celebration in 1985. March is also Women’s History Month, first named by Congress in 1987, so this bundle shares short facts about three women composers: Hildegard of Bingen, Clara Schumann and Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel. St. Patrick’s Day on March 17 is a chance to meet the instruments of Irish traditional music (the harp, fiddle, tin whistle and bodhrán), and the spring equinox, usually March 19 to 21, brings the sounds of birds, rain and frogs. The music stays simple: clapping, patting and saying words. Facts are kept short and secular, and the days stay with culture and history.

What it is

Eight complete music lessons a substitute can teach with no tech, no instruments and no music background. Each day has a step-by-step plan with words to say and timed steps, and two worksheets: one for grades K–2 and one for grades 3–5.

The eight days

  1. Composer Spotlight Reader: reading about March music and women composers.
  2. Clap the Names: speech rhythm: clap the syllables.
  3. Music Month Poster Studio: design a poster for Music In Our Schools Month.
  4. Spring Freeze: move to a tapped beat and freeze.
  5. Spring Word Puzzles: March vocabulary puzzles.
  6. Music Careers Spotlight: music jobs.
  7. Spring Chant Book: compose a four-beat chant.
  8. March Bingo Bash: review game day.

Use them in any order. The welcome sheet has a “Day #” column so the sub knows which day each class gets next.

Set up the sub tub (about 15 minutes, once)

  1. Fill in the welcome sheet (page 3).
  2. Print the plans, the quick start, the word-rhythm chart, the facts page and the sub report; copy each worksheet for your largest class.
  3. Cut out the folder labels (page 45), put each day in its own folder, and add the answer keys.

Why subs can teach it

Rhythm work is speech rhythm: clap the syllables of words, with the claps printed as dots. Nobody has to read music or make music with an instrument. Games use only voices, hands and a pencil.

Standards

Supports relating music to varied contexts (MU:Cn11.0) and generating musical ideas (MU:Cr1.1), National Core Arts Standards, Music, grades K–5.

No-prep games for the last five minutes of a class (or any gap). They need only voices, hands and a pencil.

Echo BeatSay a short phrase, like “bird, bloom, bird, bloom.” The class repeats it exactly, then once softly.1

Spring Word RoundEach student says one spring word, like “rain,” while the class pats a steady beat.any
Tulip GrowStudents crouch small. Count slowly from 1 to 5 while they grow tall like a tulip, then everyone freezes.4
Tips for a calm class ·
  • Use the teacher’s attention signal from the welcome sheet (page 3), or clap two times and have the class clap back two.
  • “Strong voice, not shouting” keeps every game safe and happy.
  • If a game gets too loud, switch to a worksheet or to Whisper Countdown.
  • Let students take turns as the leader. Keep turns short.
  • Students may know other musicians or celebrate other traditions. Thank them and move on.
Not sure which game to pick? Use Whisper Countdown to settle a class, Name Clap to wake it up, and Spring Word Round to finish with something positive.

March Music Sub PlansMarch facts at a glance
FOR THE SUB
Facts and dates
Plain facts for answering questions. Students may know more, and may celebrate other traditions. Thank them for sharing.
TopicWhenA few facts
Music In Our Schools MonthEvery MarchA program led by the National Association for Music Education (NAfME). It began as a one-day event in New York on March 14, 1973, and became a month-long celebration in 1985. Schools use it to show why music education matters.
Women’s History MonthEvery MarchObserved in the United States each March. It grew from a local celebration in Santa Rosa, California, in 1978, and in 1987 Congress first named March Women’s History Month.
Hildegard of BingenAbout 1098 – September 17, 1179Born near Bingen, in what is now Germany. A nun who led her community, she wrote chants, and she also wrote books about nature and medicine. About seventy of her musical works still exist.
Clara SchumannSeptember 13, 1819 – May 20, 1896Born in Leipzig, Germany. Pianist, composer and teacher. She gave her first big concert at the Gewandhaus in Leipzig at age nine and performed for about sixty years. Her husband, Robert Schumann, was a composer too.
Fanny Mendelssohn HenselNovember 14, 1805 – May 14, 1847Born in Hamburg, Germany. Pianist and composer who wrote more than 450 pieces. She ran a series of Sunday concerts at her family’s home in Berlin. Most of her music was not published while she lived. Her younger brother Felix was a famous composer too.
St. Patrick’s DayMarch 17A day when many people celebrate Irish culture, with parades, music and the color green. March 17 is the traditional date linked to Saint Patrick, a figure from Ireland’s early history. U.S. parades are hundreds of years old.
Irish traditional musicInstrumentsThe harp is Ireland’s official emblem and appears on its coins. Other traditional instruments are the fiddle (the violin, used in folk music), the tin whistle (a small pipe with finger holes), the bodhrán (a drum struck with a stick) and the uilleann pipes (bagpipes pumped with the elbow). The shamrock, a small three-leaf clover, is a symbol often linked with Ireland.
Spring equinoxMarch 19, 20 or 21The day in March when day and night are nearly equal and spring begins in the Northern Hemisphere. In 2027 it falls on March 20.
Sounds of springMarch and AprilBirds return and call at dawn. Small frogs called spring peepers call at dusk once the nights warm up. Rain taps on roofs and windows.
If a student asks something you do not know: say “That is a good question. Let’s ask your classroom teacher.” You never need to explain more than what is on the page. The days share these facts and never ask students to take part in a religious practice.
  • Stay with the facts on this page and in the readings. The days do not quote speeches or lyrics.
  • Treat every question kindly. If a student wants to say more, thank them and return to the plan.

Read the passage. Underline one fact that was new to you. Then answer the questions in complete sentences.
Every March, schools across the United States celebrate Music In Our Schools Month, a program led by the National Association for Music Education. It began as a one-day event in New York in 1973 and grew into a month-long celebration in 1985. March is also Women’s History Month, which Congress first named in 1987. Three women composers show how music can change history. Hildegard of Bingen, born in Germany around 1098, led a community of nuns and wrote chants, and about seventy of her pieces still exist. Clara Schumann, born in Leipzig in 1819, was a pianist who gave her first big concert at age nine and performed for about sixty years. Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel, born in Hamburg in 1805, wrote more than 450 pieces and ran Sunday concerts at her home in Berlin. March 17 is St. Patrick’s Day, when many people celebrate Irish culture. Traditional Irish music uses instruments such as the harp, the fiddle, the tin whistle and the bodhrán, a drum struck with a stick. The harp even appears on Ireland’s coins. Finally, spring begins in March, around the equinox on March 19, 20 or 21.

Clap the NamesDay 2 · Speech rhythm: clap the syllables · no tech, no prep
DAY 2
Sub plan
GoalStudents clap the syllables of composers’ names and spring words and use them to build a four-beat chant.
You needPencils, crayons, the worksheets and the word-rhythm chart (page 7). No music reading needed: every word shows its claps.
CopiesK–2: page 14
3–5: page 15
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Steady pat
    Pat your legs slowly and evenly, like a clock. Say: “Copy me!” Students pat with you. Say: “This steady pulse is called the beat.”
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Clap the words
    Say a word, then say it again while clapping once for each syllable: spring (1 clap), Cla-ra (2 claps), Hil-de-gard (3 claps). Students echo. Try five or six words from page 7.
  3. 5 minStep 3
    Chart echoes
    Show page 7. Read a line out loud, one box per beat, while clapping the dots. One dot = ta, two dots in one box = ti-ti, shh = a silent beat (open hands). Students echo. K–2 use lines 1–4; 3–5 use all 8.
  4. 15 minStep 4
    Worksheet
    K–2 page 14, 3–5 page 15. Read Part A aloud and clap each word with the class before they color or write. Answers are on page 41.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Chant parade
    Volunteers read their chant aloud while the class pats a steady beat. Everyone claps at the end.
Extra time? Name Clap from the quick games (page 6), with the names of students. Short class? Do only Part A and Part B.

Spring FreezeDay 4 · Move to a tapped beat and freeze · no tech, no prep
DAY 4
Sub plan
GoalStudents move to a steady tap, change speed with the tap, and freeze when it stops.
You needPencils, the worksheets, a clear space to move, and the picture cards on page 22 (point at them or cut them out).
CopiesK–2: page 20
3–5: page 21
about 40 min
Step by step ·Italic lines are words you can say.
  1. 5 minStep 1
    Rules of the freeze
    Say: “When I tap, you move. When I stop, you freeze like a statue. Keep your hands to yourself.” Practice once: tap a pencil on a desk for five seconds, stop, and wait for stillness.
  2. 10 minStep 2
    Weather walk
    Say: “Spring weather changes fast. A light rain is slow and soft. A big shower is fast and loud.” Show the raindrop card on page 22. Tap slowly: students walk slowly. Tap fast: they jog in place. Stop: freeze. Repeat with three different speeds.
  3. 10 minStep 3
    Picture cards
    Hold up (or point at) one card at a time. Students move as the card says while you tap. Use at least six cards. The words under each card tell you what to say.
  4. 10 minStep 4
    Reflection sheet
    Hand out the worksheet: K–2 page 20, 3–5 page 21. Students sit and fill it in. Read the directions aloud.
  5. 5 minStep 5
    Statue garden
    Tap a few beats, stop, and let the class freeze in a favorite pose. Walk around and name two poses you notice.
Extra time? Tulip Grow from the quick games (page 6). Short class? Use only four cards.

Jobs and What They DoRead, match and write
DAY 6
Grades 3–5
NameDate
Every piece of music starts somewhere, and many different jobs help it reach listeners. A composer writes and arranges original music. Hildegard of Bingen and Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel were both composers. A conductor, also called a music director, leads an orchestra or another group so that the musicians stay together. An instrument maker builds and repairs instruments, such as harps and fiddles, by working with wood, strings and tools. A pianist performs on the piano for audiences and spends many hours practicing; Clara Schumann was a famous pianist who performed for decades. A music teacher helps students learn how music works and gives them chances to try it, which is what Music In Our Schools Month celebrates. All of these jobs need careful listening, and most of them need teamwork. Imagine that you are starting a new music school. Which job from this list would you most want to hire first, and why?
