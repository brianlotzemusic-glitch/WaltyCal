The one thing to do on Class 1

Make sure every student leaves with a tempo, a clean hum of 2 to 4 seconds and a first set of slices that each play from a key, so that Class 2 is all pattern and pitch. Check each hum against the take log before they go.

With two or four periods

Two classes: Class 1 is the plan, the hum, the slices and the pattern; Class 2 is the pitch, the groove, the arrangement, the mix and short presentations. Four classes: keep Classes 1 and 2, then spend Class 3 on the groove, the bass and the arrangement, and Class 4 on the mix, presentations and reflection.

Step 4: play the chop pattern. A chop pattern is a set of hits that repeats. Each lane is one slice and each column is one 16th-note step, so a bar has 16 steps and the 4-bar pattern has 64. The model pattern below uses all 8 slices. Play your own, then draw it on the blank grid.

The model pattern, bar by bar
BarHitsSlices usedBar starts at (s, 120 BPM)

Hits per bar: 6, 6, 6 and 8, 26 in all. One slice sounds at a time, so no step holds two hits.

Reading the grid

A hit on grid step s of bar b starts at ((b − 1) × 16 + (s − 1)) × 15 ÷ BPM seconds. At 120 BPM, bar 2, grid step 4 is slice 2: (16 + 3) × 0.125 = 2.375 s.

At 120 BPM a slice lasts 3 steps. Slice 1 on grid step 1 and the next hit on grid step 4 gives it all 3 steps. A hit only 2 steps later cuts it short, if your sampler plays one slice at a time.

Step 5: pitch and flip. Every key on a keyboard is one semitone, and 12 semitones make an octave. Raising a slice by n semitones multiplies its frequency by 2^(n ÷ 12), so +12 doubles it (× 2.000), +7 gives × 1.498 and −5 gives × 0.749. Try a few and listen.

200 Hz is an example number for a low hum, not a measurement of your voice. Duration = 0.375 ÷ ratio. Intervals add and ratios multiply: +7 then +5 is +12, and 1.4983 × 1.3348 = 2.0000. To find n from a ratio, n = 12 × log2(ratio): 12 × log2(2) = 12.

Reverse and stretch (if your version has them)

Reverse: look for a reverse option in the Quick Sampler window. A reversed slice makes a good lead-in to the next section.

Time-stretch: many samplers play a recording faster when you raise its pitch, so the slice gets shorter. Some tools (Flex Time, for example) can change the length without changing the pitch, and the other way round. Try one and listen for a watery or rough sound.
