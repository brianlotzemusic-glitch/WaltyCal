What this is ·

A 3-class-period vocal chop project for high school music technology. Every student records one short hum of their own voice, slices it into 8 or 16 pieces in Quick Sampler (if their version has it), plays the pieces in a 4-bar pattern on a 16-step grid, pitches some by semitones and arranges a hook with drums and a bass. Students plan the timing with simple arithmetic, present the result and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look at the timing page, the slice map, the step grid and the pitch table (pages 11–15) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–25), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–8), Class 2 (slides 9–11) and Class 3 (slides 12–17). The slide about the three classes carries the badge for all three. Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

OVERVIEW
Vocal chop project
The project in one line

Winter Vocal Chop · Hum, slices, pattern and hook · Logic Pro
One hum of your own voice, eight slices, a hook that comes from them.

What students make ·

A hook of 8 or 16 bars, built in Logic Pro from one hummed phrase of 2 to 4 seconds. The hum is sliced into 8 or 16 pieces, each slice is put on a key, and a 4-bar chop pattern plays them on a 16-step grid. Some slices are pitched by semitones and one is reversed, and a simple drum groove and bass turn the pattern into an arrangement with an intro, a hook, a drop and an outro. Students hand in a bounced file and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play the hook and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a sample-based hook is made. Students learn to record a clean source, to cut it into slices of a planned length, to play the slices on a 16-step grid, to change pitch by semitones with an exact frequency ratio, and to arrange the result into sections, all in Logic Pro.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Plan a hook with timing math: one bar of 4/4 = 240 ÷ BPM seconds and one 16th-note step = 15 ÷ BPM seconds (0.125 s at 120 BPM)
  • Record a clean hum in your own voice and cut it into 8 or 16 slices, using slice length = hum length ÷ number of slices
  • Play slices from keys in a 4-bar, 16-step chop pattern and read it as a step grid with a count of hits per bar
  • Pitch slices by semitones with the ratio 2^(n ÷ 12) (+12 is × 2.000, +7 is × 1.498, −5 is × 0.749) and arrange an 8- or 16-bar hook

About the theme ·

A vocal chop is a short piece of a recorded voice that is cut out, played from a key or pad and repeated in a pattern, so that a hum becomes a hook. This project starts from a winter morning: a hum inspired by wind at the window, boots on snow or a kettle starting to sing. Students record their own voice, so there is no existing song, melody or recording anywhere in the project.

The skills here are the ones every sample-based track needs: record a clean source, cut it into pieces, play the pieces on a grid, change their pitch by an exact number of semitones and arrange them into sections. They carry over to beat making, remixing and sound design.

Winter Vocal ChopSetup and what to watch
SETUP
Teacher

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones.
  • A microphone, shared if you must, and a pop filter if you have one. A quiet corner with soft surfaces beats a loud room. A laptop’s built-in microphone will do for practice.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav files, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The timing page, slice map, step grid and pitch table, printed (pages 11–15), or shown on the projector.
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–25).

Before Class 1 ·

Read the timing page (page 11) so you can answer ‘how long is one step at 100 BPM?’ and ‘how long is a slice of a 3-second hum?’
Test a microphone in Logic Pro on one computer, and hum into it while watching the meter (page 12).
Open Logic Pro on a student computer and check which tools your version has: Quick Sampler and its slice mode, the Piano Roll, a Drummer or drum kit (see page 8).
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Watch for a hum that clips or is much too quiet, a slice marker in the middle of a sound, hits that sit off the grid, and a hook so crowded that the chops disappear. A good listening day: play each hook and ask the class two questions. Could you tell the hook is a voice? Which slice did you hear most often? If a slice sounds wrong, find it on the slice map together. This kit names no songs or artists.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: which step is this hit on? Which slice is this key?
  • Use only the student’s own hum, plus the drums and bass sounds that come with Logic. No existing songs, videos or recordings.
  • A student who is stuck can build one slice, one pitched copy of it and a drum groove: those already make a hook.

The voice and copyright ·

Students record their OWN voice. They do not sample songs, videos, games or any commercial recording, and they do not use a classmate’s voice without that classmate’s permission. A student who prefers not to be recorded can ask a willing classmate to hum, with permission.

Keep the volume moderate

Students listen to a short sound over and over in headphones. Tell them to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

The one thing to do on Class 1

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how a sample-based hook is made. Students learn to record a clean source, to cut it into slices of a planned length, to play the slices on a 16-step grid, to change pitch by semitones with an exact frequency ratio, and to arrange the result into sections, all in Logic Pro.

Common student struggles ·

  • The slices click or cut off a sound: move the slice marker to the start of a sound (a transient), not into the middle of one, and listen again (page 13).
  • The chops feel off the beat: check the project tempo, set the snap or grid to 16th notes and count the steps on the step grid (pages 11 and 14).
  • A pitched slice sounds too short or too fast: in many samplers a higher pitch plays the recording faster, so its slice is shorter. The pitch table shows the new length (page 15).
  • The hook sounds crowded: take layers away until the chops are clear again, and bring them back one at a time (page 17).

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Keep the tempo at 120 BPM, where a step lasts 0.125 seconds and a 3-second phrase cut into 8 slices gives slices of exactly 3 steps (page 11); slice into 8 pieces only, use the model pattern on page 14 and an 8-bar arrangement.

More Advanced: Slice into 16 pieces, write a different 4-bar pattern for the drop and pitch three or more slices (page 15). Or build a 16-bar arrangement with a second hum recorded at a different pitch.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Slices that click: the slice map page (page 13) shows the lines on a waveform and explains transient, equal and manual slices.
  • Hits that feel off the beat: the timing page (page 11) gives the length of a step at six tempos, and the step grid (page 14) shows every hit.
  • Pitch: the pitch table (page 15) gives each semitone offset with its ratio, and the worked example (pages 9–10) shows a full set of choices.
  • A crowded hook: the arrangement page (page 17) shows which of five layers plays in each bar.

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 22). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Hum & Recording (rubric row 1): listen for noise and clipping, check the take log and ask for the hum’s length in seconds (page 12).
  • Slices & Pattern (rubric row 2): check the slice map against the number of slices and compare the step grid with what plays (pages 13 and 14).
  • Pitch & Sound Design (rubric row 3): ask the student to read the ratio for each pitched slice from the pitch table (page 15).
  • Groove & Arrangement (rubric row 4): play the whole hook and find the intro, hook, drop and outro on the arrangement map (pages 16 and 17).
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): the timing page, the slice map, the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 25) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 25): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

Notes / observations ·

A good listening day: play each hook and ask the class two questions. Could you tell the hook is a voice? Which slice did you hear most often? If a slice sounds wrong, find it on the slice map together. This kit names no songs or artists.

Winter Vocal ChopLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a Winter Vocal Chop is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 and 2 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

Project and tempoFile › New…, then an Audio track to record the hum. Set the project tempo in the control bar to the BPM on your timing page and keep the time signature at 4/4. Set the snap or grid value to 16th notes (if your version shows one) so that every hit lands on a step.
Record the humOn the Audio track, choose the microphone or interface as the input and click the record-enable button (R). Watch the track’s level meter while you hum your loudest moment: set the gain on the interface, not the fader, so the peaks stay well below 0 dBFS, around −12 to −6 as a starting point. Re-record a take that clips.
Load it in Quick SamplerAdd a Software Instrument track and choose Quick Sampler (if your version has it). Drop your hum’s audio region onto the Quick Sampler window, or use Logic’s command that converts an audio region to a sampler instrument (if your version has it; the Logic Pro User Guide describes it). Trim the start and end markers to the hum.
Slice modeIn the Quick Sampler window, look for a Slice mode (if your version has it). It can place slice markers at transients, in equal divisions or by hand (manual), and it may offer a way to set how many slices (for transients, a sensitivity control). Choose 8 or 16, then listen and move any marker that cuts a sound in half.
Slices on keysSlices usually play from neighboring keys in order, starting from a key you can see in the plug-in. Play each key and write down which slice it plays and where slice 1 starts. The note names and octave numbers differ between versions, so label your own start key.
Play the patternRecord the pattern from a keyboard or pads, or draw notes in the Piano Roll with the grid set to 16th notes, 16 steps to a bar, for 4 bars. Quantize to 16th notes if hits are late. The Step Sequencer (if your version has one) can also play a pattern on a grid.
Pitch and reversePitch a slice by playing a different key, by transposing its notes by semitones in the Piano Roll, or with a tune or pitch control in Quick Sampler (if your version has one); these use semitones. Look for a reverse option in the Quick Sampler window (if your version has it). Flex Time and similar tools (if your version has them) can stretch audio without changing its pitch.
Drums and bassAdd a drum part with a Session Player such as the Drummer, or a Drum Machine Designer kit, or a loop from the Loop Browser (each if your version has it), and play the groove on page 16. For the bass, choose a bass sound from the Software Instrument library and play one low note per bar.
Mixer and bouncePress X for the Mixer and set levels and pan. Keep the master peak below 0 dBFS with room to spare. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV, for the whole hook. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

EXAMPLE

An invented plan on paper: a hook at 120 BPM from a 3-second hum. No audio is included. Student projects will differ. Page 1 of 2: the plan, the slices and the pattern.

1 · A weak plan

I will hum something and chop it up and add drums, and it will sound cool.

It names no tempo, no hum length, no slices, no pattern and no sections.

2 · A clear plan

My hook is at 120 BPM in 4/4. One bar lasts 240 ÷ 120 = 2 seconds and one 16th-note step lasts 15 ÷ 120 = 0.125 seconds. My hum is 3 seconds long, so 8 equal slices are 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 seconds each, which is exactly 3 steps. My 4-bar chop pattern uses all 8 slices and has 6, 6, 6 and 8 hits, 26 in all, and lasts 4 × 2 = 8 seconds. I pitch slice 1 up 12 semitones (× 2.000), slice 3 up 7 (× 1.498) and slice 5 down 5 (× 0.749). My arrangement is 16 bars = 32 seconds: an intro in bars 1 to 4, the hook in bars 5 to 8, the drop in bars 9 to 12 and an outro in bars 13 to 16.

Ratio = 2^(n ÷ 12): +12 gives 2.000, +7 gives 1.498 and −5 gives 0.749. If the sampler plays a pitched slice faster or slower, its length becomes 0.375 ÷ ratio: 0.1875 s at +12 and 0.5006 s at −5. One slice is also reversed, at the end of bar 4.

6 · The groove and the bass ·

One bar of drums has the kick on grid steps 1, 9, 11 (3 hits), the snare on 5 and 13 (2 hits) and the hat on every other step (8 hits): 13 hits. The bass plays one note per bar at 0, 0, −5, −7 semitones from the root.

7 · The 16-bar arrangement ·

16 bars × 2 s = 32 s. The intro starts at 0 s, the hook at 8 s, the drop at 16 s and the outro at 24 s.

8 · Three feedback questions (invented)

1. Which slice did you hear most often, and did you notice when it changed? 2. Could you tell the pitched chops from the plain ones? 3. Did the drop feel different from the hook, and why?

9 · Moments to point out in class
  • The same 3 seconds of hum make every sound in the hook, so the slice map explains everything the listener hears.
  • At 120 BPM a step is exactly 0.125 s and a slice exactly 3 steps, so the class can clap the grid and hear each hit land.

Step 1: plan the hook. In 4/4 there are 4 beats in a bar, and each beat holds four 16th-note steps, so a bar has 16 steps. These formulas turn a tempo into seconds. Every number below is computed, so you can check your own arithmetic against it.

The formulas (4/4)

Seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM

Seconds per 16th-note step = 15 ÷ BPM

Seconds per bar = 240 ÷ BPM = 16 steps

A hit on grid step s of bar b is at ((b − 1) × 16 + (s − 1)) × 15 ÷ BPM seconds

Slice length = hum length ÷ number of slices

Example: 120 BPM: 15 ÷ 120 = 0.125 s per step, and 240 ÷ 120 = 2 s per bar.

Rounding rules

Seconds per beat, bar and section to 3 decimals and seconds per step to 4; a 5 rounds up. So 15 ÷ 128 = 0.1172 seconds (the exact value is 0.1171875). Slice lengths in the second table are exact.

Step 2: record the hum. You need one short phrase, 2 to 4 seconds long, hummed in your own voice. Think of a winter morning and hum what you hear in your head: wind at the window, boots on snow, a kettle starting to sing. No words, and no tune from a song you know. The hum is the only sound the project starts with, so a clean take makes every later step easier.

The room and the microphone
I am in a quiet room with soft surfaces, and the heating, fans and phones are off or far away.
I use headphones, so nothing leaks back into the microphone.
I keep the same distance from the microphone for every take. About 10 to 30 cm is this kit’s starting range: I try two distances and listen.
If there is a pop filter, it sits between my mouth and the microphone.
Levels
I set the input gain on the interface while I hum my loudest moment.
The loudest part peaks around −12 to −6 dBFS on the track meter and never reaches 0 dBFS, the top of the scale.
A take that clips is deleted and recorded again; turning it down later does not fix it.
A quieter recording is safe: I can raise it later.
The hum
It is one phrase of 2 to 4 seconds. I time it on the take log below.
It has no words and no existing melody. It is my own idea.
It has a clear start and a few clear changes in loudness or pitch, so it slices well.
I hum it more than once and keep the cleanest take.
Copyright note: your own voice only

Record your OWN voice. Do not sample or chop a commercial recording: no songs, no clips from videos or games, no audio from the internet. Chopping someone else’s recording is not the same as making your own. A classmate’s voice is fine only if they say yes.

If you would rather not record yourself, ask a willing classmate to hum, with permission, and credit them on your reflection page.

Step 4: play the chop pattern. A chop pattern is a set of hits that repeats. Each lane is one slice and each column is one 16th-note step, so a bar has 16 steps and the 4-bar pattern has 64. The model pattern below uses all 8 slices. Play your own, then draw it on the blank grid.

Hits per bar: 6, 6, 6 and 8, 26 in all. One slice sounds at a time, so no step holds two hits.

Reading the grid

A hit on grid step s of bar b starts at ((b − 1) × 16 + (s − 1)) × 15 ÷ BPM seconds. At 120 BPM, bar 2, grid step 4 is slice 2: (16 + 3) × 0.125 = 2.375 s.

At 120 BPM a slice lasts 3 steps. Slice 1 on grid step 1 and the next hit on grid step 4 gives it all 3 steps. A hit only 2 steps later cuts it short, if your sampler plays one slice at a time.

Step 5: pitch and flip. Every key on a keyboard is one semitone, and 12 semitones make an octave. Raising a slice by n semitones multiplies its frequency by 2^(n ÷ 12), so +12 doubles it (× 2.000), +7 gives × 1.498 and −5 gives × 0.749. Try a few and listen.

Reverse and stretch (if your version has them)

Reverse: look for a reverse option in the Quick Sampler window. A reversed slice makes a good lead-in to the next section.

Time-stretch: many samplers play a recording faster when you raise its pitch, so the slice gets shorter. Some tools (Flex Time, for example) can change the length without changing the pitch, and the other way round. Try one and listen for a watery or rough sound.

Winter Vocal Chop

Music Technology | Vocal Chop Project | 3 class periods

Overview ·

Make a hook in Logic Pro from one short hummed phrase. Students record about 2 to 4 seconds of their own voice (a hum, no words, no existing melody), load it in Quick Sampler (if their version has it), and slice it into 8 or 16 pieces. They put the slices on keys, play a 4-bar chop pattern on a 16-step grid, pitch some slices by semitones, try a reversed slice, and add a simple drum groove and bass. The result is an 8- or 16-bar arrangement with an intro, a hook, a drop and an outro. Timing math drives every choice, and nobody needs to read music.

Goals ·

  • Plan a hook with timing math: one bar of 4/4 = 240 ÷ BPM seconds and one 16th-note step = 15 ÷ BPM seconds (0.125 s at 120 BPM)
  • Record a clean hum in your own voice and cut it into 8 or 16 slices, using slice length = hum length ÷ number of slices
  • Play slices from keys in a 4-bar, 16-step chop pattern and read it as a step grid with a count of hits per bar
  • Pitch slices by semitones with the ratio 2^(n ÷ 12) (+12 is × 2.000, +7 is × 1.498, −5 is × 0.749) and arrange an 8- or 16-bar hook

Key Vocabulary ·

ChopTo cut a recording into short pieces and play them in a new order or pitch
SliceOne piece of a recording between two slice markers; each slice can be played from its own key
TransientThe sudden start of a sound, such as a hard consonant or a drum hit

PatternA group of hits placed on steps that repeats; here it is 4 bars long
HookA short musical idea that is easy to remember and comes back during a piece
StepOne 16th-note slot on the grid; 16 steps make one bar of 4/4

Tips for Success ·

  • Hum on one comfortable note or a gentle slide. A steady hum slices into pieces that sound good in any order.
  • Record two or three takes and keep the cleanest. The recording is the only sound you have, so make it good before you cut it.
  • Start with a simple pattern and add one change at a time. Listen after each change.
  • Save a new version of your project at the end of each class, so you can always go back.
