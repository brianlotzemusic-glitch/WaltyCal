What this is ·

A 7-class-period sound design project for high school music technology. Each student records at least five everyday sounds, trims and normalizes them in Logic, turns them into playable instruments with Quick Sampler, and composes a 30–60 second piece with at least three layers (percussion, bass, texture), built entirely from those found sounds. It is an open-ended project from my Digital Music Production 2 course (focus: sound design), and the projects in that course can be taught in any order.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and my teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Practice the Class 1 demo: record, trim, normalize and play one sound in Quick Sampler (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 12–21), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–7), Class 2 (slides 8–10), Class 3 (slides 11–12), Class 4 (slides 13–14), Class 6 (slides 15–17) and Class 7 (slides 18–19). Class 5 is a working day with no new slide. Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

No audio included

No audio, video, songs or examples are included. Students record every sound themselves, so everything in their pieces is original. For the Class 1 hook, you make a short everyday sound yourself.

Make it fit your class ·

  • Easier or harder: my variations on page 6: a bank of sounds to use alongside their own (page 4 lists ten to record), or all-original sources and a 90-second piece.
  • Feedback board: my sheets use Padlet for the three questions; any class board works.
  • Recording: a phone or the microphone at each station; students can also record at home.
  • Grading: my rubric, /25, the average of the student’s and your score (page 18).

Found Sound · Open-Ended · Sound Design
Record everyday sounds and transform them into instruments using Quick Sampler. Compose entirely from found sounds.

What students make ·

One 30–60 second piece composed entirely from found sounds: at least five original recordings, trimmed and normalized, turned into playable instruments in Quick Sampler, arranged in at least three distinct layers, with effects and automation. They hand in a bounced .mp3 or .wav and the zipped .logicx project, plus a Padlet post with three feedback questions, then play the piece and present it.

What this project reinforces ·

Builds recording skills, Quick Sampler proficiency, and sound design thinking. Teaches students that music can come from anywhere, not just preset libraries.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Record, edit, and manipulate real-world sounds as creative tools
  • Use Quick Sampler to build playable instruments from audio recordings
  • Design at least three distinct sound layers (percussion, bass, texture)
  • Apply effects, automation, and mixing to create a polished composition

SETUP

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones. Speakers help for the final listen.
  • A way to record sound: the microphone at each station, a phone, or another recorder students already use.
  • A way to move phone recordings to the Mac (AirDrop, a cable, or your class page).
  • A place to submit an .mp3 or .wav and a zipped .logicx project (your class page).
  • A feedback board for the three questions (my sheets use Padlet).
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 12–21).

Before Class 1 ·

Practice the demo on slide 6: record, trim, normalize and play one sound in Quick Sampler.
Check that a student computer can record and play back through headphones.
Decide where students record: at their desks, around the room, on a short walk with permission, or at home.
Print the student pages; set up the submission spot and the feedback board.

Clean recordings (my first struggle) ·

Kit suggestions, not part of my sheets
  • Get close to the sound and keep the phone still.
  • Record in a quiet spot and keep wind and breath off the microphone.
  • Record three takes of each sound and keep the best one.
  • Record sounds, not songs: music playing from a speaker is someone else’s work.
  • Ask permission before recording a person, and keep recordings school-appropriate.

For my Easier variation ·

My variation is a set of pre-recorded found sounds students can use beside their own. No audio is in this kit, so make a small bank in ten minutes with your own phone. Twelve sounds to try (a kit suggestion):

a pen click · a zipper · a drawer · a stapler · a door closing · a water bottle · a chair scrape · keys · a book closing · a locker · a faucet · footsteps

Keep the volume moderate

Sliced and pitched-down sounds can be much louder than they look. Tell students to start with the headphones low.

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Builds recording skills, Quick Sampler proficiency, and sound design thinking. Teaches students that music can come from anywhere, not just preset libraries.

Common student struggles ·

  • Students record low-quality audio. Show them how to get clean recordings from their phone or the condenser mic at their station.
  • Quick Sampler setup is confusing at first. Demo all three modes (Classic, One Shot, Slice) with a real sound.
  • Students create instruments but don’t compose with them — they just audition sounds endlessly.
  • The 30–60 second length feels limiting. Remind them that density matters more than length.

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Provide a set of pre-recorded found sounds that students can use alongside their own.

More Advanced: Require all source sounds to be original recordings (no pre-made sources). Extend to 90 seconds with a clear compositional arc.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Low-quality audio: the recording tips on the setup page (page 4) and the Sound log (page 12).
  • Confusing Quick Sampler: the modes page (page 10), slide 9 and the Instrument builder (page 13).
  • Auditioning forever: the Layer plan (page 14) gives every instrument a job.
  • Length: the worked example (page 11) shows 45 seconds with three layers.

How to grade ·

  • My rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 18). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.

Suggestions from this kit, not part of my rubric:

  • Creativity & Originality: ask what each source sound was, then play the instrument made from it. My tip 4 is the test: if the audience can’t tell where the sounds came from, the transformation worked.
  • Technical Skill: look at the Sound log (page 12) and the Instrument builder (page 13). Were at least 5 sounds recorded, trimmed and normalized, and which Quick Sampler modes were used?
  • Composition & Structure: use the Layer plan (page 14). Are there at least 3 distinct layers, and does the piece change over 30–60 seconds?
  • Sound Design & Mix Quality: listen with headphones and ask which layer is leading in each section. Levels and EQ should be a choice.
  • Documentation & Presentation: the Sound log, Instrument builder, Layer plan, Padlet questions and the Reflection page (page 21) are the evidence.

Reflection · added by this kit ·

The rubric mentions reflection, but the project sheet has no reflection task, so this kit adds one (page 21): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. It adds no points and no rubric row; use it as evidence when you score Documentation & Presentation.

Notes / observations ·

Encourage students to record sounds during passing periods, lunch, or at home. The wider the variety, the better.

Found SoundLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools this project uses. Demonstrate them on Classes 1 and 2 (slides 6 and 9) and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; check the labels on yours.

Record audioAdd an Audio track (Track › New Audio Track), choose the input, arm the track and press Record (R). Use the microphone at your station, or bring in a phone recording.
Bring in a phone recordingDrag the audio file from Finder into the Tracks area, or use File › Import › Audio File. Get the file from the phone to the Mac the way your school allows (AirDrop, a cable or your class page).
Trim and clean upSplit regions (Command-T), delete what you do not need, and trim to the first and last moments of the sound. Use fades to remove clicks.
NormalizeDouble-click the region to open the Audio File Editor and choose Functions › Normalize. Menu names vary by version; listen before and after.
Quick SamplerAdd a Software Instrument track and choose Quick Sampler, or drag an audio region onto an empty Software Instrument track and choose Quick Sampler. Pick Classic, One Shot or Slice at the top.
Slice modeChoose Slice, then adjust how many slices there are and where they fall. Each slice plays from its own key.
EffectsInsert effects on the channel strip: Channel EQ, Compressor, a delay such as Echo, and a reverb such as ChromaVerb or Space Designer.
AutomationPress A to show automation, choose a parameter such as a filter knob or volume, and draw changes over time. A slowly opening filter keeps a loop from feeling static.
LayersGive each layer its own track (percussion, bass, texture) and name the tracks. Solo each one in turn to check it earns its place.
Save versionsUse File › Save As… with a numbered name before each big change.
Bounce and zipFile › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

MODES
Teacher + student

My second struggle is that Quick Sampler is confusing at first, so I demo all three modes (Classic, One Shot, Slice) with a real sound. The pictures show a waveform and the keys it plays on. Names and menus can differ in your version of Logic.

Which mode for which job ·

ModeWhat it doesTry it for
ClassicPlays the sound across the keys like an instrument, and lets you set a loop.bass, pads, a hum held under everything
One ShotPlays the whole sound each time you press a key.hits, accents, a crinkle or a slam
SliceCuts one recording at its loud moments and puts each slice on its own key.a drum kit from one recording
Kit suggestions, not part of my sheets

Slice mode is my tip 2: one recording becomes a whole kit. Try Classic for bass by playing a pitched-down sound low on the keyboard. If a slice sounds wrong, move its edge to the start of the sound.

Try this in Class 2

Give each pair one recording and ask them to play it in all three modes, then tell the class which mode they would use for percussion, bass and texture, and why.

Check your version

Quick Sampler is part of recent versions of Logic Pro. Open it with one of your own recordings before Class 1 and check that the three modes are where this page says.

An invented piece, “Passing Period”, described on paper: no audio is included, so the numbers are easy to check. Students’ pieces will differ.

1 · The five sounds ·

SoundRecorded withQuick SamplerLayerWhat it does
Locker slamphone, one takeSlice (5 slices)Percussionslices on five keys: boom, rattle, click
Pencil tapstation microphoneClassic, shortPercussionpitched up, played as a tick
Door thudphoneClassic, pitched downBassplayed as a simple bassline
Vending-machine humphone, 20 secondsClassic, loop onTextureheld under everything, filter sweeps
Water-bottle crinklestation microphoneOne ShotTextureone press at the start of the Break

2 · The plan: 45 seconds ·

Intro 0:00–0:05, Build 0:05–0:15, Full 0:15–0:35, Break 0:35–0:40, Outro 0:40–0:45. The plan is 45 seconds: inside my 30–60 second requirement.

3 · Checking the numbers

96 beats per minute is 0.625 s a beat, so a bar of 4/4 is 2.5 s. Sections: 2 + 4 + 8 + 2 + 2 = 18 bars, and 18 × 2.5 s = 45 s. From 0:15 to 0:35 all three layers play at once. Five sounds are recorded and all five are used.

4 · Why it works

Each layer has a job: the hum is the room, the taps and slams are the beat, the thud is the floor. The filter sweep means the texture changes without adding anything. A listener should struggle to guess the sources.

5 · Three Padlet questions (invented) ·

1. Which sound did you guess first, and which one fooled you? 2. If you took one layer out, which one would you miss most? 3. Where does the track change the most, and how did I do it?

Overview ·

Become a sound designer. Record everyday sounds — from your phone, the microphone at your station, or anywhere around you — and transform them into musical instruments using Logic’s Quick Sampler. Then compose a short piece built entirely from your found sounds.

Goals ·

  • Record, edit, and manipulate real-world sounds as creative tools
  • Use Quick Sampler to build playable instruments from audio recordings
  • Design at least three distinct sound layers (percussion, bass, texture)
  • Apply effects, automation, and mixing to create a polished composition

Tips for Success ·

  • The weirder the source sound, the more interesting your instruments will be. Don’t just record obvious things.
  • Spend time in Quick Sampler experimenting with Slice mode — it turns one recording into a whole kit.
  • Layer your sounds. A single found sound as a melody is thin. Stack textures underneath it.
  • The goal is transformation — if your audience can’t tell where the sounds came from, you’re doing it right.

What a reflection is

A reflection is a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Answer all four prompts in your own words. You choose how.

Written. I will write my answers on this page.
Oral. I will talk my answers through with my teacher. My teacher fills in the box at the bottom.

1  What went well? Name something in your project you are proud of, and say why it worked.

2  What did you struggle with? Say what was hard and what you tried to fix it.

3  What would you do differently next time? Be specific: a step, a choice, a sound or a tool.
