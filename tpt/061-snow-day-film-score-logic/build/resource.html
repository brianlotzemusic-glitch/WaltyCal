What this is ·

A 3-class-period film-scoring project for high school music technology. Every student scores a 20-second winter scene that they film on a phone or take from one of three ready-made storyboards. They name its mood, mark its hit points on a timeline, choose a tempo so the hits land on beats or bars, and build the score in Logic Pro from four layers. Students plan it, build and mix it, present it and reflect on it. It is an original Hudson Beat project.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and the teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Look at the storyboards, the spotting sheet and the calculator (pages 11–16) and the setup (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 11–24), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–6), Class 2 (slides 7–9) and Class 3 (slides 10–14). Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

OVERVIEW
Film scoring project
The project in one line

Snow Day Film Score · Film scoring · Logic Pro
Spot the hit points, pick the tempo, score the scene.

What students make ·

A score for one 20-second winter scene, filmed by the student or taken from a storyboard. It has a named mood and an emotional arc, 3 to 6 hit points with their times and bar numbers, and four layers in Logic Pro: a pad, a motif the student invents, percussion hits on the hit points and foley or sound-design accents. The tempo is chosen from 80 to 128 BPM so that hit points land on beats or bars. Students hand in a bounced file and the zipped .logicx project, plus their plans and a reflection, then play the scene with the score and take questions.

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how film music is planned. Students learn to watch a scene for its mood and its key moments, to turn a time in seconds into a bar and a beat with simple arithmetic, and to build a score from layers in Logic Pro, with a tempo chosen so that the music lands on the picture.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Name the mood of a 20-second winter scene and describe how its feeling changes from start to finish, its emotional arc
  • Spot the scene: mark 3 to 6 hit points with their times in seconds and say what each one needs
  • Choose a tempo so the hit points land on beats or bars, using seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM and seconds per bar = 240 ÷ BPM in 4/4
  • Score the scene in Logic Pro with a pad, a motif, percussion hits and foley accents, then mix and bounce it

At a glance ·

Before they start

Students should know how to open a project, add a software instrument and use the mixer in Logic. Nobody needs to read music: the project works with sounds, timing and a few simple formulas.

SETUP
Teacher

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones.
  • A place to hand in the .mp3 or .wav files, the zipped .logicx and the reflection.
  • The storyboards and the calculator tables, printed (pages 11–16), or shown on the projector.
  • Scenes: a phone clip of 20 seconds for each student who films, or a storyboard for everyone else. Common formats such as .mov and .mp4 usually import; if one does not, convert it in a video app first.
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 11–24).
  • A MIDI keyboard is optional: every note can be clicked in with the mouse.

Before Class 1 ·

Read the calculator page (page 16) so you can answer ‘which beat is that?’ for any time.
Open Logic Pro on a student computer and try File › Open Movie… with a phone clip. Note what your version calls it and where the Movie track appears.
Check that you can add a marker and set the tempo (see page 8).
Read the worked example (pages 9–10).

What to watch ·

Watch for hit points that are not real moments, arithmetic slips, a movie that does not start at the beginning of the project, or a score so busy it covers the picture. A good listening day: show a few seconds of each student’s scene with no sound and let the class guess where the hit points are, then play the score and see whether the class and the student agree. This kit prints no film music and names no films.

Ideas for the room
  • Ask while students work: what is the mood right now? Which beat does this hit point land on? What does the pad do here?
  • On Class 1, play a scene with the sound off on the projector and let the class call out the moments that need a sound.
  • A student who is stuck can start with the percussion hits only: one hit on every marker already sounds like a score.

Filming rules ·

Share these with students

Film an original scene with no music, no copyrighted characters and no logos in it. Ask permission before you film anyone, and follow your school’s rules about filming people. Scenes with no faces in them, such as boots, snow, hands or a sled, work well.

Keep the volume moderate

Bright, high sounds and big hits can be tiring in headphones. Tell students to start low and check the final mix at a normal level.

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Teaches how film music is planned. Students learn to watch a scene for its mood and its key moments, to turn a time in seconds into a bar and a beat with simple arithmetic, and to build a score from layers in Logic Pro, with a tempo chosen so that the music lands on the picture.

Common student struggles ·

  • No hit points stand out: ask what changes on the screen, such as a door opening or a sled leaping, and what a viewer would gasp at. Mark only those (page 15).
  • The arithmetic looks hard: give students the calculator tables (page 16), work one hit point aloud with the formula bar number = 1 + t ÷ (240 ÷ BPM), and let them check it by moving a marker in Logic.
  • A hit lands late or early in Logic: check that the movie starts at the very beginning of the project, that the tempo is the one on the spotting sheet and that each marker sits where the sheet says (page 8).
  • The score is too busy and covers the picture: take a layer away, turn the pad down and leave a little silence before each hit point.

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Use one of the three storyboards, choose just 3 hit points, use only the pad and the percussion hits, and work at 96 BPM, where the 20 seconds are exactly 8 bars (page 16).

More Advanced: Film your own scene and plan two tempos, such as 96 and 120 BPM, then compare how the hit points land at each (page 16). Or ask for a score that changes mood at a hit point, with a different motif before and after it.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Hit points that do not stand out: the storyboards (pages 11–13) show five clear moments each, and the spotting sheet (page 15) asks for a size for every hit point.
  • The arithmetic: the calculator tables (page 16) give the bar and beat for 12 times at 5 tempos, and the worked example (pages 9–10) shows every step.
  • Late or early hits: the Logic quick reference (page 8) covers the movie, the markers and the time display.
  • A busy score: the layer plan (page 17) shows when each layer enters, and the Mood and arc page (page 14) matches the sound to the feeling.

How to grade ·

  • The rubric: five categories, each scored 1–5, /25 (page 21). Students score themselves first; the final grade is the average of their score and yours.
  • Mood & Spotting (rubric row 1): read the Mood and arc page (page 14) and the spotting sheet (page 15). Are the hit points real moments, with times?
  • Tempo & Timing Math (rubric row 2): recompute one hit point with the student: bar number = 1 + t ÷ (240 ÷ BPM) (page 16).
  • Layers & Sound (rubric row 3): open the project and compare the layers with the plan (page 17). Is the motif the student’s own?
  • Sync & Mix (rubric row 4): play the bounce against the scene and listen at each marker. Does the sound land on the moment?
  • Plan & Reflection (rubric row 5): the layer plan, the feedback questions and the Reflection page (page 24) are the evidence.

Reflection ·

Every student reflects (page 24): a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. Use it as evidence for the Plan & Reflection row of the rubric.

Notes / observations ·

Snow Day Film ScoreLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools a Snow Day Film Score is likely to use. Demonstrate what your class needs on Classes 1 and 2 and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; labels move between versions, so check yours.

Project and tempoFile › New…, then a Software Instrument track to start. Set the project tempo in the control bar to the number on your spotting sheet and keep the time signature at 4/4. The hit-point arithmetic only works at that tempo, so set it first.
Import the movieIf you filmed the scene, choose File › Open Movie… (look for it in the File menu; the wording can differ by version) and pick the video file. Logic shows the picture in a Movie window and, if you switch it on, in the Movie global track above the tracks. Check that the first frame sits at the very start of the project. With a storyboard there is no movie: use the frame times printed on the board.
Seconds as well as barsWhile you place markers, switch the control bar display to time, if your version offers it. Time may show as timecode (hours, minutes, seconds, frames): the last number counts frames, not hundredths of a second, so 6.25 seconds is not 6:25.
Markers on the hit pointsShow the Marker global track (the Global Tracks controls or the View menu). Add a marker with the plus (+) button in that track, or with a Create Marker key command if your version has one, then drag or type it to the bar and beat on your spotting sheet. Name it for its moment, such as Door opens.
PadMake a Software Instrument track and open Alchemy or Retro Synth, then look in the Library for a soft pad sound with a slow attack, so each note fades in. Play long, slow notes or chords that fit the mood. A pad is a background color, not a tune.
MotifAdd another Software Instrument track and invent a motif of 3 to 5 notes. Click it in, or play it, and bring it back changed (slower, higher, softer) as the mood changes. To stay in key, use only the white keys, or switch on Scale Quantize if your version has it. This kit gives no melody: the motif must be yours.
Percussion hitsMake a Drum Machine Designer track (click the instrument slot and choose it), or use Quick Sampler. Give a pad a big, short sound such as a soft drum, a cymbal swell or a low boom, and place one hit exactly on each marker. Zoom in until the hit starts on the line of its bar and beat.
Foley and sound-design accentsOpen Quick Sampler (a pad in Drum Machine Designer has one) to import a sound or, if your version offers it, record your own with the Mac’s microphone: a crunch of snow, a door, a zip. Squeezing a bag of cornstarch is a classic stand-in for steps in snow. If your version has it, reverse or pitch the sound to make a whoosh.
Automation, mix and bouncePress A to show automation, choose a parameter such as track volume (or the filter cutoff in Alchemy or Retro Synth) and click points to draw it: fade the pad up before a hit point and let it fall after. Press X for the Mixer. Then File › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV, for the 20 seconds of the scene. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

EXAMPLE
Teacher

An invented plan on paper: a 20-second ice-skating scene scored at 96 BPM. No audio is included. Student projects will differ. Page 1 of 2: the plan, the hit points and the tempo choice.

1 · A weak plan

I will make sad winter music for my skating video.

It names no mood, no hit points, no tempo and no layers.

2 · A clear plan

My scene is 20 seconds of ice skating. The mood starts calm, turns joyful, becomes tense during a wobble and ends in relief. I marked four hit points: 2.5 s (the skater steps onto the ice), 6.25 s (the first spin), 12.5 s (the wobble) and 17.5 s (the bow). I chose 96 BPM in 4/4, so one beat lasts 60 ÷ 96 = 0.625 seconds and one bar lasts 240 ÷ 96 = 2.5 seconds. That puts the hit points at bar 2 beat 1, bar 3 beat 3, bar 6 beat 1 and bar 8 beat 1, and the scene is exactly 8 bars long. A soft pad plays throughout, my motif enters at the first hit point, and a drum hit and a foley sound land on each marker.

8 · Checking the numbers

One bar of 4/4 at 96 BPM lasts 240 ÷ 96 = 2.5 seconds, so 8 bars = 8 × 2.5 = 20 seconds. A hit point at t seconds is at bar 1 + t ÷ 2.5: 2.5 s gives bar 2.00, 6.25 s gives bar 3.50, 12.5 s gives bar 6.00 and 17.5 s gives bar 8.00. In beats from the start: 2.5 × 96 ÷ 60 = 4, 6.25 × 96 ÷ 60 = 10, 12.5 × 96 ÷ 60 = 20 and 17.5 × 96 ÷ 60 = 28; whole numbers, so every hit point lands on a beat. The mood sections last 2.5, 10, 5 and 2.5 seconds, which is 1, 4, 2 and 1 bars.

9 · Three feedback questions (invented)

1. Which hit point felt biggest, and what sound made it big? 2. Did the mood change when you expected it to? What told you? 3. Which layer would you take away first, and why?

10 · Moments to point out in class
  • The mood changes at hit points 1, 3 and 4 but not at hit point 2: the first spin happens inside the joyful section.
  • The motif stops at hit point 3, so the wobble arrives with only the pad, the low boom and the creak. That thinner sound is the tension.
  • The pad’s filter closes during the tension, bars 6 and 7, and opens again at hit point 4, so the relief sounds brighter.

Step 3: pick a tempo. In 4/4 there are 4 beats in a bar. These formulas turn a time in seconds into a bar and a beat. Every number below is computed, so you can check your own arithmetic against it.

The formulas (4/4)

Seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM

Seconds per bar = 240 ÷ BPM

Bar number = 1 + t ÷ (240 ÷ BPM)

Beats from the start = t × BPM ÷ 60

Example: t = 12.5 s at 96 BPM: 1 + 12.5 ÷ 2.5 = 6.00, so bar 6, beat 1.

Rounding rules

Seconds to 3 decimals, bar numbers to 2, a 5 rounds up. For a marker, round to the nearest sixteenth note: sixteenths = t × BPM ÷ 60 × 4, rounded to a whole number. Then bar = sixteenths ÷ 16 (whole part) + 1, and beat = 1 + (sixteenths left over after the bars) ÷ 4. A bar number of 6.25 is bar 6 and a quarter, which is beat 2.

Snow Day Film Score

Music Technology | Film Scoring Project | 3 class periods

Overview ·

Score a 20-second winter scene. Film a short scene on a phone, or choose one of the three storyboards in this kit: a snow-day morning, sledding or snowman building. Name the scene’s mood and trace how the feeling changes. Watch it with the sound off and mark the hit points, the key moments where the music must land. Choose a tempo so the hit points fall on beats or bars: a hit point at time t seconds is at bar number 1 + t ÷ (240 ÷ BPM). Then, in Logic Pro, build the score from four layers: a pad, a motif of your own, percussion hits and foley or sound-design accents. Nobody needs to read music, and no melody is given.

Goals ·

  • Name the mood of a 20-second winter scene and describe how its feeling changes from start to finish, its emotional arc
  • Spot the scene: mark 3 to 6 hit points with their times in seconds and say what each one needs
  • Choose a tempo so the hit points land on beats or bars, using seconds per beat = 60 ÷ BPM and seconds per bar = 240 ÷ BPM in 4/4
  • Score the scene in Logic Pro with a pad, a motif, percussion hits and foley accents, then mix and bounce it

Key Vocabulary ·

Hit pointA key moment in a scene, such as a door opening or a sled leaping, where a sound or a change in the music lands exactly on the picture
SpottingWatching a scene closely to decide where music is needed and what each moment needs; the notes you take are your spotting notes
MotifA short musical idea of a few notes that you invent and bring back, changed, as the mood of the scene changes
FoleyEveryday sounds recorded or made by hand to match the picture, such as footsteps in snow or the creak of a door

Tips for Success ·

  • Watch the scene with the sound off at least three times before you mark a single hit point.
  • Fewer, bigger hit points work better than many small ones: three to six is plenty for 20 seconds.
  • Leave space. A quiet moment just before a hit point makes the hit feel bigger.
  • Save a new version of your project before each big experiment, so you can always go back.
