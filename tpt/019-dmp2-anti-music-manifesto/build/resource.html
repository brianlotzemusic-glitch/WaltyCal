What this is ·

A 7-class-period experimental music project for high school music technology. Each student picks a musical convention (structure, tonality, rhythm or another) and breaks it on purpose, records or creates unconventional sounds, uses Logic’s effects and automation to distort or deconstruct them, builds a 45–60 second piece, and writes a one-paragraph artist statement. It is an open-ended project from my Digital Music Production 2 course (focus: experimental and conceptual), and the projects in that course can be taught in any order.

Quick start ·

  1. Read the pacing guide (page 5) and my teacher notes (pages 6–7).
  2. Choose a short passage for the Class 1 listening hook (page 4).
  3. Print the student pages (pages 12–22), one set per student.
  4. Open the intro deck and read the speaker notes for Class 1.

The intro deck ·

Every slide has a class: Class 1 (slides 1–7), Class 2 (slides 8–10), Class 3 (slide 11), Class 4 (slides 12–13), Class 6 (slides 14–17) and Class 7 (slides 18–19). Class 5 is a working day with no new slide. Every slide has speaker notes.

Fonts: the deck uses Oswald and Source Sans 3 (free on Google Fonts, built into Google Slides). If PowerPoint or Keynote shows a different font, install the two fonts or present the PDF copy, which shows the intended look.

No audio included

No audio, video, songs or examples are included. Students record or create every sound themselves, so everything in their pieces is original. For the listening hook, you choose a short passage yourself and find it on a service your school allows.

Make it fit your class ·

  • Easier or harder: my variations on page 6: pick from 3–5 rule cards (page 10), or a 90-second piece with a written manifesto.
  • Feedback board: my sheets use Padlet for the three questions; any class board works.
  • Listening: my notes suggest a brief listening session of experimental music first; you choose the passages.
  • Grading: my rubric, /25, the average of the student’s and your score (page 19).
In one sentence

Students choose a rule of music, break it on purpose, and prove it was on purpose with a piece and a paragraph.

What students make ·

One 45–60 second piece of anti-music that intentionally breaks at least one major convention, built from sounds they record or create and deconstructed with effects and automation, plus a one-paragraph artist statement. They hand in a bounced .mp3 or .wav, the zipped .logicx project and the statement, plus a Padlet post with three feedback questions, then play the piece and present the concept.

What this project reinforces ·

Pushes creative boundaries and critical thinking about what music is. Requires students to articulate artistic intent, which strengthens their ability to talk about creative work.

Standards ·

Supports the National Core Arts Standards for Music (Music Technology, high school): generating and developing ideas (MU:Cr1.1, MU:Cr2.1), presenting work (MU:Pr6.1) and responding to it (MU:Re7.2).

Goals ·

  • Challenge conventional ideas of what music is and can be
  • Use Logic Pro to create a deliberate, expressive piece that defies expectations
  • Explore unconventional sound sources, effects, and techniques
  • Articulate artistic intent through a written artist statement

Anti-Music ManifestoSetup and a safe start
SETUP
Teacher

You need ·

  • A Mac with Logic Pro for each student, and headphones. Speakers help for the final listen.
  • A way to record sound: a microphone, the computer’s built-in microphone, or another recorder students already use.
  • A place to submit an .mp3 or .wav, a zipped .logicx project and the artist statement (your class page).
  • A feedback board for the three questions (my sheets use Padlet).
  • Printed student pages, one set each (pages 12–22).

Before Class 1 ·

Choose one or two short passages of experimental music for slide 2, preview them at a low volume, and find them yourself.
Check that a student computer can record a sound and play it back through headphones.
Decide how students will record: at their desks, around the room, or on a short walk with permission.
Print the student pages; set up the submission spot and the feedback board.

Making it feel safe ·

My notes say some students are uncomfortable making “bad” music on purpose. A plan that works:

  • Say it first. This is artistic exploration, not failure. A rule broken on purpose is a skill.
  • Concept before sound. Nobody opens a session until the Concept sheet (page 12) names a rule and a reason.
  • Offer a way in. For students who want one, hand out the rule cards on page 10.
Kit suggestions, not part of my sheets
  • Keep headphone volume moderate: distortion and noise can be much louder than they sound.
  • Ask permission before recording a person, and keep recordings school-appropriate.
  • Name and log every sound the moment it is recorded (Sound log, page 13).
  • Save a numbered version before every big change (page 9).
Listening routine for slide 2

Play the passage once. Ask: what rule does it seem to break, and does it feel planned or accidental? Play it again and take answers. The same sounds can feel either way, and that gap is the lesson: intention.

TEACHER NOTES
1 of 2

What this project reinforces ·

Pushes creative boundaries and critical thinking about what music is. Requires students to articulate artistic intent, which strengthens their ability to talk about creative work.

Common student struggles ·

  • Students make random noise and call it anti-music. Push them to define their concept BEFORE they start building.
  • Artist statements are vague. Require them to name the specific rule they’re breaking and explain their reasoning.
  • Some students are uncomfortable making “bad” music on purpose. Frame it as artistic exploration, not failure.
  • Students don’t use Logic’s tools — they just bang on a keyboard. Encourage effects, automation, and sound manipulation.

Suggested variations ·

Easier: Provide 3–5 specific rules to break (e.g., “make a track with no rhythm,” “use only dissonant chords”) and let students choose one.

More Advanced: Require a 90-second piece with a written manifesto (not just a statement) defending their artistic philosophy. Pair with research on an experimental artist.

Where this kit helps with each struggle
  • Random noise: the Concept sheet (page 12) asks for the rule and the reason before anything is built.
  • Vague statements: the Statement planner (page 15) and the sample statement on page 11.
  • Uncomfortable with “bad” music: the Rules page (page 10) has six rule cards and says breaking a rule is a choice.
  • Not using Logic’s tools: the Sound log (page 13) and the quick reference (page 9).

How to grade ·

Suggestions from this kit, not part of my rubric:

  • Creativity & Originality and Composition & Structure: ask the student to say, in one sentence, the rule they broke (the Concept sheet, page 12, holds it). A piece that breaks no rule, or breaks one by accident, is the “random noise” my notes warn about.

Students write it or talk it through with you; either way they answer the four prompts. Allow about 10 minutes after the presentations. It adds no points and no rubric row; use it as evidence when you score Documentation & Presentation.

Anti-Music ManifestoLogic quick reference
SETUP
Teacher + student

Where to find the tools this project uses. Demonstrate them on Class 2 (slide 10) and then let students work. Names are from current Logic Pro versions; check the labels on yours.

Record audioAdd an Audio track (Track › New Audio Track), choose the input, arm the track and press Record (R). Use the computer’s built-in microphone, or any microphone you have.
Make a sound in LogicAdd a Software Instrument track and play or draw notes, or open an Apple loop, then change it. Any sound can be a starting point.
ReverseSelect an audio region and tick Reverse in the Region inspector, or reverse it in the Audio File Editor. Reverse it again to hear the original.
Speed and pitchUse the Region inspector’s Transpose and Fine Tuning, or turn on Flex and drag the region’s ends to stretch it. Extreme values are welcome here.
DistortionInsert a distortion effect on the channel strip (Overdrive, Distortion, Bitcrusher or Clip Distortion), then raise its drive. Listen with the volume low first.
Delay and reverbInsert a delay (Echo or Delay Designer) and raise the feedback until it smears. Reverb: ChromaVerb or Space Designer.
FilterUse Channel EQ or a filter plug-in and sweep the cutoff while the sound plays. A sound that is mostly filtered away is still a sound.
AutomationPress A to show automation, choose a parameter such as an effect knob or volume, and draw changes over time. This is how a sound breaks down gradually.
Edit and cutSplit regions (Command-T), delete pieces and move them off the grid. Turn Snap off to place sounds anywhere on purpose.
Save versionsUse File › Save As… with a numbered name before each big change, so a good accident is never lost.
Bounce and zipFile › Bounce › Project or Section (Command-B), MP3 or WAV. In Finder, Control-click the .logicx and choose Compress.

Anti-Music ManifestoWorked example · an invented piece
EXAMPLE
Teacher

An invented piece, “Kitchen Table”, and its artist statement, described on paper: no audio is included, so the numbers are easy to check. Students’ pieces will differ.

1 · The concept ·

Rule brokenConceptSounds (recorded)Effects and automation
Rhythm: a steady pulseA pulse is a promise; the piece breaks it, then returns it wrongA drawer click, a kettle hiss, a chair scrape played in reverseDistortion automated up to 85% by 0:28; delay on the clicks; hiss filtered to a whisper in the Hush

2 · The plan: 50 seconds ·

Promise 0:00–0:10, Break 0:10–0:28, Hush 0:28–0:36, Wrong return 0:36–0:50. The plan is 50 seconds: inside my 45–60 second requirement.

3 · Checking the numbers

Promise: 10 clicks, each 1 s apart, which is 60 per minute. Break: 14 clicks whose gaps shrink from 0.9 s to 0.3 s, then stretch to 3.8 s. Return: 8 clicks with gaps of 0.1 s to 3.9 s, so no pulse comes back. Sections: 10 + 18 + 8 + 14 = 50 s.

4 · Why it is on purpose

A listener hears a clear pulse first, so they know what is being broken. Every sound has a job: the drawer is the pulse, the kettle is the quiet, the reversed chair is the pull. The automation moves with the rule instead of being random.

5 · A sample artist statement (one paragraph) ·

“Kitchen Table” breaks the rule that a pulse should stay steady. I recorded a drawer, a kettle and a chair, and for the first ten seconds the drawer clicks once a second like a metronome. Then I sped the clicks up and stretched them out while pushing the distortion higher, until the pulse fell apart, because I wanted the listener to feel a promise being broken. The hiss in the middle is the quiet that follows. When the clicks return they are uneven, so the piece never gets back to normal. Listen for the moment the second click arrives too early.

Overview ·

Break the rules on purpose. Create an intentional piece of “anti-music” — a work that deliberately defies traditional structure, tonality, or rhythm while still expressing a clear artistic concept. This isn’t random noise — it’s noise with a vision.

Goals ·

  • Challenge conventional ideas of what music is and can be
  • Use Logic Pro to create a deliberate, expressive piece that defies expectations
  • Explore unconventional sound sources, effects, and techniques
  • Articulate artistic intent through a written artist statement

What a reflection is

A reflection is a written or oral conversation about how you did on the project and what you would do differently next time. Answer all four prompts in your own words. You choose how.

Written. I will write my answers on this page.
Oral. I will talk my answers through with my teacher. My teacher fills in the box at the bottom.

1  What went well? Name something in your project you are proud of, and say why it worked.

2  What did you struggle with? Say what was hard and what you tried to fix it.

3  What would you do differently next time? Be specific: a step, a choice, a sound or a tool.

4  Look at the rubric (page 19). Which category are you proudest of, and which would you work on first next time?
